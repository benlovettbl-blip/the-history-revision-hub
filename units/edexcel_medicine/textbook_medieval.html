KEY HISTORICAL INDIVIDUALS & ACTORS
Hippocrates (c.460–c.370 BC) Greek "Father of Medicine"

Formulated the Four Humours, clinical bedside observation, and the Hippocratic Oath anchoring medieval university training.

Claudius Galen (129–c.216 AD) Roman Physician & Anatomist

Developed the Theory of Opposites; his animal dissections introduced errors (two-bone jaw, porous heart septum) backed by the Church.

Avicenna (Ibn Sina, 980–1037) Islamic Medical Encyclopaedist

Authored The Canon of Medicine synthesising Galen with Islamic pharmacology, used as a core European university textbook until 1650.

John of Arderne (1307–1392) English Battlefield Surgeon

Wrote Practica of Surgery (1376), using opium/henbane pain-numbing ointments rather than boiling cautery irons and charging rich knights more.

Roger Bacon (c.1219–c.1292) Franciscan Friar & Scholar

Questioned blind reliance on ancient authorities and urged practical experimentation, leading Church superiors to confine him.

King Edward III (1312–1377) Monarch During Black Death

Ordered church prayers and London street-cleaning in 1349 to combat miasma, though royal government lacked public-health infrastructure.

KEY ENQUIRY “Why did the medieval Catholic Church hold an unshakeable monopoly over beliefs about the causes of disease?”
STAGE 1 Divine Retribution and Original Sin c1250–1500
“Why did medieval Christians view epidemic disease as punishment from God?”

Between 1250 and 1500, the Roman Catholic Church was the supreme authority governing medieval thought and daily life. Ordinary peasants and learned scholars believed that disease was not merely a physical malfunction, but a direct manifestation of God’s will. Deeply rooted in Christian teachings on original sin, sickness was accepted as divine punishment for sin sent to test faith or purge wickedness.

Because suffering was viewed as spiritual chastisement, medieval society turned to religious intervention rather than physical science. The Church taught that enduring illness patiently on earth could shorten a soul's time in Purgatory. Consequently, whenever epidemics struck, communities relied on prayer and repentance as their primary cure, organising penitential street processions, strict fasting, and solemn confessions before seeking earthly herbal remedies.

STAGE 2 Moral Taint: Leprosy and Social Exclusion c1250–1500
“How did Biblical moral stigma shape the treatment of medieval lepers?”

Few diseases illustrated the fusion of physical illness and spiritual judgment as vividly as leprosy. Lepers were believed to bear an outward physical sign of internal corruption and sinful lust, suffering intense biblical moral stigma surrounding leprosy. Once diagnosed by parish priests or bishops, sufferers were cast out through formal religious separation ceremonies symbolising that they were legally dead to the world.

Segregated victims were forced into permanent exile to isolated Lazar houses established outside city walls. Sufferers were legally forbidden from entering narrow town streets, touching healthy children, or drinking from public wells. To prevent contagion, lepers were compelled to wear distinctive grey cloaks, sounding wooden clappers and hand-bells to alert passing travellers of their approach along rural parish roads.

Supernatural & Religious Explanations of Disease — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 Astrological Influences and the Zodiac Man 1345
“How did physicians use Zodiac Man charts and the 1345 alignment?”

Alongside divine retribution, medieval society relied heavily on astrology to explain the unpredictable timing and severity of disease. Drawing upon translated Arabic scholarship, medieval physicians believed celestial movements governed human anatomy. Astrological charts such as Zodiac Man parchment charts mapped zodiac constellations directly onto human organs, teaching that the Moon, Mars, and Saturn controlled internal organs, humours, and vital blood circulation.

Astrological timing was considered essential before performing physical treatments like bloodletting or surgery. When the catastrophic Black Death struck Europe, university scholars blamed the planetary crisis on the infamous 1345 planetary conjunction, arguing that an ominous alignment of Mars, Saturn, and Jupiter in the sign of Aquarius had drawn corrupt poisonous vapours from the earth to infect the atmosphere.

STAGE 4 Spiritual Intercession, Relics, and Miracle Cures c1350
“Why did sick people turn to pilgrimage shrines rather than doctors?”

Because disease was viewed as supernatural in origin, treatments were predominantly religious and spiritual. Suffering patients undertook an arduous pilgrimage to Canterbury to pray at the sacred tomb of Archbishop Thomas Becket, or travelled to Walsingham. Pilgrims sought miraculous healing through kissing holy relics of saints, believing that touching holy bones, dried blood, or fragments of the True Cross channelled miraculous divine grace.

Wealthy medieval families secured spiritual protection by funding perpetual chantry masses and purchasing indulgences, paying parish priests to recite prayers for sick relatives to hasten healing or purge sins. Monasteries and cathedrals organised solemn processions carrying jeweled reliquaries through crowded streets during pestilence, demonstrating how medieval healing remained an act of collective religious devotion rather than clinical intervention.

STAGE 5 Monastic Scriptoria, Galen and Church Censorship c1400
“Why did church enforcement of Galen prevent medical progress?”

While the Catholic Church preserved ancient medical manuscripts through Latin copying in monastic scriptoria, it strictly policed intellectual inquiry. Church authorities enthusiastically embraced the classical writings of Galen because his teachings promoted a single divine creator, turning Galen’s ‘purposeful creator’ doctrine into indisputable theological truth. Questioning Galen's anatomical accuracy was condemned as dangerous heresy that challenged God's perfect design of the human body.

The Church enforced absolute intellectual conformity across medical universities, fiercely suppressing empirical scientific investigation. When English friar Roger Bacon was imprisoned around 1277 by Franciscan superiors after advocating direct scientific experiment and observation over ancient texts, scholars were intimidated into silence. Consequently, medical education remained frozen for over two centuries, prioritising rote recitation of ancient Latin authorities over original clinical research.

KEY ENQUIRY “Why did ancient ideas like the Four Humours and miasma dominate medieval medicine?”
STAGE 1 Hippocrates and the Secular Shift in Medicine c460 BC
“How did Hippocrates separate medicine from superstition?”

In fifth-century BC Greece, Hippocrates revolutionised healing by pioneering the Hippocratic clinical method. Instead of attributing illness to divine wrath or demonic possession, he championed careful bedside observation, recording patient symptoms, pulse, and fevers systematically. This revolutionary approach represented a dramatic rejection of gods and supernatural superstition, establishing the enduring principle that physical disease was caused by natural bodily imbalances.

Hippocrates also established medical professionalism through the Hippocratic Oath ethics, which bound physicians to preserve life, protect patient confidentiality, and do no deliberate harm. His extensive written treatises formed the foundation of Western rational medicine. When translated into Latin during the Middle Ages, his secular diagnostic principles became the core curriculum taught in newly founded European university medical faculties.

STAGE 2 The Four Humours and Humoral Equilibrium c1250
“Why did humoral theory seem logical to medieval people?”

The cornerstone of classical rational medicine was the Theory of the four bodily humours, developed by Hippocrates and expanded by Galen. According to this theory, the human body comprised four essential fluids: blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile. Good health required an exact equilibrium of these humours, corresponding directly to the four universal elements of air, fire, water, and earth.

Humoral balance fluctuated according to individual temperament, diet, age, and a complex seasonal balance system. For example, wet and cold winter seasons increased phlegm, causing coughs, while hot summer weather elevated dry yellow bile, causing fevers. When humours became corrupted or unbalanced, illness resulted, providing medieval doctors with a comprehensive, rational framework to diagnose ailments without reference to supernatural intervention.

Rational Explanations: Hippocrates, Galen & The Four Humours — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 Galen's Theory of Opposites and Animal Dissections c1280
“How did the Theory of Opposites provide practical treatment rules?”

In second-century Rome, Claudius Galen expanded Hippocratic humoralism by developing Galen’s influential Theory of Opposites. While Hippocrates relied on passive rest, Galen introduced active treatment based on the principle that contraries cure contraries. For instance, a cold, moist fever was aggressively treated with hot, dry remedies like crushed pepper and wine, while hot inflammatory fevers were treated with cooling cucumber.

Galen also produced extensive anatomical treatises, but because Roman religious law strictly prohibited human dissection, he relied on pigs, dogs, and apes. This led to serious animal dissection errors, including claiming the human jawbone comprised two bones and that blood passed through invisible heart septum pores. Canonised by the medieval Church, Galen’s anatomical mistakes remained unchallenged for fourteen centuries.

STAGE 4 Diagnostic Uroscopy: The Matula and the Urine Wheel c1300
“Why did uroscopy give physicians great diagnostic authority?”

Medieval physicians commanded immense diagnostic prestige without ever performing surgery or physically touching their wealthy patients. The primary clinical tool was the uroscopy urine flask (matula), a pear-shaped glass vessel designed to resemble the human bladder. Physicians held the matula up to the light, closely scrutinising the urine sample's clarity, sediment, density, and even tasting drops to detect sweet or acidic imbalances.

The physician cross-referenced findings against a colorful urine diagnostic wheel, matching twenty distinct shades ranging from white to tawny, red, and black to determine which internal humour was corrupted. Finally, physicians checked planetary positions for Zodiac Man alignment before prescribing treatments, ensuring that bloodletting or purging occurred only under favourable astrological auspices to avoid exacerbating internal humoral corruption.

STAGE 5 Miasma Theory and Foul Environmental Vapours c1350
“Why did rational physicians link bad smells directly to epidemics?”

To explain why thousands of citizens perished simultaneously during epidemic outbreaks, rational ancient and medieval doctors formulated the miasma theory (bad air). Originating in classical Greece, this theory taught that infectious pestilence was generated when poisonous fumes rose from rotting animal corpses, stagnant swamps, unburied bodies, and decaying organic waste, contaminating the surrounding atmosphere with invisible airborne toxins.

Medieval urban life intensified cesspool stench fears, as open sewage, butchery offal, and rotting waste clogged unpaved town streets. Breathing this foul, pestilential air was believed to poison vital spirits inside the lungs, causing acute unbalanced bodily fluids and rapid humoral decay. This environmental explanation encouraged municipal councils to clear street dung and burn fragrant tar barrels during epidemics to purify the air.

KEY ENQUIRY “Why did standard medieval medical treatments frequently endanger the lives of the patients they were meant to cure?”
STAGE 1 Spiritual Remedies: Prayer, Penance and Processions c1250
“Why did ordinary people consider prayer more vital than medicine?”

Before administering physical medicines, medieval patients sought divine reconciliation to restore spiritual grace. Because sickness was interpreted as a trial or punishment sent by God, religious treatments were mandatory. Communities engaged in devout penance and fasting, while monastic clergy recited continuous chanted Latin prayers and held special intercessory masses beside sickbeds, imploring saints to intercede and beg God for physical and spiritual deliverance.

During catastrophic epidemics like the 1348 Black Death, religious panic reached extreme heights across Europe. Believing the apocalypse was at hand, radical religious sects formed flagellant whipping processions, marching barefoot between towns while scourging their bare backs with iron-tipped whips to appease God’s wrath. Although extreme, flagellation demonstrated how deeply ordinary medieval people believed that spiritual penance was the only true cure.

STAGE 2 Phlebotomy: Restoring Humoral Balance by Bloodletting c1300
“Why was bloodletting the most universal physical treatment?”

The most widespread rational physical treatment in medieval medicine was bloodletting, or phlebotomy. Humoral pathology taught that excess blood caused inflammation, fever, and violent disease. Surgeons performed phlebotomy (vein incision) using specialised iron fleams, or applied freshwater leeches to delicate skin, allowing these blood-sucking worms to extract corrupted fluid until the patient turned pale or faint.

Another popular bloodletting method was dry or wet cupping, using heated brass or pewter cupping vessels to create a vacuum over scarified skin, drawing blood to the surface. Barber-surgeons collected the extracted blood into measured pewter bowls to inspect its colour, thickness, and smell. Bloodletting was administered routinely as preventative healthcare every spring, leaving millions of medieval patients dangerously weakened.

Approaches to Prevention & Treatment: Rituals, Bleeding & Purging — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 Purging, Emetics and Clysters: Evacuating Corrupt Humours c1320
“How was purging believed to restore bodily equilibrium?”

When phlebotomy was deemed insufficient to balance humours, physicians prescribed systemic purging to cleanse the digestive tract. To induce violent vomiting, doctors administered powerful emetics (black hellebore), bitter herbs, and antimony potions that forced the stomach to expel toxic yellow bile. These caustic herbal concoctions caused agonising abdominal cramps and dehydration, yet were celebrated for cleansing internal impurities.

To purge the lower intestines, medical practitioners administered herbal laxatives and clysters (enemas). Using a hollow pewter pipe attached to an oiled pig's bladder, barber-surgeons injected warm water, bitter herbs, and crushed chamomile deep into the rectum. This aggressive humoural fluid evacuation was intended to expel black bile and putrid matter, reflecting the core medieval belief that expelling fluids restored internal bodily harmony.

STAGE 4 Herbal Remedies, Simples and the Great Theriac c1350
“Why did herbal recipes remain popular across all social classes?”

When treating non-fatal everyday illnesses, medieval patients turned to herbal pharmacopeia drawn from classical manuscripts like Dioscorides' Herbal Materia Medica. Healers gathered local medicinal plants, preparing herbal teas, soothing honey syrups, and warm poultices from mint, chamomile, sage, and plantain. For ordinary peasant households, these traditional botanical remedies provided affordable, comforting relief for coughs, cuts, joint aches, and digestive discomfort.

For wealthy elites, apothecaries compounded the legendary universal antidote theriac (70 ingredients), which blended crushed snake flesh, opium, exotic Arabian spices, and wine. Sold as expensive remedies by local apothecaries, theriac was believed to neutralise internal poisons and ward off pestilence. While herbs like willow bark offered mild anti-inflammatory relief, most complex medieval compounds possessed zero clinical power against virulent bacterial infections.

STAGE 5 The Regimen Sanitatis and Preventative Health c1400
“How did wealthy barons attempt to prevent sickness before it struck?”

Alongside curative treatments, medieval medicine emphasised preventative lifestyle moderation codified in the widely translated Salernitan Regimen Sanitatis rules. Originating from the medical school at Salerno, this guide instructed citizens to balance six essential factors: diet, exercise, sleep, digestion, fresh air, and emotional tranquility. Moderation was key: overeating, sudden rage, and excessive sleeping were believed to destabilise delicate humoral equilibrium.

Because miasma was feared as a major cause of disease, medieval prevention focused on environmental cleansing. Wealthy townspeople carried sweet pomander scents, holding hollow silver orbs filled with fragrant ambergris and cloves to their noses. Citizens also purified indoor air by burning fragrant lavender indoors and lighting tar barrels in the streets to counteract foul atmospheric stenches, illustrating rational attempts to ward off airborne infection.

KEY ENQUIRY “How did social hierarchy and the Catholic Church shape who provided medical care in medieval England?”
STAGE 1 University-Trained Physicians: Theory Over Practice c1250
“Why were university physicians accessible only to the ultra-wealthy?”

Medieval healthcare in England was strictly stratified by social wealth. At the top of the professional medical hierarchy stood elite university-trained physicians, educated at Oxford, Cambridge, Paris, or Bologna. These doctors spent up to ten years studying ancient Latin medical texts and astrological star charts, mastering Galenic humoral theory and astrological almanacs without ever touching a patient or receiving practical surgical instruction.

Because few university physicians practised in England—fewer than one hundred by 1300—they charged exorbitant high diagnostic fees, catering exclusively to the royal court, wealthy bishops, and noble aristocrats. A physician evaluated uroscopy samples, consulted astrological charts, and wrote Latin prescriptions, but considered manual surgical labour beneath their professional dignity, delegating physical operations to practical barber-surgeons.

STAGE 2 Guild Apothecaries and Spicers: Retail Medicine c1300
“How did apothecaries provide accessible medicines to townspeople?”

For urban merchants and craftspeople who could not afford a university physician, the local apothecary shop was the first port of call. Operating under strict apothecary guild trade regulations, apothecaries trained through rigorous apprenticeships rather than academic degrees. They possessed detailed practical knowledge of botanical chemistry, compounding simples, electuaries, mineral powders, and medicinal syrups imported from Mediterranean and Eastern trade routes.

In their shops, diverse herbal preparations were sold alongside spices, preserved fruits, and scented candles. Although physicians viewed apothecaries with professional suspicion, accusing them of prescribing drugs without diagnostic training, apothecaries flourished by providing cheaper urban access to medicine. Ordinary townspeople frequently consulted apothecaries directly, purchasing inexpensive herbal purgatives, warming chest cordials, and teething balms without paying an expensive physician’s consultation fee.

Medical Care Providers & Monastic Hospitals: ‘Care Not Cure’ — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 Barber-Surgeons and Practical Craft Medicine c1320
“Why were barber-surgeons viewed as lower-status craftsmen?”

For physical trauma, wound care, and operative procedures, medieval townspeople relied on the practical craftsmen of the barber-surgeon guild. Ranked far below physicians in social status, barber-surgeons served apprenticeships and were associated with barbers because both professions wielded sharp razors. They performed everyday minor surgery, providing bloodletting and tooth extraction, lancing painful boils, and stitching battlefield flesh wounds.

Barber-surgeons also tackled dangerous emergency operations, including emergency cauterisation and amputation of crushed limbs and removing bladder stones using specialised iron scoops. Without anaesthetics or antiseptic knowledge, patients were held down by assistants while surgeons worked with raw speed. Wounds were sealed with boiling elderberry oil or red-hot irons, resulting in immense shock, agony, and high mortality rates from postoperative hospital gangrene.

STAGE 4 Monastic Hospitals: The Philosophy of 'Care Not Cure' c1350
“Why did monastic hospitals deliberately avoid medical treatment?”

By 1500, England possessed over 1,100 hospitals, virtually all founded, funded, and operated by Christian religious orders as monastic charity hospitals. Institutions such as St Bartholomew's and St Thomas' in London were run by Catholic monks and nuns. Rather than treating contagious diseases, hospitals provided refuge for travellers, elderly pilgrims, the blind, and the dying, offering food and shelter.

Medieval hospitals operated strictly under a care not cure philosophy, viewing spiritual salvation as far more important than physical bodily healing. Patients were kept warm, given clean linen, fed wholesome soups, and supported by quiet bed rest, cleanliness, and prayer. Chapels were positioned so bedridden inmates could observe the elevation of the Host, preparing their souls for heaven rather than undergoing medical surgery.

STAGE 5 Domestic Women Healers and Household Medicine c1400
“Why did the vast majority of sick people rely on female family members?”

Over ninety percent of England’s medieval population lived in rural villages, far from university physicians or town guild apothecaries. For these ordinary families, healthcare was delivered almost entirely by domestic women healers, including mothers, grandmothers, and local village wise women. Women held responsibility for family health, preparing remedies from handwritten recipe books (leechbooks) passed down through generations of rural homemakers.

Relying on extensive local herb gardening, women grew sage, chamomile, mint, and elderberry to brew soothing fever teas, poultices, and cough syrups. Wise women also performed vital midwifery and domestic nursing, delivering newborn infants, caring for the elderly, and nursing bedridden neighbours. Despite delivering practical bedside care for centuries, their position remained vulnerable to accusations of witchcraft if treatments failed to cure.

STAGE 1 The Pestilence Arrives: Maritime Trade and Melcombe Regis 1348
“How did trade routes unwittingly bring the plague to England?”

In the summer of 1348, the Second Plague Pandemic struck England through international maritime commerce. A merchant cog from Gascony dropped anchor at Melcombe Regis in Dorset in June 1348, carrying a lethal cargo across the English Channel. Within the ship's hold, cargo crates carried the microscopic Yersinia pestis bacteria, harboured within the blood of black rats and transmitted by parasitic tropical rat fleas.

When dockers unloaded wool sacks and grain baskets onto wharves, infected black rats and Oriental rat fleas escaped into crowded port alleyways. Medieval timber dwellings, open rubbish heaps, and unpaved streets provided the ideal environment for the rodent population to multiply rapidly. Within weeks, the infection established an unstoppable foothold, spreading inland along river trade routes and reaching London by autumn 1348.

STAGE 2 The Dual Pathology: Bubonic Swellings and Pneumonic Terror Autumn 1348
“Why was the pneumonic strain far deadlier than the bubonic form?”

The Black Death presented in two lethal clinical manifestations. In its bubonic form, flea bites injected plague bacteria directly into the human lymphatic system, producing agonising bubonic groin swellings (buboes), blackened neck tumours, and armpit sores that oozed foul pus and blood. Sufferers experienced raging fevers, violent vomiting, dark subcutaneous blotches, and unbearable muscle spasms before falling into comatose states.

Far more terrifying was the pneumonic form, which occurred when bacteria infected the lungs. Victims began pneumonic bloody coughing, aerosolising bacteria directly into shared room air and infecting entire households through breathing. The virulence was devastating, with mortality in three days following initial fever, killing nearly one hundred percent of pneumonic victims and leaving entire peasant families dead in their timber cottages.

STAGE 3 Desperate Cures: Live Hens, Bloodletting and Crushed Emeralds Winter 1348
“Why were medical treatments completely useless against the plague?”

Lacking understanding of bacterial pathology, medieval medical treatments proved completely futile against the plague. Convinced the pandemic was caused by divine wrath and corrupt miasma, healers tried sympathetic remedies to draw poison from the body. Folk practitioners recommended lancing swollen buboes or strapping live plucked hens or warm dried toads directly against swollen groin sores, which only caused agonising pain, blood poisoning, and septicaemia.

Qualified physicians relied on aggressive bloodletting and costly apothecary potions, draining pints of blood from weakened patients and feeding wealthy nobles expensive potions containing crushed gemstones dissolved in vinegar. Concurrently, patients swallowed powdered theriac containing snake venom, while religious zealots engaged in public flagellation. None of these desperate treatments possessed any pharmacological efficacy, and mortality rates remained catastrophic across all social classes.

STAGE 4 Quarantine, Butchery Orders and Environmental Cleansing 1349
“How did belief in miasma shape royal prevention orders?”

As death rates soared, municipal authorities attempted emergency public health measures. In Gloucester and London, town councils introduced rudimentary quarantine isolation rules, shutting infected families inside their houses and barring visitors from affected neighbouring parishes. However, because authorities had no concept of flea-borne transmission, isolating humans inside flea-infested timber dwellings actually ensured that entire trapped families perished together in quarantine.

Attributing the epidemic to corrupt miasma, King Edward III sent urgent letters demanding cleaning town butchery waste from London streets, ordering butchers to stop dumping offal and animal entrails into the River Thames. Furthermore, councils constructed continuous street fire smoke barriers, burning pitch, sulphur, and sweet herbs at crossroads day and night to purify the corrupt atmosphere, though these measures failed to stop plague fleas.

STAGE 5 Demographic Collapse: Mass Graves and Feudal Transformation 1349–1350
“How did losing a third of England’s people weaken feudal serfdom?”

By winter 1349, the immense volume of dead overwhelmed churchyards. Authorities purchased suburban fields, excavating deep mass burial trenches at East Smithfield outside London walls, where corpses were stacked five deep in rough timber coffins. Between 1348 and 1350, between one-third and one-half of England’s population perished, wiping out an estimated 1.5 to 2 million people across England and leaving entire villages completely abandoned.

This demographic catastrophe permanently transformed medieval feudal society. The sudden annihilation of agricultural workers generated an acute labour shortage and rising peasant wages, empowering surviving peasants to demand higher wages and freely leave their lords' manors. When Parliament attempted to freeze wages through the 1351 Statute of Labourers, deep peasant resentment erupted, fatally undermining feudal servitude and culminating in the 1381 Peasants' Revolt.

God's Punishment Enquiry 1

The widespread medieval belief that epidemic disease was a divine retribution sent by God to punish humanity for its sins and test Christian faith.

Four Humours Enquiry 2

The foundational ancient medical doctrine asserting that human health depended on an equilibrium among four bodily fluids: blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile.

Bloodletting Enquiry 3

Draining excess blood by cutting a vein, applying leeches, or using heated cups to rebalance the Four Humours.

Monastic Infirmary Enquiry 4

Hospital wards established inside medieval monasteries and abbeys offering hospitality, shelter, and palliative care to travellers, pilgrims, and the infirm.

Black Death Enquiry 5

The catastrophic bubonic and pneumonic plague pandemic that swept England in 1348–49, killing between 30% and 50% of the population.

Flagellation Enquiry 1

A ritual of public self-scourging with knotted ropes and iron tips undertaken by religious penitents to appease divine wrath during plague outbreaks.

Astrology Enquiry 1

The medical belief that celestial movements, planetary conjunctions, and eclipses directly influenced bodily humours and precipitated epidemics.

The Medieval Church Enquiry 1

The supreme institutional authority that controlled education, medical training, libraries, and hospitals, enforcing strict adherence to Christian and Galenic orthodoxy.

Theory of Opposites Enquiry 2

Galen's therapeutic principle prescribing remedies possessing qualities contrary to the disease (e.g. treating cold, wet phlegm with hot, dry pepper).

Hippocratic Oath Enquiry 2

An ancient ethical code sworn by medical practitioners binding them to treat patients diligently, protect life, and preserve confidentiality.

Clinical Observation Enquiry 2

The diagnostic practice pioneered by Hippocrates of carefully examining, recording, and monitoring a patient's symptoms at the bedside before formulating a diagnosis.

Galenic Dogma Enquiry 2

The unchallengeable medical authority attributed to Claudius Galen, whose anatomical writings were preserved and dogmatically enforced by the Catholic Church.

Purging Enquiry 3

Emptying the stomach or bowels using emetics (to cause vomiting) or laxatives to clear excess humours.

Regimen Sanitatis Enquiry 3

Preventative health rules on diet, exercise, bathing, sleep, and clean air prescribed by physicians to stay healthy.
