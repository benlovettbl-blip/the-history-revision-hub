Source A: A private diary entry from an RAMC surgeon at a Casualty Clearing Station (CCS) near the Somme, August 1916.
Gas gangrene is claiming too many of our men with deep shrapnel wounds. Today we fully implemented the Carrel-Dakin method of chemical wound irrigation. By inserting rubber tubes directly into the deep tissue, we can flush the wound continuously with a sterilised salt solution. Source B: An official British Army medical directive issued to RAMC units in late 1915.
URGENT: PREVENTATIVE MEASURES FOR TETANUS. It is strictly ordered that all stretcher bearers and medical personnel at Regimental Aid Posts must ensure the immediate administration of anti-tetanus serum to any casualty presenting with shrapnel or gunshot wounds. The high concentration of manure in the French soil has led to unacceptable mortality rates from tetanus infection. Immediate injection is mandatory before evacuation down the line.
1 (a) Describe one feature of the Thomas splint used on the Western Front.(2 marks)
Examiner Model Answer (2/2 Marks)

Feature: Introduced across the Western Front from December 1915 by Sir Robert Jones, the Thomas splint used a padded iron ring and rigid metal side-bars to hold a fractured thigh bone (femur) under continuous traction.

Detail: Immobilising the jagged broken bone ends stopped them grinding together during bumpy stretcher and ambulance journeys, preventing severed femoral arteries, fatal blood loss, and gas gangrene—slashing compound femur fracture mortality from ~80% in 1914–15 to under 20% by 1916–17.

1 (b) Describe one feature of communication trenches on the Western Front.(2 marks)
Examiner Model Answer (2/2 Marks)

Feature: Communication trenches ran perpendicular behind the frontline, linking the firing trench rearward to the support trench, reserve trench, and Regimental Aid Posts.

Detail: Dug in zig-zagging angles below ground level to shield reinforcements and RAMC stretcher bearers from German sniper fire and shell splinters, their narrow width and knee-deep mud frequently created severe bottlenecks during casualty evacuation.

How useful are Sources A and B for an enquiry into the prevention and surgical management of deep wound infections on the Western Front?

Explain your answer, using Sources A and B and your knowledge of the historical context.(8 marks)
Examiner Model Answer (8/8 Marks • Grade 9)

Source A is highly useful for an enquiry into the surgical management of deep wound infections because it records the frontline clinical adoption of the Carrel-Dakin method at a Somme Casualty Clearing Station in August 1916. The surgeon notes that gas gangrene was killing men with deep shrapnel wounds, prompting his team to thread perforated rubber tubes into excised wound cavities to flush them continuously with freshly mixed sterilised Dakin’s solution (buffered sodium hypochlorite), noticeably cutting infections and amputations. This aligns closely with contextual knowledge: traditional Listerian surface carbolic dressings failed against anaerobic Clostridium perfringens driven deep into muscle with manured soil, forcing surgeons to combine radical wound debridement (cutting away dead tissue and khaki cloth) with Carrel-Dakin chemical irrigation before delayed stitching. Its provenance as a candid August 1916 private diary by an operating CCS surgeon during the Somme offensive gives reliable eyewitness insight into both the method’s success and its practical drawback—that unstable hypochlorite took six hours to prepare and expired quickly during mass casualty surges.

Source B is equally useful because it demonstrates the British Army’s highly successful preventative response to a second lethal soil-borne wound infection: tetanus. Issued as an urgent late-1915 army directive, it orders all medical personnel at Regimental Aid Posts to inject anti-tetanus serum immediately into every shrapnel or gunshot casualty before evacuation down the line because manured French farmland caused high tetanus mortality. Contextually, tetanus killed scores of wounded British soldiers during the autumn 1914 battles on the Marne and First Ypres, but once prophylactic anti-tetanus serum injections were made routine at RAPs and Dressing Stations from late 1914–1915, tetanus deaths plummeted almost to zero—contrasting starkly with gas gangrene, for which no vaccine or serum cure succeeded. As a binding official RAMC order, Source B reliably proves army-wide preventative policy, though an order alone does not show supply challenges during the chaotic opening weeks of 1914.

Overall, Sources A and B are highly useful together: Source B shows how biological serum injections solved tetanus right at the RAP, whereas Source A shows why gas gangrene required complex surgical irrigation further back at the CCS.

Section B: Medicine in Britain, c1250–present (36 Marks)
3. Explain one way in which care in hospitals in the medieval period (c1250–c1500) was different from care in hospitals in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries (c1700–c1900).(4 marks)
Examiner Model Answer (4/4 Marks • Grade 9)

One major difference was that medieval hospitals provided religious hospitality ("care, not cure") run by monks and nuns, whereas nineteenth-century hospitals evolved into secular medical institutions focused on clinical nursing and antiseptic surgery.

In Medieval England (c1250–c1500), around 1,100 Church-run hospitals such as St Bartholomew’s offered rest, clean linen, broth, and constant chapel prayers for the patient’s soul; infectious or terminal cases were usually turned away (except specialist leper houses), and physicians rarely visited.

By contrast, in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Britain (c1700–c1900), voluntary and municipal hospitals employed salaried surgeons, teaching doctors, and—following Florence Nightingale’s 1860 Training School at St Thomas’s and Joseph Lister’s 1865 carbolic antiseptics—trained secular nurses and sterile operating theatres actively curing surgical and infectious disease.

4. Explain why medical treatments and daily bedside care remained largely stagnant during the Renaissance period (c1500–c1700), despite new scientific and anatomical discoveries. You may use the following in your answer: • William Harvey’s circulation of the blood • the continuing influence of the Theory of the Four Humours You must also use information of your own.(12 marks)
Stimulus points: William Harvey's circulation of the blood • The continuing influence of the Theory of the Four Humours
Examiner Model Answer (12/12 Marks • Grade 9)

The primary reason why Renaissance medical treatments remained stagnant was that the era's groundbreaking anatomical and physiological discoveries had no direct, practical application for curing sick patients at the bedside. This clinical gap is perfectly illustrated by William Harvey's discovery of the circulation of the blood, published in De Motu Cordis (1628). Harvey utilised rigorous experimentation and dissection to prove that the heart acted as a muscular pump circulating blood in a one-way system, disproving Galen's classical theory that the liver constantly manufactured new blood from food. However, while Harvey's work permanently corrected human physiology, it could not cure illnesses. Renaissance doctors lacked the microscopic technology to see bacteria, understand the causes of infection, or safely perform blood transfusions. Consequently, a physician in 1640 might fully accept Harvey's circulation theory, yet possess no new therapeutic tools to treat a patient, ensuring absolute continuity in bedside treatments.

Because scientific progress failed to provide new clinical cures, both professional physicians and the general public clung to the familiar Theory of the Four Humours as the only logical explanation for disease. Sickness continued to be diagnosed as an excess or deficiency of blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile. Consequently, physicians continued to prescribe traditional humoural therapies like bleeding, purging, and the Theory of Opposites to restore internal fluid balance. The strength of this continuity was demonstrated in 1685 when royal doctors treated King Charles II. Despite living in the age of the Scientific Revolution, the King's physicians subjected him to extensive bleeding, blistering, and purging-treatments directly inherited from Galen and Hippocrates. Because humoural theory remained the only dominant conceptual framework for pathology, treatments remained completely frozen.

Furthermore, the stagnation of daily care was reinforced by the deep conservatism of licensing bodies and the decentralised, non-scientific nature of community healthcare, which provides a distinct third factor beyond the stimulus points. The Royal College of Physicians, which regulated medical practice in London, was highly conservative and frequently persecuted free-thinkers who attempted to abandon classical therapies. At the same time, the vast majority of the population could not afford university-trained physicians and relied on traditional community healers. Ordinary citizens bought cheap herbal remedies from local apothecaries, underwent bloodletting by barber-surgeons, or were treated at home by female family members using recipes passed down through generations. Because these community practitioners operated within an oral, traditional culture completely separated from the academic discoveries of Vesalius or Harvey, daily bedside care remained untouched by scientific progress, maintaining absolute continuity for the sick.

Question 5 Examiner Model Answer (20/20 Marks • Grade 9)

To a moderate extent, I agree that the reliance on Claudius Galen's unscientific ideas was a major factor in the ineffectiveness of medieval medical care and treatment. Because Galen's physiology was accepted as absolute truth, medieval treatments were logically designed to target incorrect internal causes, often resulting in harmful practices like bloodletting. However, Galen's authority was merely a symptom of a larger, structural root cause: the Roman Catholic Church's absolute monopoly over education and university curricula, which actively prohibited scientific human dissection. Furthermore, the total absence of microscopic diagnostic technology made it physically impossible to identify the true, biological causes of disease, leaving medieval healers with no option but to rely on humoural and supernatural explanations.

On the one hand, medical treatment was highly ineffective because physicians based their clinical therapies on Galen's unscientific models, particularly the Theory of Opposites. Galen had expanded Hippocrates' Theory of the Four Humours by teaching that good health required blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile to be in perfect balance. Under the Theory of Opposites, Galen taught that an excess of one humour should be treated with its opposite quality-such as treating a cold, wet winter fever (phlegm) with hot pepper or dry wine. Because Galen was prohibited from dissecting human bodies by Roman law, he relied on animal dissections (pigs and apes), leading him to make major errors, such as claiming the human lower jaw consists of two bones. However, medieval physicians accepted his texts as infallible. Consequently, when treating patients, they focused on extracting supposed humoural excesses through extensive bloodletting and purging. Rather than curing infections, draining blood from critically ill or elderly patients severely weakened their immune systems and often directly caused death, proving that Galenic theory made medieval medicine actively dangerous.

On the other hand, the primary reason why these unscientific ideas remained completely unchallenged was because the medieval Catholic Church used its immense institutional power to freeze medical progress. The Church controlled all universities, libraries, and the copying of medical manuscripts by monastic scribes. Because Galen's anatomical writings argued that the human body was so incredibly complex and purposeful that it must have been designed by a single 'Creator,' his ideas aligned perfectly with Christian creation theology. Consequently, the Church declared Galen's texts infallible and banned human dissection for scientific research, believing that the physical body had to be buried whole to allow the soul to go to heaven. In rare academic demonstrations, the body of an executed criminal was dissected by an untrained assistant while the university professor sat far away, reading Galen's words aloud strictly to prove the book was correct. If an anatomical detail on the table contradicted the text, the professor would claim the body itself was deformed rather than questioning Galen. This religious monopoly completely blocked any progress.

Furthermore, medieval medical care was heavily limited by the spiritual, non-clinical function of hospitals, combined with a total lack of diagnostic technology. Run by monks and nuns, the 1,100 charity hospitals in England by 1500 did not aim to provide medical cures. Instead, their primary focus was on spiritual care and hospitality-providing the poor, travellers, and the elderly with shelter, clean sheets, and hot meals while preparing their souls for the afterlife. Patients suffering from highly infectious diseases (such as leprosy or the plague) or terminal illnesses were strictly excluded to prevent the spread of infection. Additionally, in an era before the invention of powerful microscopes, it was physically impossible to see microscopic bacteria or prove that pathogens caused infections. This lack of technology left medieval people with no alternative but to rely on other unscientific, non-Galenic ideas, such as believing that God sent the Black Death in 1348 as a punishment for sin or that bad air (miasma) corrupted the body's humours.

In conclusion, I disagree only to a moderate extent that Galen was the main cause of ineffective medical care. While it is true that humoural treatments like the Theory of Opposites were clinically harmful and biologically useless, Galen's ideas did not survive on their own merit. The true root cause of medical stagnation was the institutional authority of the Church, which used its control over libraries and education to ban human dissection and prosecute free-thinkers. Without the intellectual freedom to experiment, and lacking the microscope technology required to discover microbes, medieval medicine was structurally doomed to remain ineffective.

Question 6 Examiner Model Answer (20/20 Marks • Grade 9)

The creation of the National Health Service (NHS) on 5 July 1948 was unquestionably a watershed in the delivery and accessibility of healthcare in Britain, ending centuries in which life-saving medical care depended on ability to pay. Yet when assessing care and treatment across the whole modern period (c1900–present), the NHS was an organisational delivery system rather than a cure in itself: without the pharmaceutical revolution of magic bullets and mass-produced antibiotics, and the technological revolution of high-tech surgery and radiotherapy, NHS doctors would have had remarkably few effective treatments to offer.

In support of the statement, Aneurin Bevan’s launch of the NHS in 1948 transformed care for tens of millions of Britons previously excluded from affordable medicine. Even after Lloyd George’s 1911 National Insurance Act gave low-paid wage-earners GP cover, uninsured women, children, the elderly, and the chronically sick still faced ruinous doctor’s bills or charity means-testing, leaving millions with untreated dental decay, defective eyesight, and uterine prolapse. Funded through central taxation and structured into a 'tripartite' network of over 2,600 nationalised hospitals, family GPs/dentists/opticians, and local authority maternity and vaccination clinics, the NHS guaranteed 'cradle-to-grave' care free at the point of delivery—prompting over 8 million pairs of spectacles and hundreds of millions of prescriptions in its early years and steadily lifting British life expectancy.

However, chemical 'magic bullets' and antibiotics were arguably an even more decisive breakthrough in treatment, because for the first time in human history doctors could destroy lethal bacteria inside the bloodstream. Before the twentieth century, even wealthy private patients died routinely from septicaemia, pneumonia, scarlet fever, or meningitis. Paul Ehrlich and Sahachiro Hata’s Salvarsan 606 (1909) against syphilis and Gerhard Domagk’s Prontosil sulphonamide (1932) proved targeted chemical therapy worked, before Howard Florey and Ernst Chain purified Alexander Fleming’s Penicillium mould (1940–41) and partnered with US pharmaceutical firms to mass-produce penicillin by 1944. Penicillin and successor antibiotics (such as streptomycin for tuberculosis, 1943) slashed infectious disease mortality by over 90%, saving millions of lives globally whether inside or outside the NHS—though modern MRSA antibiotic resistance now poses a growing challenge.

Furthermore, scientific and engineering advances in high-tech hospital surgery and oncology transformed previously untreatable organ failures and cancers. Building on Karl Landsteiner’s ABO blood groups (1901), refrigerated blood banks, and precision anaesthetics, post-war surgeons pioneered open-heart bypass machines, Sir John Charnley’s hip replacements (1962), kidney transplants (1954) and Christiaan Barnard’s heart transplant (1967) aided by immunosuppressant drugs, fibre-optic keyhole laparoscopy, robotic microsurgery, CyberKnife stereotactic radiotherapy, and targeted cancer immunotherapies. These technological marvels dramatically reduced surgical trauma, hospital stays, and fatal post-operative complications.

In conclusion, neither the NHS nor scientific therapeutics could have achieved modern Britain’s medical revolution alone. Magic bullets, antibiotics, and high-tech surgical engineering created the actual biological and mechanical power to cure disease, while the creation of the NHS in 1948 was the decisive social and institutional breakthrough that ensured every citizen—rich or poor—received those life-saving treatments equally.
