Medicine in Britain, c1250–Present & The British Sector of the Western Front, 1914–1918

Complete 30-milestone revision chronology across Key Topics 1–5, anchored to the 6 Paper 1 thematic factors (Q4 12m & Q5/Q6 16m + 4 SPaG) and Section B Q3 [4m] cross-period comparisons.

The 6 Paper 1 Thematic Factors (Change & Continuity Drivers)

Individuals & Genius
Individual pioneers—from Vesalius, Harvey, and Jenner to Pasteur, Koch, Nightingale, Lister, Florey, Chain, and Bevan—challenged orthodoxy through observation, persistence, and bold experimentation.
Institutions: Church & Government
Institutional power shifted from the Medieval Catholic Church enforcing Galenic orthodoxy to the secular Royal Society (1660), compulsory Victorian sanitation (1875), Allied penicillin funding, the NHS (1948), and modern anti-smoking legislation.
Science & Technology
Scientific method and technology evolved from mechanical pumps and early microscopes to 1,000x compound lenses, agar plates, chemical dyes, X-rays, deep-tank fermentation, DNA crystallography, CT/MRI scanners, and robotic keyhole surgery.
War & Teamwork
Conflict accelerated urgent practical teamwork: Crimean mortality drove Nightingale’s ward reforms, Franco-Prussian rivalry spurred Pasteur and Koch, WWI forged the Thomas splint, X-ray vans, plastic surgery, and blood banks, and WWII mass-produced penicillin.
Communication
Gutenberg’s printing press (c.1440), Royal Society journals (1665), Snow’s epidemiological maps (1854), The Lancet (1867), Nature (1953), global genomic databases, and televised/plain-pack NHS campaigns spread and tested breakthroughs.
Attitudes in Society
Societal attitudes acted as both brake and accelerator—moving from medieval deference to authority and Victorian laissez-faire resistance toward scientific enquiry, working-class democratic pressure (1867), and post-1945 universal welfare.
KT1 • MEDIEVAL

KEY TOPIC 1: Medicine in Medieval England, c1250–c1500

Lessons KT1.1–KT1.5 • Church Authority, Four Humours, Miasma, Monastic Care Not Cure & The Black Death (1348–49)

c.1250

1. Church Authority, Sin & Monastic Manuscript Preservation

KT1.1: Supernatural & Religious Explanations of Disease Institutions: Church & GovtAttitudes in Society
Before / Context: Across medieval England, the Catholic Church dominated literacy, parish preaching, and university medical training at Oxford and Cambridge, teaching that famine, leprosy, and epidemic disease could be sent by God to punish sin or test faith.
Breakthrough / Action: Monastic scriptoria painstakingly hand-copied hundreds of Greek, Roman, and Arabic medical manuscripts (including Hippocrates, Galen, and Avicenna’s Canon of Medicine), preserving ancient medical scholarship across Europe.
Impact & Limits: Because Claudius Galen wrote that the body’s design proved a single Creator, the Church endorsed Galenic texts as unquestionable orthodoxy, restricted human dissection, and marginalised Franciscan scholar Roger Bacon (c.1277) when he urged independent experimentation.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Difference vs KT2: Renaissance (c1500–1700)): “Explain one way in which institutional control over medical knowledge in medieval England (c1250–c1500) was different from institutional support for medical science in Renaissance Britain (c1500–c1700). (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: In Medieval England (c1250–1500), the Catholic Church controlled universities and book copying to prove Galen’s ancient texts right rather than test new ideas. By contrast, in Renaissance Britain (1660), King Charles II chartered the secular Royal Society under Nullius in verba ("take nobody’s word for it") to sponsor practical experiments and publish peer-reviewed findings in Philosophical Transactions.
c.1250–1350

2. Hippocrates, Galen & The Four Humours Orthodoxy

KT1.2: Hippocrates, Galen & The Four Humours Individuals & GeniusAttitudes in Society
Before / Context: Alongside religious and astrological explanations, university-educated medieval physicians relied on natural theories inherited from Hippocrates (5th century BC) and Claudius Galen (2nd century AD).
Breakthrough / Action: Physicians diagnosed patients using uroscopy charts (inspecting urine colour, clarity, smell, and taste) and zodiac calendars to determine which of the Four Humours—blood, phlegm, yellow bile, or black bile—was imbalanced, while blaming foul miasma for corrupting bodily humours.
Impact & Limits: Galen’s Theory of Opposites (treating hot, wet fevers with cool cucumber or cold phlegm with hot pepper) appeared logical and all-embracing, trapping European medicine in humoral stagnation for over 1,400 years without identifying germs.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Difference vs KT3: Industrial (c1700–1900)): “Explain one way in which rational ideas about the causes of disease in medieval England (c1250–c1500) were different from scientific explanations of disease in Britain during the late nineteenth century. (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: In Medieval England (c1250–1500), rational medicine blamed internal imbalance of the Four Humours or poisonous miasma inhaled from rotting matter. By contrast, after 1861–1884 in Industrial Britain, Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch proved scientifically that specific external micro-organisms invade the body to cause specific diseases like TB and cholera.
c.1300–1400

3. Humoral Treatments: Phlebotomy, Purging, Theriac & Regimen Sanitatis

KT1.3: Rituals, Bleeding & Purging Attitudes in Society
Before / Context: Because medieval practitioners believed sickness resulted from excess or putrefied humours, treatments sought to drain fluids from the body while prevention focused on spiritual purity and moderate lifestyle.
Breakthrough / Action: Barber-surgeons practised phlebotomy (cutting veins, applying leeches, or heated cupping guided by Vein Man charts), apothecaries mixed herbal laxatives, emetics, and up to 60-ingredient theriac, and physicians wrote personalised Regimen Sanitatis guides advising bathing, moderate diet, sleep, and clean air.
Impact & Limits: While herbal remedies (such as honey, aloe, and mint) had mild soothing or antiseptic properties and Regimen hygiene helped wealthy patrons, repeated bloodletting and violent purging often weakened and killed feverish patients.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Similarity vs KT2: Renaissance (c1500–1700)): “Explain one way in which everyday medical treatments in medieval England (c1250–c1500) were similar to everyday medical treatments in Medical Renaissance Britain (c1500–c1700). (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: Across both Medieval (c1250–1500) and Renaissance (c1500–1700) England, healers continued to treat illness with phlebotomy, herbal purges, and theriac—even bleeding King Charles II repeatedly before his death in 1685—because anatomical breakthroughs by Vesalius and Harvey had not replaced humoral ideas of disease.
1123–c.1450

4. Medieval Care Providers & Monastic Hospitals ('Care Not Cure')

KT1.4: Medical Care Providers & Monastic Hospitals ('Care Not Cure') Institutions: Church & GovtAttitudes in Society
Before / Context: With fewer than a hundred university-trained physicians in 14th-century England charging steep fees, ordinary families relied on home care by wise women and mothers, apothecaries, and barber-surgeons.
Breakthrough / Action: By 1500, England possessed roughly 1,100 small hospitals—around 30% run directly by monasteries and the rest funded by wealthy endowments for their souls (such as St Bartholomew’s, founded 1123)—offering clean linen, warm soup, rest, and round-the-clock chapel prayer.
Impact & Limits: Operating on the Christian principle of ‘care, not cure’ (since tampering with God-sent illness seemed presumptuous), most hospitals employed no doctors and barred infectious, terminal, mentally ill, or pregnant patients, isolating lepers in separate lazar houses.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Difference vs KT3: Industrial (c1700–1900)): “Explain one way in which hospital care in medieval England (c1250–c1500) was different from hospital care in Britain in the second half of the nineteenth century. (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: Medieval monastic hospitals (c1250–1500) focused on spiritual hospitality—prayer, confession, and rest—while turning away infectious patients and rarely admitting physicians. By contrast, after Florence Nightingale’s reforms (1854–1863), Victorian pavilion hospitals employed secular, professionally trained nurses and doctors inside scrubbed, cross-ventilated wards with isolation wings dedicated to curing disease.
1348–1349

5. Case Study: The Black Death Arrives in England

KT1.5: Case Study: Dealing with the Black Death (1348–49) Institutions: Church & GovtAttitudes in Society
Before / Context: When Yersinia pestis arrived at Melcombe Regis in Dorset in June 1348, medieval contemporaries blamed God’s wrath for human sin, an ominous 1345 alignment of Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn, and corrupt miasma.
Breakthrough / Action: Clergy led national fasting and barefoot prayer processions (while visiting Flagellants whipped themselves in public), healers lanced buboes or applied herbal poultices and live chickens, households burned sweet herbs and carried pomanders, and King Edward III ordered London’s Mayor in 1349 to clear rotting filth from streets.
Impact & Limits: Ignorant of rat fleas and airborne droplets—and lacking organised household quarantine (even gathering crowds in church processions)—medieval England lost between one-third and half of its entire population within eighteen months.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Similarity vs KT2: Renaissance (c1500–1700)): “Explain one way in which ideas and methods used to prevent plague during the Black Death (1348–49) were similar to those used during the Great Plague of London (1665). (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: During both the 1348–49 Black Death and the 1665 Great Plague, people had no knowledge of bacteria or flea vectors and blamed God’s punishment alongside miasma (foul air). Consequently, both eras combined public fasting and prayers ("Lord have mercy upon us") with carrying herbal pomanders and lighting street fires to purify bad air.
KT2 • RENAISSANCE

KEY TOPIC 2: The Medical Renaissance in England, c1500–c1700

Lessons KT2.1–KT2.5 • Printing Press, Vesalius (1543), Harvey (1628), Royal Society (1660), Sydenham (1676) & Great Plague (1665)

c.1440–1500

6. Gutenberg's Printing Press & Renaissance Medical Humanism

KT2.1: Humanism, Printing Press & The Royal Society CommunicationScience & TechnologyAttitudes in Society
Before / Context: Medieval monastic hand-copying was slow, expensive, prone to scribal copying errors, incapable of reproducing uniform anatomical diagrams, and tightly policed by Church authorities.
Breakthrough / Action: Johannes Gutenberg’s moveable-type printing press (c.1440)—reaching Westminster via William Caxton in 1476—merged with Renaissance Humanism and lifelike anatomical art to mass-produce thousands of identical books and precise woodcuts outside Church control.
Impact & Limits: Breaking the Church’s monopoly on scholarship allowed anatomists and physicians across Padua, Paris, and London to compare observations directly against Galen—though high book costs and rural illiteracy meant everyday village remedies barely changed.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Similarity vs KT4: Modern (c1900–Present)): “Explain one way in which communication technology helped medical knowledge spread in the Medical Renaissance (c1500–c1700) similarly to how communication technology spread medical breakthroughs in modern Britain (c1900–present). (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: In both the Renaissance (c1500–1700) and Modern Britain (c1900–present), breakthroughs in communication allowed scientists to verify visual evidence internationally: the printing press spread Vesalius’s 1543 anatomical woodcuts across European universities just as peer-reviewed journals like Nature (1953) and internet databases shared the DNA double helix and Human Genome Project worldwide.
1543

7. Andreas Vesalius Publishes De Humani Corporis Fabrica

KT2.3: Andreas Vesalius & The Anatomical Revolution Individuals & GeniusScience & TechnologyCommunication
Before / Context: Because Roman law banned human dissection, Galen had dissected pigs, dogs, and Barbary apes, creating hundreds of anatomical myths that medieval professors read aloud from lecterns while a lowly demonstrator pointed.
Breakthrough / Action: Secured a steady supply of executed criminals by a Padua judge, Andreas Vesalius performed dissections with his own hands and published De Humani Corporis Fabrica (1543) with ~300 intricate woodcuts, correcting over 300 Galenic errors—proving the human lower jaw is one bone (not two), the liver lacks five lobes, and no invisible pores pierce the heart’s septum.
Impact & Limits: Copied into English via Thomas Geminus’s Compendiosa (1545) for Henry VIII’s barber-surgeons, Vesalius shattered blind faith in Galen and made human dissection central to medical training—yet cured zero diseases in the short term, as accurate anatomy could not explain germs or infection.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Difference vs KT1: Medieval (c1250–1500)): “Explain one way in which attitudes towards human dissection and Galen’s authority in medieval England (c1250–c1500) were different from attitudes during the Medical Renaissance (c1500–c1700). (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: In Medieval universities (c1250–1500), dissections were rare rituals conducted only to illustrate Galen’s words, and any discrepancy was blamed on the corpse. By contrast, in 1543, Andreas Vesalius dissected human bodies himself, used printed anatomical plates as proof, and openly urged doctors to trust direct observation over ancient authority.
1628

8. William Harvey Proves the Circulation of the Blood (De Motu Cordis)

KT2.4: William Harvey & The Circulation of the Blood Individuals & GeniusScience & TechnologyAttitudes in Society
Before / Context: Galen taught that the liver constantly manufactured new blood from digested food to be consumed around the body like firewood—a doctrine that underpinned centuries of bloodletting to remove "excess" blood.
Breakthrough / Action: Physician to James I and Charles I, William Harvey compared the heart to new mechanical fire-engine water pumps, observed slow-beating hearts in cold-blooded reptiles, pressed one-way arm-vein valves, and published De Motu Cordis (1628), proving mathematically that the heart pumps a fixed volume of blood endlessly around a closed one-way circuit.
Impact & Limits: Traditionalists ridiculed Harvey ("crack-brained"), he could not yet see capillaries connecting arteries and veins (observed by Malpighi in 1661), and phlebotomy persisted for another 200 years while 1660s animal-blood transfusions failed—though modern physiology, cardiology, and eventual transfusions all depended on his discovery.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Difference vs KT5: Western Front (1914–1918)): “Explain one way in which the practical surgical use of knowledge about blood in seventeenth-century Britain was different from the use of blood transfusions on the Western Front (1914–1918). (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: Although William Harvey (1628) proved blood circulated, 17th-century transfusions using sheep’s blood killed patients because clotting and blood-group incompatibility were unknown. By contrast, on the Western Front (1917), Landsteiner’s ABO blood groups, citrate-glucose anticoagulant, and Oswald Robertson’s Cambrai blood depot turned blood circulation knowledge into lifesaving shock therapy.
1660 & 1665

9. Charter of the Royal Society & Hooke's Micrographia

KT2.1: Humanism, Printing Press & The Royal Society Institutions: Church & GovtScience & TechnologyCommunication
Before / Context: Before 1660, natural philosophers lacked a permanent secular forum in Britain to witness live experiments, verify instruments, and publish findings in accessible English rather than Latin.
Breakthrough / Action: Founded at Gresham College in 1660 and granted a Royal Charter by King Charles II in 1662 under the motto Nullius in verba ("take nobody’s word for it"), The Royal Society launched the world’s first scientific journal, Philosophical Transactions (1665), and published Robert Hooke’s Micrographia (1665) (followed by Leeuwenhoek’s 1683 "animalcules" letters).
Impact & Limits: Royal endorsement made experimental questioning respectable and collaborative, yet 17th-century microscope lenses suffered chromatic blur (~30–200x magnification), leaving scientists unable to connect microscopic "animalcules" to human disease for two more centuries.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Difference vs KT3: Industrial (c1700–1900)): “Explain one way in which the use of microscopes to investigate living organisms in seventeenth-century Britain was different from Robert Koch’s bacteriological methods in the 1870s and 1880s. (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: In 1665–1683, Robert Hooke and Leeuwenhoek used early microscopes to sketch fleas, cork cells, and tiny "animalcules", but blurry glass prevented anyone linking microbes to specific diseases. By contrast, in 1876–1884, Robert Koch combined 1,000x compound lenses, solid agar plates, and chemical aniline dyes to isolate the exact bacteria causing anthrax, TB, and cholera.
1665

10. Case Study: The Great Plague of London

KT2.5: Continuity & The Great Plague of London (1665) Institutions: Church & GovtAttitudes in Society
Before / Context: When England’s last major bubonic plague epidemic killed nearly 100,000 Londoners (~20% of the city) in 1665, people still blamed miasma, planetary alignments, and sin—smoking tobacco, wearing herb-stuffed plague-doctor beaks, drinking plague water, and strapping live chickens to buboes (transference).
Breakthrough / Action: King Charles II and London’s Lord Mayor & Aldermen issued far better-organised Plague Orders: paying parish Women Searchers to compile printed Bills of Mortality, sealing infected houses for 28–40 days under armed watchmen with a red cross ("Lord have mercy upon us"), opening suburban pest houses, burning tar barrels, banning plays, fairs, and public funerals, and culling 40,000 dogs and 200,000 cats.
Impact & Limits: Enforced quarantine, banned public gatherings, and statistical Bills of Mortality marked real progress in local government public health over 1348, yet slaughtering cats and dogs wiped out natural predators of flea-ridden rats, and only harsh autumn/winter frosts ended the outbreak.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Difference vs KT1: Medieval (c1250–1500)): “Explain one way in which local government actions during the Black Death (1348–49) were different from local government actions during the Great Plague of London (1665). (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: During the 1348–49 Black Death, authorities relied mainly on crowded church prayer processions and basic street-sweeping orders without enforced household isolation. By contrast, during the 1665 Great Plague, London’s Lord Mayor and parish officials banned public assemblies, hired Searchers to track deaths, opened pest houses, and stationed watchmen outside red-crossed doors to enforce 28–40 day household quarantine.
1676

11. Thomas Sydenham's Observationes Medicae ('The English Hippocrates')

KT2.2: Thomas Sydenham & Bedside Observation Individuals & GeniusCommunicationAttitudes in Society
Before / Context: Classical Galenic doctors assumed every fever was unique to the individual patient’s humoral temperament, discouraging physicians from looking for shared disease patterns across patients.
Breakthrough / Action: Known as the ‘English Hippocrates’, London physician Thomas Sydenham published Observationes Medicae (1676), urging doctors to leave ancient textbooks for the patient’s bedside and classify illnesses into distinct "species" just like botanists classify plants—cleanly separating measles from scarlet fever and championing Peruvian cinchona bark (quinine) for malaria and "cool therapy" for smallpox.
Impact & Limits: Observationes Medicae served as Britain’s standard clinical manual for two centuries and created the essential conceptual bridge to modern pathology (if a disease is a distinct species, it has a distinct cause)—even though Sydenham himself distrusted microscopes and still blamed atmospheric "constitutions".
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Difference vs KT1: Medieval (c1250–1500)): “Explain one way in which methods of diagnosing illness in medieval England (c1250–c1500) were different from Thomas Sydenham’s clinical approach in the late seventeenth century. (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: Medieval physicians (c1250–1500) relied on uroscopy charts and astrology to treat each patient’s personal humoral imbalance rather than naming an external disease. By contrast, Thomas Sydenham (1676) insisted on systematic bedside observation of symptoms across many patients to classify objective diseases such as measles and scarlet fever.
KT3 • INDUSTRIAL

KEY TOPIC 3: Medicine in 18th- and 19th-Century Industrial Britain, c1700–c1900

Lessons KT3.1–KT3.5 • Jenner (1796), Chloroform (1847), Snow (1854), Nightingale (1854–59), Germ Theory (1861), Lister (1865), 1875 Health Act & Koch (1876–84)

1796–1853

12. Edward Jenner's Smallpox Vaccine & Compulsory Vaccination

KT3.2: Edward Jenner & The Smallpox Vaccine Individuals & GeniusInstitutions: Church & GovtAttitudes in Society
Before / Context: Smallpox killed tens of thousands in 18th-century Britain; variolation (inoculation) with live smallpox pus (1721) cost high fees, excluded the poor, and frequently killed patients or sparked new outbreaks.
Breakthrough / Action: Testing rural folklore that cowpox protected milkmaids, Gloucestershire surgeon Edward Jenner vaccinated James Phipps with cowpox from Sarah Nelmes on 14 May 1796, proved immunity when smallpox exposure failed six weeks later, self-published 23 cases (1798), and won £30,000 from Parliament (1802/1807)—leading the government to ban inoculation (1840), make infant vaccination compulsory (1853), and appoint Vaccination Officers (1871).
Impact & Limits: Clergy, wealthy inoculators, the Royal Society (which refused to print Jenner’s paper), and the Anti-Vaccine Society fought vaccination fiercely, and because Jenner could not explain Germ Theory, zero further vaccines could be copied from his one-off link until Pasteur in the 1880s—yet smallpox deaths collapsed and the disease was eradicated globally by 1980.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Similarity vs KT4: Modern (c1900–Present)): “Explain one way in which national government action to prevent smallpox in nineteenth-century Britain was similar to government action to prevent lung cancer in modern Britain (c1900–present). (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: In both Industrial (1853–1871) and Modern (2007–2016) Britain, the national government overrode laissez-faire and personal-liberty objections to enforce mass disease prevention: Parliament passed compulsory smallpox vaccination laws (1853/1871) with fines for non-compliance just as modern governments enacted the 2007 indoor smoking ban and 2016 plain packaging to prevent lung cancer.
1842 & 1848

13. Chadwick's Sanitary Report & The Permissive 1848 Public Health Act

KT3.5: Public Health, John Snow & The 1875 Act Individuals & GeniusInstitutions: Church & GovtAttitudes in Society
Before / Context: Industrial cities crammed factory workers into damp courts beside overflowing cesspits while MPs defended laissez-faire non-intervention and middle-class ratepayers ("the Dirty Party") opposed paying for pipes and sewers.
Breakthrough / Action: Poor Law official Edwin Chadwick published his 1842 Sanitary Report, marshalling stark mortality statistics (labourers’ life expectancy averaged 15 in Liverpool vs 38 in rural Rutland) to argue clean water and drainage would reduce Poor Rate taxes; when cholera returned in 1848, Parliament passed the 1848 Public Health Act, establishing a national Central Board of Health.
Impact & Limits: Chadwick still blamed miasma (leading some towns to flush sewage into drinking-water rivers!), and crucially the 1848 Act was permissive, not compulsory: councils only had to act if 10% of ratepayers petitioned or the death rate topped 23 per 1,000, so most ignored it and the Central Board closed in 1854.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Similarity vs KT1: Medieval (c1250–1500)): “Explain one way in which ideas behind cleaning up urban filth during the Black Death (1348–49) were similar to Edwin Chadwick’s arguments for sanitary reform in the 1840s. (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: In both Medieval England (1349) and early Industrial Britain (1842–1848), authorities promoted street cleaning and waste removal because they believed disease spread through poisonous miasma (foul air) rather than germs: King Edward III ordered London’s streets cleared of human waste in 1349 to stop bad smells causing plague, just as Edwin Chadwick (1842) campaigned for drainage and the 1848 Public Health Act because he believed sewer gas caused epidemics.
Nov 1847

14. James Young Simpson Discovers Chloroform Anaesthesia

KT3.4: Simpson, Lister & The Conquest of Pain and Sepsis Individuals & GeniusScience & TechnologyAttitudes in Society
Before / Context: Before anaesthesia, patients endured agonising amputations held down by assistants while nitrous oxide proved too weak and ether (1846) caused violent coughing and risked exploding near operating-room gaslights.
Breakthrough / Action: In November 1847, Edinburgh midwifery professor James Young Simpson and his assistants tested chemical vapours at home and awoke on the floor to discover chloroform as a rapid, non-flammable anaesthetic; John Snow engineered a regulated inhaler (1848), and Queen Victoria’s praise of "that blessed chloroform" during Prince Leopold’s birth (1853) swept aside moral and religious opposition.
Impact & Limits: Dosing remained risky (15-year-old Hannah Greener died of cardiac arrest during a toenail operation in Jan 1848), and worse still, pain-free surgery triggered the ‘Black Period of Surgery’ (1850s–1870s): surgeons attempted slower, deeper invasive operations with unsterilised hands, driving sepsis deaths higher than before until Lister’s antiseptics.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Difference vs KT4: Modern (c1900–Present)): “Explain one way in which pain relief and surgical safety in the mid-nineteenth century were different from modern minimally invasive surgery in Britain (c1900–present). (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: In 1847–1850s, dripping chloroform onto a handkerchief carried sudden overdose risks and encouraged deep open incisions that spread fatal ward gangrene before antisepsis. By contrast, in Modern Britain (c1900–present), electronic anaesthetic delivery pairs with sterile fibre-optic endoscopes and robotic keyhole surgery to operate through tiny ports with minimal pain, bleeding, or infection.
Sept 1854

15. John Snow Maps the Broad Street Cholera Outbreak in Soho

KT3.5: Public Health, John Snow & The 1875 Act Individuals & GeniusScience & TechnologyCommunication
Before / Context: Medical orthodoxy—including Chadwick, Nightingale, and the General Board of Health—remained convinced that cholera spread via airborne miasma, dismissing Snow’s 1849 essay On the Mode of Communication of Cholera.
Breakthrough / Action: When over 500 Soho residents (nearly 700 across the outbreak) died within days in August–September 1854, John Snow mapped every fatality street-by-street, solved the key exceptions (Lion Brewery workers drank beer, workhouse inmates had a separate supply, while widow Susannah Eley died miles away in Hampstead after drinking bottled Broad Street water), persuaded the St James parish vestry to remove the pump handle, and later exposed a leaking cesspit three feet from the well.
Impact & Limits: Removing the handle halted new infections locally and founded modern epidemiology; yet because Snow lacked Germ Theory proof of how water carried cholera (until Koch isolated Vibrio cholerae in 1883–84), the General Board of Health rejected his report and Parliament took action on London’s sewers only after the 1858 Great Stink.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Difference vs KT1: Medieval (c1250–1500)): “Explain one way in which methods of investigating the spread of epidemic disease during the Black Death (1348–49) were different from John Snow’s investigation of cholera in 1854. (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: During the 1348–49 Black Death, observers accepted astrological and miasma theories without empirical testing, never suspecting fleas on rats. By contrast, during the 1854 Soho cholera outbreak, John Snow gathered systematic doorstep statistics and drew a spatial spot map around the Broad Street pump to prove scientifically that contaminated water—not bad air—spread the disease.
1854–1859

16. Florence Nightingale, Mary Seacole & Pavilion Hospital Reform

KT3.3: Florence Nightingale, Mary Seacole & Hospital Care Individuals & GeniusWar & TeamworkInstitutions: Church & Govt
Before / Context: Early 19th-century hospitals were cramped, unwashed, poorly ventilated, and staffed by untrained attendants with a reputation for drunkenness, while typhus, dysentery, and cholera devastated British soldiers in the Crimean War (1854–56).
Breakthrough / Action: Leading 38 nurses to Scutari in 1854, Florence Nightingale scrubbed wards, boiled linen, cleared blocked sewers, and cut ward mortality from ~42% to ~2% (while Mary Seacole self-funded her frontline ‘British Hotel’ near Balaclava after the War Office rejected her); back in Britain, Nightingale published Notes on Nursing (1859) and Notes on Hospitals (1863), promoted airy pavilion-plan hospitals with washable surfaces and isolation wards, and opened the Nightingale School for Nurses at St Thomas’ Hospital (1860) using the £44,000 Nightingale Fund.
Impact & Limits: Even though Nightingale championed fresh air and scrubbing to banish miasma rather than germs (and Mary Seacole’s independent work faded from official memory after 1857), her reforms turned nursing into a skilled, respectable secular profession and transformed British hospital survival by 1900.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Similarity vs KT5: Western Front (1914–1918)): “Explain one way in which wartime medical emergencies changed the role of female nurses and medical volunteers during the Crimean War (1854–56) similarly to the First World War (1914–1918). (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: In both the Crimean War (1854–56) and the First World War (1914–18), military medical emergencies overcame official reluctance toward women serving near the front: Nightingale’s Scutari nurses proved disciplined ward sanitation slashed death rates just as QAIMNS nurses, VADs, and FANY ambulance drivers proved vital across Western Front Casualty Clearing Stations and Base Hospitals.
1861

17. Louis Pasteur Publishes Germ Theory (Swan-Neck Flasks)

KT3.1: Pasteur’s Germ Theory & Koch’s Microbe Hunters Individuals & GeniusScience & TechnologyCommunication
Before / Context: Into the 1860s, doctors who saw bacteria under improved microscopes assumed spontaneous generation—claiming decaying tissue created microbes rather than microbes causing decay—alongside enduring belief in miasma.
Breakthrough / Action: Investigating why French sugar-beet alcohol soured, chemist Louis Pasteur boiled nutrient broth inside swan-neck flasks (which admitted air while trapping dust in the curved neck), publishing Germ Theory in 1861 to prove airborne microbes cause putrefaction—extending it to human infection in 1878 and creating weakened vaccines for chicken cholera (1879), anthrax (1881), and rabies (1885).
Impact & Limits: Germ Theory eventually swept away 1,500 years of humoralism and miasma, yet acceptance in Britain was gradual: Pasteur was a French chemist studying wine/beer rather than patients, and prominent London professor Dr Henry Charlton Bastian led fierce medical opposition defending spontaneous generation into the late 1870s despite John Tyndall and Joseph Lister.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Similarity vs KT2: Renaissance (c1500–1700)): “Explain one way in which reactions to Andreas Vesalius’s anatomical discoveries in the sixteenth century were similar to British medical reactions to Louis Pasteur’s Germ Theory in the 1860s and 1870s. (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: Both Vesalius (1543) and Pasteur (1861) used empirical proof to overturn centuries of orthodoxy yet met strong conservative pushback before treatments improved: Galenic loyalists dismissed Vesalius’s dissections while Dr Henry Charlton Bastian rallied British physicians against Pasteur’s Germ Theory throughout the 1860s–1870s before microbe-specific cures existed.
1865–1890s

18. Joseph Lister's Carbolic Antisepsis & The Shift to Aseptic Surgery

KT3.4: Simpson, Lister & The Conquest of Pain and Sepsis Individuals & GeniusScience & TechnologyCommunication
Before / Context: During surgery’s post-chloroform ‘Black Period’, surgeons took pride in blood-encrusted coats and unwashed scalpels, losing nearly half of amputation patients to septicaemia and hospital gangrene.
Breakthrough / Action: Connecting Pasteur’s 1861 Germ Theory with carbolic acid used on Carlisle sewage, Glasgow surgeon Joseph Lister dressed 11-year-old Jamie Greenlees’s compound leg fracture in carbolic-soaked lint (August 1865), published 11 gangrene-free cases in The Lancet (1867), introduced the carbolic steam spray and carbolic catgut ligatures, and slashed his amputation death rate from ~46% to ~15%.
Impact & Limits: Critics complained carbolic spray cracked nurses’ hands, obscured vision, and slowed surgery—until Koch (1878) isolated wound-sepsis bacteria; by the 1890s, Lister’s antisepsis (killing germs in the wound) evolved into aseptic surgery (preventing germs entering the theatre via steam sterilisers, scrubbed tiled rooms, sterile gowns/masks, and Halsted’s rubber gloves).
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Difference vs KT5: Western Front (1914–1918)): “Explain one way in which methods of preventing surgical wound infection in British hospitals by 1900 were different from surgical methods used on the Western Front (1914–1918). (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: By 1900, civilian British hospitals relied on aseptic steam-sterilised theatres to keep germs out of clean surgical incisions from the start. However, on the Western Front (1914–18), shrapnel blasted manure-fertilised Flanders soil and gas gangrene spores deep into wounds before evacuation, forcing surgeons to cut away infected flesh (debridement) and irrigate continuously with Carrel-Dakin solution.
1858–1875

19. The Great Stink, Bazalgette's Sewers & The Compulsory 1875 Public Health Act

KT3.5: Public Health, John Snow & The 1875 Act Institutions: Church & GovtIndividuals & GeniusScience & Technology
Before / Context: Raw sewage pouring into the tidal Thames produced the stifling ‘Great Stink’ of summer 1858, driving MPs from riverside committee rooms at Westminster despite lime-soaked curtains.
Breakthrough / Action: Parliament voted £3 million in 18 days for Chief Engineer Sir Joseph Bazalgette to build 83 miles of main intercepting sewers, ~1,300 miles of brick street sewers, Thames embankments, and steam pumping stations (1858–1875); then—after working-class men won the vote (1867) and Germ Theory proved dirty water carried bacteria—Disraeli’s government passed the compulsory 1875 Public Health Act, forcing every council to provide clean water, covered sewers, street scavenging, food checks, and a Medical Officer of Health.
Impact & Limits: Once East London joined Bazalgette’s system after 1866, cholera never returned to the capital, and the 1875 Act permanently ended laissez-faire in sanitation—though personal doctors and hospital care still cost money that poor families struggled to afford until 1911 and 1948.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Difference vs KT4: Modern (c1900–Present)): “Explain one way in which government responsibility for public health under the 1875 Public Health Act was different from state healthcare provision under the National Health Service from 1948. (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: The 1875 Public Health Act compelled local councils to clean up the external environment (sewers, clean water, and waste collection), but sick citizens still had to pay private doctors or seek charity for treatment. By contrast, Aneurin Bevan’s NHS (1948) provided comprehensive personal healthcare—hospitals, GPs, surgery, maternity care, and prescriptions—funded by national taxation and free at the point of delivery.
1876–1884

20. Robert Koch Identifies Specific Disease Microbes (Anthrax, TB & Cholera)

KT3.1: Pasteur’s Germ Theory & Koch’s Microbe Hunters Individuals & GeniusScience & TechnologyWar & Teamwork
Before / Context: Pasteur’s liquid broths swarmed with mixed micro-organisms, making it nearly impossible to isolate a single pure microbe and prove beyond doubt which germ caused a specific human disease.
Breakthrough / Action: Backed by German imperial funding amid Franco-Prussian rivalry (1870–71), Robert Koch developed solid agar-jelly culture plates to grow pure bacterial colonies, stained transparent microbes with chemical aniline dyes, and photographed them through 1,000x microscopes—identifying the specific bacteria causing anthrax (1876), septicaemia (1878), tuberculosis (1882), and cholera (1883–84).
Impact & Limits: Koch’s methods silenced Britain’s remaining Germ Theory sceptics, validated John Snow’s 1854 water hypothesis, and enabled vaccines for diphtheria, tetanus, and typhoid—yet identifying a bacterium still could not cure an infected patient until 20th-century magic bullets (1909) and antibiotics (1941).
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Difference vs KT4: Modern (c1900–Present)): “Explain one way in which late nineteenth-century discoveries about the causes of disease were different from late twentieth-century discoveries about genetics and DNA. (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: In the 1870s–1880s, Robert Koch proved how external bacterial microbes cause infectious diseases such as tuberculosis and cholera, but bacteriology could not explain inherited conditions. By contrast, in Modern Britain (1953–2003), Franklin, Watson, and Crick’s DNA discovery and the Human Genome Project explained internal genetic mutations behind hereditary illnesses such as cystic fibrosis, sickle-cell anaemia, and BRCA breast cancer.

1909 & 1932

21. Chemical 'Magic Bullets': Ehrlich & Hata's Salvarsan 606 & Domagk's Prontosil

KT4.3: Magic Bullets, High-Tech Treatments & The NHS Individuals & GeniusScience & Technology
Before / Context: Up to 1909, even though vaccines prevented several diseases, doctors possessed no internal chemical drug able to seek out and destroy bacteria inside a patient’s bloodstream without killing human tissue.
Breakthrough / Action: Inspired by Koch’s selective chemical dyes and natural antibodies, Paul Ehrlich’s team synthesised arsenic compounds until Sahachiro Hata (1909) re-tested the 606th—Salvarsan 606—proving it killed the syphilis microbe; in 1932, Gerhard Domagk found the red dye Prontosil cured Streptococcus blood poisoning (saving his own daughter Hildegard), unlocking sulphonamide drugs like M&B 693 (1938) for pneumonia, meningitis, and puerperal fever.
Impact & Limits: Sulphonamides slashed British maternal deaths from childbed fever from ~20% to under 5%, yet Salvarsan 606 was arsenic-based, painful, occasionally fatal if mis-oxidised, and worked only on syphilis, while sulphonamides risked kidney damage and failed against Staphylococcus wound bacteria.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Similarity vs KT3: Industrial Bacteriology (1876–1884)): “Explain one way in which the role of chemical dyes and laboratory science in Robert Koch’s work (1876–84) was similar to the development of chemical ‘magic bullets’ in the early twentieth century (1909–32). (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: Both late 19th-century microbe hunting and early 20th-century drug cures depended directly on the chemical dye industry and methodical laboratory teamwork: Robert Koch used synthetic aniline dyes to stain specific bacteria on slides (1876–84), directly inspiring his student Paul Ehrlich (1909) and Gerhard Domagk (1932, Prontosil red dye) to turn selective chemical stains into internal germ-killing "magic bullets".
1928–1944

26. Case Study 1: Fleming, Florey & Chain and Allied Mass Production of Penicillin

KT4.4: Case Study 1: Fleming, Florey & Chain and Penicillin Individuals & GeniusScience & TechnologyWar & TeamworkInstitutions: Church & Govt
Before / Context: When Alexander Fleming spotted Penicillium notatum mould lysing Staphylococcus colonies on an unwashed St Mary’s dish in September 1928, he lacked biochemists or funds to stabilise pure penicillin, publishing in 1929 before abandoning clinical development.
Breakthrough / Action: At Oxford (1938–41), Howard Florey, Ernst Chain, and Norman Heatley purified penicillin (curing four infected mice in May 1940 and rallying policeman Albert Alexander in Feb 1941 until supplies ran dry); with wartime Britain under Blitz bombardment, Florey flew to the USA (July 1941), where US government grants and 21 chemical/drug companies (including Pfizer) used corn-steep liquor and deep-tank aerated vats to produce 2.3 million doses for D-Day (June 1944).
Impact & Limits: The first true antibiotic cured septicaemia, pneumonia, scarlet fever, and syphilis (inspiring streptomycin for TB in 1944), saving an estimated 12–15% of wounded Allied soldiers from fatal infection and amputation; however, antibiotics are useless against viruses, and over-prescription has bred antibiotic-resistant superbugs such as MRSA.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Similarity vs KT3: Industrial (c1700–1900)): “Explain one way in which government involvement in developing Edward Jenner’s smallpox vaccine in the nineteenth century was similar to government involvement in mass-producing penicillin in the 1940s. (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: Both smallpox vaccination (1796–1853) and penicillin (1928–1944) needed decisive government backing to turn a pioneer’s discovery into nationwide protection: Parliament awarded Jenner £30,000 and funded free public vaccination (1840–53), just as US and British wartime governments funded industrial deep-tank fermentation (1941–44) and distributed penicillin free through the NHS (1948).
5 July 1948

27. Aneurin Bevan Launches the National Health Service (NHS)

KT4.3: Magic Bullets, High-Tech Treatments & The NHS Institutions: Church & GovtIndividuals & GeniusAttitudes in SocietyWar & Teamwork
Before / Context: Lloyd George’s 1911 National Insurance Act covered only wage-earning workers—leaving over half of Britain (wives, children, the elderly, and the unemployed) forced to pay GP bills, join clubs, or avoid medical care altogether.
Breakthrough / Action: Driven by wartime solidarity, the WWII Emergency Medical Service, and William Beveridge’s 1942 Report, Labour Health Minister Aneurin Bevan overcame intense British Medical Association (BMA) opposition (allowing hospital consultants private fee-paying beds and GPs capitation fees) to launch the tripartite NHS on 5 July 1948—uniting 2,688+ nationalised hospitals, GPs/dentists/opticians, and local health services free at the point of delivery.
Impact & Limits: Universal access to GPs, maternity care, antibiotics, and national vaccination drives (diphtheria, polio, MMR, HPV) lifted UK life expectancy from ~66 (1948) to ~81+ today and halved maternal mortality within a decade; however, huge pent-up demand forced prescription/dental charges (1951–52), while ageing Victorian hospital stock awaited the 1962 Hospital Plan.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Difference vs KT1: Medieval (c1250–1500)): “Explain one way in which access to hospital care and trained doctors in medieval England (c1250–c1500) was different from healthcare access in Britain after the creation of the NHS in 1948. (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: In Medieval England (c1250–1500), hospitals were small Church-run charities offering rest and prayer while excluding infectious or pregnant people, and trained physicians served only the wealthy. By contrast, from 5 July 1948, the state-run NHS guaranteed every citizen universal, tax-funded access to specialist hospitals, doctors, maternity wards, and surgery free at the point of delivery.
1952–1953

28. Franklin's Photo 51 & Watson and Crick's Double-Helix Structure of DNA

KT4.1: Genetics, DNA & The Human Genome Project Science & TechnologyIndividuals & GeniusCommunication
Before / Context: While Germ Theory explained infectious diseases caused by invading pathogens, mid-20th-century medicine still could not explain how congenital and hereditary disorders passed from parents to children inside human cells.
Breakthrough / Action: Using X-ray crystallography at King’s College London (with Maurice Wilkins and Raymond Gosling), Rosalind Franklin captured the X-shaped diffraction pattern ‘Photograph 51’ (May 1952); combining her X-ray data with 3D molecular models at Cambridge, James Watson and Francis Crick published the double-helix structure of DNA in Nature (April 1953), unlocking how genetic codes copy—and occasionally mutate—across cells.
Impact & Limits: DNA science explained hereditary conditions like Down’s syndrome (1959), cystic fibrosis, sickle-cell anaemia, and Huntington’s disease, enabling genetic counselling and BRCA1/BRCA2 preventative breast-cancer screening; yet understanding a faulty gene still has not produced routine cures for most genetic disorders, as gene therapy remains experimental and expensive.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Similarity vs KT2: Renaissance (c1500–1700)): “Explain one way in which the short-term impact on everyday treatments of William Harvey’s discovery of blood circulation (1628) was similar to the impact of discovering the structure of DNA (1953). (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: Both William Harvey’s circulation of the blood (1628) and the discovery of DNA’s structure (1953) revolutionised medical understanding of how the body works decades before yielding direct treatments: Harvey could not perform safe blood transfusions until 1901–17, just as identifying faulty genes enabled screening (BRCA1/2) while leaving hereditary conditions like Huntington’s or Down’s syndrome without a genetic cure.
1972–2003

29. High-Tech Diagnostics (CT/MRI), Robotic Keyhole Surgery & The Human Genome Project

KT4.2: Lifestyle Factors & High-Tech Diagnostics Science & TechnologyInstitutions: Church & GovtCommunication
Before / Context: Well into the 1960s, flat 2D X-rays missed soft-tissue tumours, forcing surgeons to cut open cavities for exploratory diagnosis and subjecting patients to heavy trauma and long hospital stays.
Breakthrough / Action: Microprocessors, lasers, and global collaboration transformed diagnosis and treatment: Godfrey Hounsfield’s CT scanner (1972), MRI soft-tissue magnets, ultrasound, automated blood labs, and fibre-optic endoscopes enabled keyhole (laparoscopic) and robotic surgery, heart bypasses, kidney dialysis, prosthetic hip replacements, and organ transplants—while 18 countries completed the Human Genome Project (1990–2003) mapping all ~3 billion human DNA base pairs.
Impact & Limits: High-tech scans catch cancers and strokes early while keyhole incisions slash infection risk and recovery times to days; however, multimillion-pound scanners, transplant immunosuppressants, and an ageing population stretch NHS finances and specialist waiting lists.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Difference vs KT1: Medieval (c1250–1500)): “Explain one way in which methods of medical diagnosis in medieval England (c1250–c1500) were different from high-tech diagnostic methods in modern Britain (c1900–present). (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: Medieval physicians (c1250–1500) diagnosed illness by examining urine colour/taste on a uroscopy wheel and consulting astrological charts to guess humoral balance. By contrast, in Modern Britain (1972–present), doctors combine laboratory blood pathology, 3D CT and MRI scans, ultrasound, and fibre-optic endoscopes to inspect internal organs and cells directly before operating.
1950–2016

30. Case Study 2: Fighting Lung Cancer — Diagnosis, Immunotherapy & Anti-Smoking Laws

KT4.5: Case Study 2: Public Health & Lung Cancer Institutions: Church & GovtScience & TechnologyAttitudes in Society
Before / Context: Mass cigarette smoking (normalised by free WWI/WWII army rations and glamour advertising) made lung cancer the UK’s biggest cancer killer (~35,000+ deaths/year); even after Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill (1950) proved the tobacco link, vast tax revenues and lobbying slowed state intervention while standard X-rays detected tumours too late.
Breakthrough / Action: Modern NHS care integrates low-dose CT/PET-CT scans and bronchoscopy biopsy with keyhole lung resection, targeted radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and genomic immunotherapy—matched by escalating government prevention: banning TV cigarette adverts (1965) and all tobacco promotion/F1 sponsorship (2002–05), outlawing smoking in indoor workplaces and pubs (July 2007), raising purchase age from 16 to 18 (2007), banning smoking in cars with children (2015), mandating plain packaging (2016), and funding NHS Stop-Smoking services.
Impact & Limits: Adult UK smoking rates fell from ~45% (1974) to under 13%, saving hundreds of thousands of lives; yet because early lung tumours rarely cause pain or symptoms, roughly two-thirds of patients are still diagnosed at late Stage 3 or 4, limiting five-year survival rates despite targeted mobile CT lung-check trials.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Difference vs KT3: Industrial (c1700–1900)): “Explain one way in which government public health measures in late nineteenth-century Britain were different from government campaigns to prevent lung cancer in modern Britain (c1950–present). (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: In 1875, Victorian public health laws targeted infectious waterborne diseases like cholera and typhoid by compelling local councils to build sewers and clean water supplies. By contrast, in Modern Britain (c1950–present), government prevention increasingly targets non-infectious lifestyle illnesses like lung cancer through tobacco taxation, the 2007 indoor smoking ban, plain packaging (2016), and NHS education campaigns.

22. Western Front Terrain, Trench Illnesses & Gas Warfare (Ypres, Somme & Arras)

KT5.1: British Sector Terrain, Trench Illnesses & Gas War & TeamworkScience & Technology
Before / Context: Trench deadlock from Flanders (Ypres/Passchendaele) to northern France (The Somme, Arras, Cambrai) exposed troops to flooded clay soil heavily fertilised with manure carrying anaerobic Clostridium perfringens (gas gangrene) spores.
Breakthrough / Action: Combating trench foot (20,000 cases in winter 1914–15), lice-borne trench fever (linked to body lice in 1918), NYDN ("shell shock"), high-explosive shrapnel, and chlorine (Apr 1915), phosgene (Dec 1915), and blistering mustard gas (July 1917), the British Army enforced whale-oil foot rubbing and dry socks, steam delousing, Brodie steel helmets (1915, cutting fatal head wounds ~80%), Small Box Respirators (1916), and excavated an 800-bed electrified underground hospital beneath Arras (1917).
Impact & Limits: Preventative military medicine kept gas deaths under 6,000 among British forces and curbed gangrenous trench-foot amputations, yet waist-deep mud at Third Ypres / Passchendaele (1917) still needed 6–8 stretcher-bearers per stretcher and fatally delayed surgical evacuation.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Similarity vs KT3: Industrial (c1700–1900)): “Explain one way in which preventative hygiene measures used to protect soldiers’ health in the Crimean War (1854–56) were similar to preventative measures used on the Western Front (1914–1918). (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: In both the Crimean War (1854–56) and the Western Front (1914–18), non-combat environmental illnesses threatened military collapse until strict preventative sanitation intervened: Nightingale scrubbed wards and boiled louse-ridden sheets at Scutari just as the RAMC instituted whale-oil foot inspections, water chlorination, and steam delousing stations.

23. The RAMC & FANY Chain of Evacuation (RAP → ADS → CCS → Base Hospital)

KT5.4: The Chain of Evacuation (RAP to Base Hospital) War & TeamworkInstitutions: Church & Govt
Before / Context: In August 1914, the British Army deployed to France with zero motor ambulances, relying on horse-drawn wagons that shook fractured bones over cratered roads while major surgery waited far back at coastal Base Hospitals.
Breakthrough / Action: Growing from 9,000 to 113,000+ RAMC personnel alongside QAIMNS, VADs, and FANY volunteer drivers/mechanics like Pat Beauchamp (funded initially by The Times appeal for 512 motor ambulances), Britain built the staged Chain of Evacuation: Stretcher-Bearers → RAP (200m behind front, first-aid bandages/morphine) → Dressing Stations (ADS/MDS) → Casualty Clearing Stations (CCS) (7–10 miles back beside railway lines, sorting patients by triage for urgent life-saving surgery) → Ambulance Trains / Smooth Canal Barges → Base Hospitals (specialist neuro, gas, or orthopaedic divisions).
Impact & Limits: Shifting emergency surgery forward from coastal Base Hospitals into Casualty Clearing Stations stopped gas gangrene and haemorrhage within the golden first 12 hours, though massive offensives (57,470 casualties on 1 July 1916 at the Somme) still overwhelmed ADS tents planned for 150–400 men.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Difference vs KT1: Medieval (c1250–1500)): “Explain one way in which the organisation and admission of patients in medieval hospitals (c1250–c1500) was different from the RAMC Chain of Evacuation on the Western Front (1914–1918). (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: Medieval monastic hospitals (c1250–1500) admitted travellers and the elderly into a single open hall for prayer and rest while turning away severely wounded or contagious people. By contrast, on the Western Front (1914–18), the RAMC Chain of Evacuation used clinical triage at Casualty Clearing Stations to rush critically injured soldiers straight into sterile operating theatres before specialist recovery at Base Hospitals.
1915–1916

24. Wound Excision, Carrel-Dakin Solution, Mobile X-Rays & The Thomas Splint

KT5.5: Thomas Splint, Debridement & Mobile X-Rays War & TeamworkScience & TechnologyIndividuals & Genius
Before / Context: In 1914–15, 80% of soldiers suffering a compound femur fracture died from blood loss and shock as jagged bone ends severed blood vessels during stretcher transport, while stitching dirty shrapnel wounds trapped fatal gas gangrene inside.
Breakthrough / Action: From December 1915, Sir Robert Jones trained frontline stretcher-bearers to fit Hugh Owen Thomas’s traction splint (slashing femur mortality from 80% to under 20%); CCS surgeons practised wound excision (debridement) (cutting away all dead flesh and uniform scraps before delayed primary suture), flushed deep cavities via perforated tubes with Carrel-Dakin sodium hypochlorite solution, and pinpointed metal shrapnel using Roentgen X-rays housed inside mobile X-ray vans (pioneered by Marie Curie).
Impact & Limits: Pre-operative X-rays, traction splints, and Carrel-Dakin debridement prevented thousands of amputations before antibiotics existed; yet glass X-ray tubes overheated after ~60 minutes, exposed crews to radiation, missed buried uniform cloth, and Carrel-Dakin solution had to be mixed fresh on site because it expired within six hours.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Difference vs KT3: Industrial (c1700–1900)): “Explain one way in which surgical methods for dealing with deep infected wounds in the 1870s were different from surgical techniques developed on the Western Front (1915–1918). (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: 19th-century surgeons like Joseph Lister had to probe bullet wounds manually and used harsh carbolic acid that burned deep internal tissue and failed against anaerobic gas gangrene spores. By contrast, on the Western Front (1915–18), surgeons used mobile X-ray vans to locate shrapnel before cutting and irrigated excised deep wounds continuously with non-corrosive Carrel-Dakin solution.

25. Cambrai Blood Bank, Cushing's Neurosurgery & Gillies's Plastic Surgery

KT5.6: Blood Storage, Brain Surgery & Plastic Reconstruction War & TeamworkScience & TechnologyIndividuals & Genius
Before / Context: Even after Karl Landsteiner identified ABO blood groups (1901), blood clotted minutes after leaving a vein, restricting doctors to cumbersome donor-to-patient (Kimpton-Brown or syringe-cannula) transfusions unsuited to frontline surges.
Breakthrough / Action: Combining Lewisohn’s sodium citrate (1915), Weil’s refrigeration, and Rous & Turner’s citrate-glucose solution (1916, preserving blood up to 4 weeks), US RAMC-attached Captain Oswald Hope Robertson stockpiled bottled Type O universal-donor blood in ice-packed ammunition boxes for the Battle of Cambrai (Nov 1917) (saving 11 of 20 severely shocked casualties)—alongside Dr Harvey Cushing’s electromagnet brain surgery under local anaesthetic (halving head-wound deaths from ~65% to ~29%) and Harold Gillies’s infection-free tube-pedicle skin grafts across 11,000+ facial operations at Queen’s Hospital, Sidcup (1917).
Impact & Limits: Separating donor from recipient in space and time created the modern Blood Bank, transforming civilian trauma care and complex surgery after 1918 while founding modern neurosurgery and reconstructive plastic surgery.
Paper 1 Q3 [4m] (Difference vs KT3: Industrial (c1700–1900)): “Explain one way in which surgeons’ ability to overcome catastrophic blood loss during nineteenth-century operations was different from trauma surgery at the Battle of Cambrai in 1917. (4 marks)”
Model Comparison: In Industrial Britain (c1847–1900), even after Simpson’s chloroform conquered pain and Lister reduced infection, surgeons still had no safe way to store or transfuse compatible human blood, meaning patients frequently died of haemorrhagic shock during major operations. By contrast, at Cambrai in November 1917, Captain Oswald Hope Robertson combined Landsteiner’s ABO blood groups with citrate-glucose anticoagulant to pre-store bottled Type O blood on ice, successfully resuscitating shocked casualties before surgery.
