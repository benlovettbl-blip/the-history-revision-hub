LESSON 1 • Evidence, Change and Social Rank in Roman Britain

Roman Law, Twelve Tables and Bath Curse Tablets (AD 43–410)

Key Enquiry: How did the Romans catch thieves and judge crimes without police?
[1.1] AD 43–200: Written Twelve Tables and Basilica Magistrates
“How did Roman magistrates judge crimes inside town basilicas after AD 43?”

After AD 43, Roman governors brought Britain’s first written laws, shaped by Rome’s ancient Twelve Tables. In towns like Londinium, officials displayed clear rules on wooden and bronze boards so traders knew what was legal. Written rules stopped local chiefs making up unfair laws on the spot.

Serious crimes were judged inside a grand stone hall called a basilica [buh-SIL-ih-kuh] beside the market forum. There a senior judge called a magistrate [MAJ-ih-strayt] weighed witness evidence, checked bronze market scales, and ordered lawful fines or sentences.

[1.2] AD 43–300: No Police Force and Unequal Roman Ranks
“Why did Roman courts punish citizens and enslaved people so differently?”

Surprisingly, Roman Britain had no police force to investigate everyday thefts! Roman legionaries [lee-juh-NAIR-eez] guarded forts and roads, while town vigiles [VIJ-ih-leez] patrolled dark streets with water buckets to spot fires. Ordinary victims had to chase and catch suspects themselves!

Roman courts also punished people unequally by social rank. Wealthy Roman citizens usually paid money fines or faced exile abroad, whereas enslaved people had no rights and suffered harsh whipping, hard labour in dark mines, or death in the amphitheatre.

[1.3] c. AD 100–400: Stolen Cloaks and Lead Curse Tablets at Bath
“Why did robbed bathers at Bath drop lead curses into the spring?”

What could a bather do if nobody saw who snatched their warm wool cloak? At Aquae Sulis [AK-way SOO-liss], now Bath, busy bathers left tunics, rings, and coins on stone benches beside steaming green pools.

Furious victims bought thin sheets of soft grey lead called curse tablets and scratched Latin pleas with a sharp iron pen. Folding each tablet tightly, they dropped it into the hot spring, begging goddess Sulis Minerva to punish the thief until their stolen goods came back!

LESSON 2 • Community Justice, Compensation and Belief

Anglo-Saxon Tithings, Wergild and Trial by Ordeal (AD 410–1066)

Key Enquiry: Were Anglo-Saxon laws fair community rules or painful superstitions?
[2.1] AD 410–900: Ten-Person Tithings and the Hue and Cry
“How did tithings and the Hue and Cry catch thieves without police?”

After Roman rule ended around AD 410, Anglo-Saxon families farmed around small timber villages with no prisons and no police. Instead, every male villager aged twelve and over joined a group of ten neighbours called a tithing [TY-thing].

If one member broke the King’s Peace, the other nine men had to bring him to court or pay a fine! Anyone spotting a crime blew a horn and shouted the Hue and Cry, forcing every villager in earshot to chase the thief.

[2.2] c. AD 600–900: King Alfred’s Laws and Silver Wergild
“Why did King Alfred fix silver wergild fines for injuries?”

Early on, an injury could spark a deadly blood feud as rival families took revenge. To keep peace, rulers from King Aethelberht to King Alfred the Great wrote Old English law codes fixing silver compensation called wergild [WER-gild], meaning "person-price".

Every body part and social rank carried an exact silver value! Yet wergild was unequal: a noble thane was worth 1,200 shillings, six times a free ceorl [CHURL] farmer at 200 shillings. Repeat traitors or coin-forgers still faced hanging or losing a hand.

[2.3] AD 600–1066: Hundred Courts and Trial by Ordeal
“When did Anglo-Saxon courts use Trial by Ordeal inside a church?”

Each month, Anglo-Saxon elders met outdoors at the Hundred Court, where respected neighbours called oath-helpers swore before God that a suspect spoke truthfully. Only when witnesses still could not tell who lied did priests hold Trial by Ordeal inside a stone church.

In Trial by Hot Iron, the suspect carried a glowing iron bar three paces and had their burned hand bandaged for three days. A clean healing burn meant God declared them innocent, while a festering wound—or floating in blessed cold water—meant guilty!

LESSON 3 • Continuity and Change Across 1066

Norman Forest Laws, Sanctuary and Royal Courts (1066–1485)

Key Enquiry: How did Norman kings and medieval royal judges change English justice after 1066?
[3.1] 1066–1150: Norman Forest Laws and Royal Fines
“Why did villagers resent William the Conqueror’s Forest Laws after 1066?”

After 1066, William the Conqueror kept Anglo-Saxon tithings and the Hue and Cry, because they caught village thieves cheaply! However, Normans ended wergild to families—fines now went straight into the King’s treasury, and nobles could demand Trial by Combat.

Villagers hated William’s Forest Laws across places like the New Forest. Hunting a wild deer or cutting firewood became poaching [POH-ching], punished by blinding or heavy fines! Because neighbours sympathised with hungry hunters, Robin Hood ballads cheered greenwood outlaws.

[3.2] 1154–1215: Henry II’s Juries and 1215 Magna Carta
“How did Henry II and 1215 change how trials were decided?”

From 1154, King Henry II made English courts much fairer by sending travelling royal judges around counties to enforce one shared Common Law. Twelve local neighbours sat as a jury [JOOR-ee] to weigh evidence inside royal courts like the King’s Bench.

Two giant changes arrived in 1215. At Runnymede, King John sealed Magna Carta, promising no free person could be jailed without lawful judgment by peers. That same year, Pope Innocent III banned priests from Trial by Ordeal—making twelve-person juries England’s everyday standard!

[3.3] 1100–1485: Cathedral Sanctuary, Stocks and Pillories
“How did Church sanctuary and wooden stocks work in medieval towns?”

The medieval Church also held huge legal power. A fugitive who struck the bronze Sanctuary Knocker on Durham Cathedral won 37 to 40 days of safe sanctuary [SANK-choo-er-ee]. That paused the sheriff until the fugitive chose royal trial or lifelong exile abroad!

Meanwhile, towns shamed petty cheats in public on market day. Drunkards sat with ankles locked in low wooden stocks, while bakers selling underweight bread stood upright with head and hands locked in the pillory [PIL-uh-ree]!

LESSON 4 • Causation, Fear and Changing Attitudes

Tudor Vagrants, High Treason and Witchcraft Trials (1485–1700)

Key Enquiry: Why did Tudor and Stuart rulers fear vagrants, plotters, and accused witches so fiercely?
[4.1] 1485–1603: Tudor Vagrants and the 1601 Poor Law
“How did Elizabeth I’s 1601 Poor Law treat different poor people?”

During Tudor times, rising food prices, bad harvests, and fenced sheep pastures left thousands of farmworkers jobless and hungry. With only unpaid parish constables and night watchmen to keep order, towns feared wandering strangers called vagrants [VAY-gruntz] or vagabonds.

Queen Elizabeth I’s 1601 Poor Law split poor people into two clear groups! Elderly or sick locals received helpful parish poor relief, whereas healthy "sturdy beggars" were locked in wooden stocks, whipped behind a cart, or sent to Bridewell workhouses.

[4.2] 1605: High Treason and the Gunpowder Plot
“How did guards foil Guy Fawkes’s 1605 Gunpowder Plot?”

Fierce religious divisions made rulers dread High Treason—plotting to kill the monarch or destroy Parliament. In 1605, Robert Catesby and explosives soldier Guy Fawkes hid 36 barrels of gunpowder beneath stacked firewood under the House of Lords to blow up King James I!

Tipped off by the anonymous Monteagle Letter, royal guards searched the dark cellar before midnight on 4–5 November 1605, catching Guy Fawkes holding his shuttered iron dark lantern! Rowed through Traitors’ Gate, the plotters faced terrifying public deterrence.

[4.3] 1645–1736: Witchcraft Panics and Scientific Proof
“Why did witch trials peak in 1645 and end after 1660?”

Towns wheeled wooden ducking stools to rivers for scolding neighbours, yet superstition turned deadly during the 1645–1647 Civil War. In East Anglia, self-named "Witchfinder General" Matthew Hopkins charged frightened towns fees, keeping poor older women sleepless for nights until they confessed!

Why did witch trials finally vanish? After 1660, peace returned, the Royal Society promoted science, and calm royal judges banned unfair "swimming" tests and demanded real courtroom proof—until Parliament’s 1736 Witchcraft Act ended witch trials for good!

LESSON 5 • Cause, Consequence and Unintended Outcomes

Smugglers, Highwaymen and The Bloody Code (1700–1820)

Key Enquiry: Why did Britain pass over 200 death-penalty laws—and why did juries stop using them?
[5.1] 1700–1800: Coastal Tea Smugglers and Highwaymen
“Why did smuggling and highway robbery grow across Britain in the 1700s?”

In the 1700s across Britain, heavy government import taxes made legal tea, silk, and brandy expensive. Along Cornwall and Sussex coves, midnight smuggling [SMUG-ling] gangs rowed untaxed barrels ashore in secret—another "social crime" cheered by whole villages eager to buy cheap tea!

Inland, dark unlit turnpike roads, cash-carrying stagecoaches, and pocket flintlock pistols helped mounted highwaymen like Dick Turpin (hanged at York in 1739) shout "Stand and deliver!" Highwaymen vanished once paper banknotes, fast armed Royal Mail coaches, and horse patrols spread.

[5.2] 1688–1820: 220 Capital Laws and Reluctant Juries
“Why did twelve-person juries undermine the harsh Bloody Code?”

Wealthy Members of Parliament still refused to pay taxes for a national police force. Instead, landowners passed the harsh "Bloody Code": by 1810, over 220 crimes—including stealing five shillings from a shop, poaching rabbits, or cutting down young trees—carried the death penalty!

Yet terrifying gallows deterrence backfired completely! Ordinary twelve-person juries hated hanging hungry teenagers for tiny thefts, so kind jurors deliberately valued stolen goods under five shillings or acquitted suspects—proving Britain needed fairer laws.

[5.3] 1787–1868: Hulks, Australia and Elizabeth Fry
“How did transportation and Elizabeth Fry reshape British punishment?”

Instead of hanging every thief, judges locked prisoners aboard damp Thames prison hulks before shipping over 160,000 men, women, and teenagers via transportation to Australia (1787–1868). After an eight-month sea voyage, convicts laboured in chains building colonial roads and farms.

Back in London’s filthy Newgate Prison, Quaker reformer Elizabeth Fry visited freezing women and children in 1813. Opening a bright prison school and paid sewing rooms in 1817, Fry proved humane rehabilitation [ree-huh-bil-ih-TAY-shun] beat cruelty—inspiring the 1823 Gaols Act!

LESSON 6 • Synoptic Change, Prevention and Science

Peel’s 1829 Bobbies, Victorian Prisons and Forensics (1829–Present)

Key Enquiry: How have uniformed police, prison reform, and forensic science transformed justice since 1829?
[6.1] 1829–1856: Sir Robert Peel’s Unarmed "Bobbies"
“How did Sir Robert Peel’s 1829 police win public trust in London?”

Fast-growing London outgrew elderly night watchmen! After slashing over 180 hanging laws from the Bloody Code, Home Secretary Sir Robert Peel passed the 1829 Metropolitan Police Act. He sent 1,000 paid "Peelers" or "Bobbies" onto the streets from Scotland Yard.

Crucially, Peel dressed his officers in dark blue coats and tall top hats—never red army tunics! Carrying only a wooden truncheon and loud wooden rattle rather than guns, Bobbies walked regular foot beats to prevent crime peacefully through policing by consent.

[6.2] 1842–1965: Victorian Prisons to Abolishing Hanging
“How did British punishments change between 1842 Pentonville and 1965?”

As convict transportation ended (1868), purpose-built prisons became Britain’s main punishment. Opened in 1842, Pentonville Prison enforced the silent Separate System inside solitary cells alongside exhausting hard labour climbing wooden treadwheels or turning heavy cell cranks!

Gradually, Victorian harshness gave way to humane reform. Britain ended public street hanging in 1868 and abolished the death penalty for murder in 1965, while modern courts focus on rehabilitation, youth courts, probation, and unpaid community payback.

[6.3] 1901–Present: Fingerprints, 1984 DNA and Modern Policing
“How did 1901 fingerprints and 1984 DNA transform modern trials?”

Modern science transformed how detectives prove guilt fairly! In 1901, Scotland Yard opened its Fingerprint Bureau, matching unique fingertip whorls left at crime scenes. In 1937, 999 emergency phone calls linked citizens to fast radio patrol cars, later joined by town CCTV.

Then in 1984, Professor Sir Alec Jeffreys at Leicester University discovered genetic DNA profiling—identifying guilty offenders AND clearing innocent suspects from a single hair or drop of saliva! Today, forensics and digital cyber-crime teams work alongside neighbourhood officers.

KEY STAGE 2 ILLUSTRATED GLOSSARY SYLLABLE HELPERS & DEFINITIONS

Historian's Word Bank (24 Essential Terms)

Clap the italic syllables out loud together to master new historical words with confidence!

Twelve Tables Twelve Ta-bles
Rome’s famous ancient code of written laws, establishing the rule that laws should be written down clearly for citizens.
Magistrate Mag-is-trate
An official Roman judge who sat inside a town’s stone basilica hall to hear witnesses and decide court punishments.
Vigiles Vig-i-les
Roman town night-watchmen who patrolled streets with buckets to spot dangerous fires and stop noisy night brawls.
Aquae Sulis A-quae Su-lis
The Roman spa town of Bath in Somerset, famous for steaming mineral baths sacred to the goddess Sulis Minerva.
Tithing Ti-thing
A group of ten Anglo-Saxon neighbours aged twelve and over who promised to keep one another honest or bring lawbreakers to court.
Hue and Cry Hue and Cry
A loud alarm shouted or blown on a horn forcing every villager in earshot to drop their tools and chase a thief at once.
Wergild Wer-gild
Old English for "person-price"—a fixed payment of silver coins given to an injured victim’s family to stop violent blood feuds.
Hundred Court Hun-dred Court
An open-air monthly Anglo-Saxon court where local elders heard witness oaths and settled village disputes.
Forest Laws For-est Laws
Strict Norman laws brought in by William the Conqueror banning villagers from hunting wild deer or chopping wood in royal forests.
Poaching Poach-ing
Secretly hunting deer, rabbits, or fish on land claimed by the king or wealthy lords—seen by villagers as a "social crime".
Common Law Com-mon Law
One shared system of royal law across all England created under King Henry II using travelling judges and twelve-person juries.
Magna Carta Mag-na Car-ta
The 1215 "Great Charter" sealed by King John at Runnymede, promising that free citizens could not be jailed without a lawful trial.
Vagrant Va-grant
A homeless Tudor person walking from parish to parish without a job—punished harshly if judged an able-bodied "sturdy beggar".
Poor Law Poor Law
Queen Elizabeth I’s 1601 law ordering parishes to care for sick or elderly locals while forcing "sturdy beggars" to work or face whipping.
High Treason High Trea-son
Plotting to kill or overthrow the monarch or blow up Parliament—punished fiercely in public as terrifying deterrence.
Ducking Stool Duck-ing Stool
A wooden chair on a long waterside lever used in Tudor and Stuart towns to plunge accused scolds or cheats into cold water.
Smuggling Smug-gling
Secretly landing goods like tea, brandy, and silk onto dark beaches at night to dodge heavy government import taxes.
Highwayman High-way-man
An armed robber on horseback—like Dick Turpin—who held up stagecoaches on lonely unlit roads with flintlock pistols.
Bloody Code Blood-y Code
Nickname for harsh 18th-century laws that punished over 220 crimes—even stealing five shillings—with the death penalty.
Transportation Trans-por-ta-tion
Shipping over 160,000 convicted British prisoners in chains between 1787 and 1868 to work in Australian colonies.
Peelers Peel-ers
Popular nickname (alongside "Bobbies") for Britain’s first paid uniformed police, founded in London by Sir Robert Peel in 1829.
Policing by Consent Po-lic-ing by Con-sent
Sir Robert Peel’s famous rule that unarmed British police earn citizens’ trust and respect rather than ruling through army fear.
Separate System Sep-a-rate Sys-tem
Strict Victorian prison plan at Pentonville (1842) keeping prisoners alone in silent cells alongside hard-labour treadwheels.
Forensics Fo-ren-sics
Scientific crime-scene tests—such as 1901 fingerprints, microscope fibres, and DNA—used to prove truth fairly in court.

c. AD 600–899
Saxon Tithings & Wergild: Ten-person tithings, Hue and Cry chases, and King Alfred’s silver wergild stop blood feuds.
c. AD 1079
Norman Royal Forest Laws: William I shifts fines to the Crown and punishes peasant deer poaching severely.
AD 1166 & 1215
12-Person Juries & Magna Carta: Henry II’s Common Law juries triumph as 1215 brings Magna Carta and ends Trial by Ordeal.
AD 1601 & 1605
Poor Law & Gunpowder Treason: 1601 Poor Law splits poor locals from sturdy beggars; Guy Fawkes is caught beside 36 barrels.
AD 1645–1736
Witch Panics Ended by Science: Hopkins exploits Civil War fear until post-1660 science and strict judges end witch trials.
c. AD 1700–1810
Smugglers & The Bloody Code: Tea smugglers and highwaymen prompt 220+ hanging laws—yet merciful juries balk.
AD 1787–1823
Australia & Elizabeth Fry: 160,000+ convicts sail to Australia; Elizabeth Fry’s Newgate school inspires the 1823 Gaols Act.
AD 1829 & 1842

AD 1901–1984+
Fingerprints, 1965 & DNA: 1901 fingerprints, 1965 murder-hanging abolition, and 1984 Leicester DNA transform justice.
