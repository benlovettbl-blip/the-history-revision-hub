KEY ENQUIRY “How did Egypt and Israel make peace—and why did it cost Sadat his life?”
STAGE 1 Kissinger’s Shuttle Diplomacy 1973–1975
“How did Kissinger’s shuttle diplomacy achieve pullbacks and reopen Suez?”

Shocked by the 1973–74 Arab oil crisis that quadrupled fuel prices and determined to sideline Soviet influence in the Middle East, US Secretary of State Henry Kissinger launched tireless Shuttle Diplomacy aboard his aircraft between Jerusalem, Cairo, and Damascus. Rather than risking a deadlocked multi-nation peace conference, Kissinger pursued practical step-by-step bilateral military pullbacks. Kissinger successfully brokered the Sinai I Disengagement Agreement (January 1974), a Golan Heights pullback with Syria (May 1974), and Sinai II (September 1975). Israeli forces withdrew away from the canal banks, enabling Sadat to clear and reopen the Suez Canal in June 1975, restore vital Egyptian toll revenues, and move Cairo firmly into a strategic partnership with Washington.

STAGE 2 Sadat’s Historic Visit to Jerusalem May–Dec 1977
“Why did Sadat visit Jerusalem in 1977, and how did Begin respond in Egypt?”

In May 1977, Israel’s right-wing Likud party won a historic election victory under former Irgun commander Menachem Begin. Facing severe economic hardship and January 1977 bread riots in Egypt—while fearing US-Soviet Geneva talks would stall—President Anwar Sadat took a bold gamble by announcing to Egypt’s parliament that he was ready to travel to Jerusalem himself. In November 1977, Sadat became the first Arab leader to visit Israel officially, addressing the Knesset to offer full peace and recognition in return for occupied Arab land. Begin reciprocated in December 1977 by flying to meet Sadat at Ismailia in Egypt, shattering thirty years of taboo even though direct talks soon deadlocked over Sinai settlements and Palestinian self-rule.

STAGE 3 The Camp David Summit Sept 1978
“How did Jimmy Carter broker the two frameworks of the Camp David Accords?”

In September 1978, US President Jimmy Carter invited Begin and Sadat to the secluded presidential retreat at Camp David, Maryland. Carter sequestered both national delegations for thirteen days of exhaustive negotiations, acting as mediator, draftsman, and diplomatic cajoler when direct personal discussions between the stubborn Israeli Prime Minister and emotional Egyptian President broke down completely in mutual frustration. On 17 September 1978, the leaders signed the historic Camp David Accords. The landmark agreement contained two frameworks: the first detailed terms for an Egyptian-Israeli bilateral peace treaty, while the second promised vague, five-year self-governing autonomy for Palestinians in the West Bank and Gaza, though it avoided addressing sovereign Palestinian statehood or the contested status of East Jerusalem.

STAGE 4 The Egypt-Israel Peace Treaty 26 March 1979
“What territorial and aid terms were agreed in the 1979 Treaty of Washington?”

On 26 March 1979, Sadat and Begin signed the formal Egypt-Israel Peace Treaty—officially known as the Treaty of Washington—on the White House lawn (Egypt and Israel, hosted by the USA), witnessed by President Carter. Ending thirty years of war, the treaty established full diplomatic relations, embassies, and bilateral trade while removing Egypt from any future Arab military coalition against Israel. Under the treaty’s terms, Israel completed a phased withdrawal from the entire Sinai Peninsula, dismantling Jewish settlements including Yamit and returning Sinai oil fields to Egypt. In return, Egypt demilitarised Sinai and guaranteed free Israeli shipping through the Suez Canal and Straits of Tiran, underwritten by billions of dollars in annual US economic and military aid.

STAGE 5 Arab Boycott & The Assassination of Sadat 1979–Oct 1981
“How did Arab states boycott Egypt, and why was Sadat assassinated in 1981?”

The separate peace treaty triggered furious condemnation across the Arab world, which accused Sadat of betraying Palestinian national rights simply to recover Egyptian territory. The Arab League suspended Egypt's membership, severed formal diplomatic relations, moved its headquarters from Cairo to Tunis, and organized sweeping economic boycotts against Egyptian businesses to isolate the Cairo government completely from the Arab community. At home, domestic Islamist fundamentalists condemned Sadat as a traitor to the Islamic cause. On 6 October 1981, while reviewing a military parade in Cairo celebrating the 1973 canal crossing, Sadat was assassinated by Egyptian Islamic Jihad soldiers. His successor took power, upholding the formal treaty with Israel while gradually repairing Egypt's severed diplomatic relations with Arab neighbors over the following decade.

KEY ENQUIRY “How did the Palestinian struggle shift from war in Lebanon to uprising at home?”
STAGE 1 The Israeli Invasion of Lebanon 1974–Aug 1982
“How did Arafat’s 1974 UN speech, PLO raids, and 1982 invasion expel the PLO?”

In November 1974, PLO Chairman Yasser Arafat addressed the UN General Assembly declaring he carried "an olive branch and a freedom fighter’s gun," winning official UN observer status and international recognition for Palestinian self-determination. Simultaneously, PLO cross-border rocket and guerrilla raids from southern Lebanon (Fatahland) provoked Israel’s 1978 Operation Litani incursion and deployment of UNIFIL peacekeepers. In June 1982, Israeli Defence Minister Ariel Sharon launched Operation Peace for Galilee, an armoured invasion intended to push PLO forces 40 kilometres north of Israel’s border. Advancing far beyond cabinet limits, Sharon laid siege to Beirut for ten weeks until US mediation evacuated Arafat and 14,000 PLO fighters by sea in August 1982 to distant exile in Tunis.

STAGE 2 The Sabra and Shatila Massacres Sept 1982–1985
“How did Sabra and Shatila and Hezbollah damage Israel’s campaign in Lebanon?”

Weeks after PLO fighters evacuated to Tunis, Lebanon’s pro-Israeli Christian President-elect Bachir Gemayel was assassinated in a Beirut bomb blast on 14 September 1982. Israeli forces occupied West Beirut, surrounded the Sabra and Shatila refugee camps, and allowed allied Lebanese Christian Phalangist (Kataeb) militiamen inside to clear alleged guerrilla hideouts. Over three days beneath Israeli illumination flares, Phalangist fighters slaughtered between 800 and 2,000 unarmed Palestinian civilians, sparking global outrage and a protest by 400,000 citizens in Tel Aviv. Israel’s independent Kahan Commission of Inquiry found Sharon bore personal responsibility, forcing his resignation; by 1985 Israel withdrew to a southern security zone facing the new Iranian-backed militia Hezbollah.

STAGE 3 Outbreak of the First Intifada Dec 1987–1993
“Why did twenty years of military occupation spark the First Intifada in 1987?”

In December 1987, an Israeli military transport collided with Palestinian civilian cars in Gaza, killing four workers. The funeral sparked spontaneous demonstrations that rapidly spread across the occupied territories, igniting the First Intifada ("shaking off"). Frustrated by twenty years of military occupation, economic stagnation, and lack of civil rights, local Palestinians launched a massive grassroots rebellion against Israeli rule. Directed by the underground UNLU (Unified National Leadership of the Uprising), the rebellion relied on civil disobedience, general strikes, tax boycotts, and commercial shutdowns. Palestinian youths armed with stones and petrol bombs confronting heavily armed Israeli soldiers became the defining global symbol of grassroots resistance against military rule, transforming the international perception of the conflict and discrediting the status quo.

STAGE 4 The Israeli Response & The Rise of Hamas 1987–1988
“How did Israel’s "Iron Fist" affect world opinion, and why did Hamas emerge?”

Israeli Defence Minister Yitzhak Rabin implemented a harsh "Iron Fist" policy, deploying thousands of soldiers authorized to use live ammunition, tear gas, collective curfews, mass detentions, and home demolitions. Worldwide television footage showing Israeli soldiers beating unarmed teenage protesters caused intense moral soul-searching inside Israel and severely damaged the country’s international diplomatic standing across Western capitals. The uprising also spurred radical Islamist militancy across the territories. In December 1987, Sheikh Ahmed Yassin founded [Key Organisation: Hamas] (the Islamic Resistance Movement) in Gaza as a militant rival to the secular PLO. Rejecting all diplomatic compromise, Hamas published an uncompromising charter declaring all of historic Palestine a sacred Islamic trust, pledging to eliminate the State of Israel through armed jihad.

STAGE 5 Arafat Renounces Terrorism & Accepts Resolution 242 Nov–Dec 1988
“How did Arafat’s 1988 UN speech renouncing terrorism open US-PLO dialogue?”

After Jordan’s King Hussein dropped his territorial claim to the West Bank in July 1988 and grassroots Intifada leaders and Hamas challenged PLO authority, Yasser Arafat seized the diplomatic initiative. Meeting in Algiers in November 1988, the Palestinian National Council proclaimed the Declaration of Independence of the State of Palestine, implicitly recognising Israel within a two-state framework. Addressing a special UN General Assembly session in Geneva on 13 December 1988 (December 1988), Arafat explicitly renounced terrorism in all forms and accepted UN Resolution 242 and Resolution 338, confirming Israel’s right to exist in peace and security. Satisfying strict American conditions prompted President Reagan’s administration to open official diplomatic dialogue with the PLO.

KEY ENQUIRY “Why did the Oslo Accords bring hope for peace, yet fail to end the conflict?”
STAGE 1 The Madrid Peace Conference 1989–Oct 1991
“How did the Soviet collapse and 1991 Gulf War lead to the Madrid talks?”

The collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991 ended superpower arms, diplomatic vetoes, and financial patronage for Arab confrontation states, while decisive US victory in the 1990–1991 Gulf War left Washington with unrivalled influence across the Middle East. Crucially, Yasser Arafat’s grave miscalculation in backing Iraq’s Saddam Hussein alienated Saudi Arabia and Kuwait, costing the PLO hundreds of millions of dollars in vital Gulf Arab funding and making Arafat desperate for diplomacy. US President George H. W. Bush and Secretary of State James Baker used this post-war leverage to co-sponsor the Madrid Peace Conference in October 1991. Bringing Israeli, Syrian, Lebanese, and joint Jordanian-Palestinian delegations face to face broke forty-three years of diplomatic taboos, even though Prime Minister Yitzhak Shamir’s hardline Likud stance stalled immediate territorial deals.

STAGE 2 The Secret Oslo Negotiations 1992–Aug 1993
“Why did Rabin and the PLO open secret talks in Oslo, and what was agreed?”

In June 1992, the Israeli Labour Party returned to national power under Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin and Foreign Minister Shimon Peres, who actively favored territorial compromise for peace. Frustrated by deadlocked public talks in Washington, Israeli academics and PLO representatives began holding clandestine backchannel negotiations in Norway in early 1993 with the quiet backing of the Israeli foreign ministry. Hosted by Norwegian diplomats in secluded country houses near Oslo, the negotiators operated in complete secrecy away from media scrutiny and domestic political posturing. Over months of intense dialogue, both sides drafted the Declaration of Principles, establishing a phased five-year plan for Palestinian interim self-government in Gaza and Jericho in exchange for mutual diplomatic recognition and renunciation of violence.

From the Oslo Accords to Oslo II — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 The Oslo I Accord & The White House Handshake 1993–1994
“What did the 1993 Oslo I Accord establish — and leave unresolved?”

On 13 September 1993, 3,000 guests gathered on the White House lawn to witness the signing of the Oslo I Accord, officially titled the Declaration of Principles on Interim Self-Government Arrangements. US President Bill Clinton nudged Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin and PLO Chairman Yasser Arafat into a historic handshake days after Israel and the PLO exchanged formal letters of mutual recognition. Under Oslo’s phased five-year interim framework, Israeli troops withdrew from Gaza and Jericho in May 1994, where the newly established Palestinian National Authority (PNA) assumed civil administration, taxation, and internal policing as Arafat returned triumphantly from exile. However, Oslo I deliberately postponed the hardest final-status issues—Jerusalem, Palestinian refugees, Israeli settlements, security, and permanent borders—for five years.

STAGE 4 Israel-Jordan Peace Treaty & Oslo II 1994–Sept 1995
“What did the 1994 Jordan Treaty and Oslo II (Areas A, B, and C) establish?”

The diplomatic momentum generated by Oslo paved the way for Jordan's King Hussein and Prime Minister Rabin to sign the formal Israel-Jordan Peace Treaty in October 1994 in the Arava desert. The historic pact resolved longstanding territorial and River Jordan water disputes, establishing open borders and making Jordan only the second Arab nation to sign a peace treaty with Israel. In September 1995, Rabin and Arafat signed the comprehensive Oslo II interim agreement, dividing the occupied West Bank into three distinct administrative zones: Area A (full Palestinian civil and security control), Area B (Palestinian civil and joint Israeli security control), and Area C (full Israeli military and civil control, comprising 60% of the territory including settlements and strategic roads).

STAGE 5 Extremist Violence & The Assassination of Rabin 1994–Nov 1995
“How did suicide bombings, Hebron violence, and Rabin’s murder stall peace?”

The advancing peace process triggered fierce, deadly resistance from religious and political extremists on both sides. In February 1994, radical Jewish settler Baruch Goldstein slaughtered 29 Palestinian worshippers in Hebron. Militant factions including Hamas and Islamic Jihad retaliated by unleashing devastating suicide bus bombings in Tel Aviv and Jerusalem, shattering the Israeli public's sense of personal security and eroding support for the accords. Right-wing Israeli hardliners fiercely denounced Rabin as a traitor for ceding biblical land. On 4 November 1995, after addressing a massive peace rally in Tel Aviv, Rabin was assassinated by Yigal Amir, an orthodox Jewish law student opposed to the peace process. Rabin’s murder dealt a catastrophic psychological blow to the peace process, slowing implementation and permanently deepening political divisions inside Israeli society.
