WALK-IN LESSON PLANS • ENQUIRIES 1 & 2 NON-SPECIALIST PRIMARY TEACHER GUIDE
Enquiry 1: Cities in the Rainforest — How Did the Maya Build Thriving Cities in Mesoamerica? (c. 2000 BC–AD 600) TB p.4 • WB pp.3–4
Key Enquiry: How did the Maya build thriving stone cities in Mesoamerica without metal tools or wheels?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This opening Key Stage 2 enquiry locates the Maya world across Mesoamerica (southern Mexico, Guatemala, Belize, Honduras, and El Salvador) and explores its three contrasting zones: southern volcanic highlands, central tropical rainforest lowlands, and the northern limestone Yucatán Peninsula. Primary pupils investigate how Maya engineers solved severe water challenges using cenote sinkholes, plastered stone reservoirs, chultuns, raised wetland fields, and hillside terraces, how Three Sisters companion planting (maize, beans, and squash) fed tens of thousands without metal ploughs or wheeled carts, and how Maya stone cities contrasted with Anglo-Saxon Britain around AD 900.
Learning Objectives:
  • Locate Mesoamerica on a world map and compare the resources of its three contrasting Maya zones.
  • Explain how Maya engineers stored water and grew Three Sisters crops to feed huge cities.
  • Contrast Maya stone rainforest cities with everyday village life in Anglo-Saxon Britain around AD 900.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Around AD 900, travelling from Anglo-Saxon Britain to a Maya city in Mesoamerica would amaze any visitor because the two worlds solved daily challenges in completely different ways. In Anglo-Saxon Britain, families used iron-tipped ploughs and oxen to farm muddy fields around small timber villages and wooden royal halls. Across the Atlantic in Mesoamerica, the Maya had no iron tools or wheeled carts, yet Three Sisters maize harvests and plastered reservoirs supported tens of thousands of people living around towering limestone step-pyramids, stone observatories, and bustling markets!”
Enquiry 2: Step Pyramids & Jade Kings — How Were Rival Maya City-States Ruled? (c. AD 250–900) TB p.5 • WB pp.5–6
Key Enquiry: Were the Classic Maya ruled as one empire, and what reveals the power of their kings and queens?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This second Key Stage 2 enquiry explores Classic Maya governance and monument building (c. AD 250–900). Drawing a clear parallel with Ancient Greece, primary pupils learn that the Maya lived in over forty rival independent city-states (including Tikal, Calakmul, Palenque, Copán, and Yaxchilán), each ruled by a sacred K'uhul Ajaw ("holy lord"). Pupils investigate limestone step-pyramids with corbelled stone vaults, trace King K'inich Janaab' Pakal’s 68-year reign and green jade mosaic funerary mask at Palenque, and examine powerful royal women such as Lady Sak K'uk' and Lady Six Sky of Naranjo.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain why Classic Maya cities were independent rival city-states rather than one single empire.
  • Use archaeological evidence from Palenque to explain how King Pakal displayed sacred royal power.
  • Describe the leadership of powerful royal Maya women and the vibrancy of painted palace courts.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Archaeological discoveries across Mesoamerica reveal that Classic Maya kings and royal women held extraordinary sacred and political power. Every K'uhul Ajaw was honoured as a holy messenger to the gods, proven by King Pakal’s sixty-eight-year reign and his green jade mosaic mask sealed beneath Palenque’s Temple of the Inscriptions. Carved limestone stelae also prove royal women shaped kingdoms directly: Lady Sak K'uk' crowned young Pakal, while Lady Six Sky rebuilt Naranjo and stood victorious on royal monuments alongside vivid palace ceremonies like those painted at Bonampak!”

Enquiry 3: Glyphs, Bark-Paper Books & Zero — Why Were Maya Scribes & Astronomers So Brilliant? (c. AD 300–900) TB p.6 • WB pp.7–8
Key Enquiry: Why do historians celebrate Maya scribes, mathematicians, and astronomers as world pioneers?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This third Key Stage 2 enquiry explores the intellectual achievements of Classic Maya scribes, mathematicians, and astronomers (c. AD 300–900). Primary pupils examine Mesoamerica’s fullest writing system (over 800 word and syllable glyphs carved on stelae and Copán’s 63-step Hieroglyphic Stairway, and painted with turkey-quill brushes onto accordion-folded fig-bark codices like the Dresden Codex). Pupils then master Maya base-20 place-value maths (shell = 0, dot = 1, bar = 5) and trace naked-eye astronomy at Chichén Itzá’s El Caracol observatory linking the 260-day Tzolk'in and 365-day Haab' calendars every 52 years.
Learning Objectives:
  • Describe how Maya scribes recorded history on stone stelae and accordion-folded fig-bark codices.
  • Explain why the Maya independent invention of place-value zero in base-20 maths was so significant.
  • Explain how Maya astronomers tracked the sky from El Caracol to run two interlocking calendars.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Historians consider Maya scribes, mathematicians, and astronomers among the cleverest scholars in world history because they excelled in writing, maths, and sky science all at once! First, scribes created ancient America’s fullest hieroglyphic writing system, using over 800 glyphs on monuments like Copán’s Hieroglyphic Stairway and in folding bark-paper codices. Second, mathematicians independently invented place-value zero in base-20 using just shells, dots, and bars. Finally, sighting Venus from towers like El Caracol without glass telescopes, astronomers predicted eclipses and meshed the 260-day Tzolk'in and 365-day Haab' calendars every 52 years!”
Enquiry 4: Maize Tortillas, Frothy Cacao & Pok-ta-Pok — What Was Everyday Maya Life Like? (c. AD 600–900) TB p.7 • WB pp.9–10
Key Enquiry: What did Classic Maya people eat, trade, and play in everyday life between AD 600 and 900?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This Key Stage 2 enquiry steps inside everyday Classic Maya life (c. AD 600–900). Primary pupils discover how soaking maize in limewater (nixtamal) and grinding it on volcanic stone metates nourished whole cities with tortillas and tamales, how bitter chilli-spiced cacao worked as both a royal drink and everyday market currency, how human porters and canoes traded razor-sharp obsidian and green jade without pack horses, and how athletes played the sacred rubber-ball game Pok-ta-Pok using padded hips.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain how Maya cooks prepared nutritious maize dough on stone metates and brewed frothy spiced cacao.
  • Describe how Maya traders moved obsidian, jade, and quetzal feathers without horses or wheeled carts, using cacao beans as money.
  • Infer how and why the Maya played the sacred rubber-ball game Pok-ta-Pok from ballcourt rings and player figurines.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Everyday food, bustling trade, and sacred Pok-ta-Pok prove the Classic Maya were remarkably clever because cooks used nixtamal limewater soaking and stone metates to unlock vital nutrients from maize dough. Across hundreds of miles without horses, canoe crews and porters linked highland obsidian and green jade with lowland salt and cacao-bean money. Finally, roaring stone ballcourts show how Pok-ta-Pok blended athletic skill in padded hip yokes with deep religious devotion to the gods of sun and rain!”

Enquiry 5: Maize Gods, Rain Caves & Chichén Itzá — How Did Maya Beliefs Shape Their World? (c. AD 600–1200) TB p.8 • WB pp.11–12
Key Enquiry: What did the Maya believe about creation and the gods, and how did beliefs shape Chichén Itzá?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This Key Stage 2 enquiry explores how sacred stories and astronomy shaped Maya art, caves, and pyramids (c. AD 600–1200). Primary pupils read the K'iche' Maya Popol Vuh creation account (mud and wood people failing before gods kneaded yellow and white maize dough, plus the Hero Twins outwitting the lords of Xibalba), meet the Maize God, long-nosed rain god Chaac in sacred cenotes, and feathered serpent Kukulkan, and marvel at Chichén Itzá’s El Castillo (4 × 91 steps + top platform = 365 solar days, casting an equinox serpent shadow).
Learning Objectives:
  • Retell key episodes from the sacred Maya book Popol Vuh, including maize-dough humans and the Hero Twins in Xibalba.
  • Explain how and why Maya communities honoured the Maize God, rain god Chaac, and feathered serpent Kukulkan.
  • Show how Maya builders combined religion and solar astronomy at Chichén Itzá’s El Castillo pyramid.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Sacred beliefs about maize, rain caves, and sky gods shaped almost everything the Maya built and made. Because the Popol Vuh taught that gods kneaded humans from yellow and white maize dough, protecting the corn harvest was a sacred duty. Families honoured the Maize God in Pok-ta-Pok and entered caves and cenotes — seen as doorways to Xibalba — to offer Maya Blue gifts to rain god Chaac. Finally, at Chichén Itzá, devotion to Kukulkan inspired El Castillo’s astonishing 365-step equinox calendar pyramid!”
Enquiry 6: The AD 900 Mystery & Living Maya Today — Did the Maya Really Vanish? (c. AD 800–Present) TB p.9 • WB pp.13–14
Key Enquiry: Why were southern Maya cities abandoned around AD 900, and why is the "vanished Maya" story a myth?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This capstone Key Stage 2 enquiry trains primary pupils in multi-causal historical explanation while dismantling the popular misconception that the Maya "mysteriously vanished." Pupils weigh three linked pressures behind the c. AD 800–950 abandonment of southern lowland cities like Tikal, Palenque, and Copán (lake-core shell evidence of severe multi-year droughts, deforestation from farming and burning lime plaster causing soil erosion, and non-stop royal warfare blocking trade), before tracing Postclassic flourishing in northern cities like Chichén Itzá, Uxmal, and coastal Tulum, courageous resistance to 16th-century Spanish conquest, and over seven million Maya people speaking 30+ Mayan languages today.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain how severe droughts, deforestation, and rival warfare combined to empty southern Maya cities between AD 800 and 950.
  • Prove that Maya civilisation continued flourishing after AD 900 in northern Yucatán cities like Chichén Itzá, Uxmal, and coastal Tulum.
  • Celebrate how over seven million Maya people keep Mayan languages, maize farming, and backstrap weaving alive today.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Weighing archaeological and living evidence proves that the "vanished Maya" story is a complete myth! What really ended around AD 900 was royal rule in southern rainforest cities like Tikal, Palenque, and Copán after multi-year droughts, deforestation, and warfare pushed families out. Farther north, Maya civilisation flourished for centuries around deep cenotes at Chichén Itzá and Uxmal and along Caribbean sea-trade routes at cliffside Tulum. Best of all, over seven million Maya people still speak Mayan languages, farm maize, and weave vibrant huipil cloth today!”
