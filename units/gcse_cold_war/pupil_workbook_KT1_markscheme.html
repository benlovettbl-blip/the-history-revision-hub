The Grand Alliance & Wartime Conferences, 1941–45
Enquiry Question (Page 2): Why did the wartime Grand Alliance unravel between Tehran (1943) and Potsdam (1945)?
Page 3 • Question 1 [4 Marks] Level 1: 1–2m | Level 2: 3–4m (AO1 + AO2)
Explain one consequence of the Potsdam Conference (1945). [4 marks]
Option A (4/4): Divided Zones → Soviet Eastern Bloc Buffer (1945–48):

One consequence of the Potsdam Conference in July–August 1945 was a breakdown in superpower trust that led Stalin to tighten Communist control across Eastern Europe and divided Germany. After President Truman confronted Stalin over Poland following America’s atomic test and blocked heavy reparations from Western zones, Stalin accelerated one-party Soviet satellite takeovers across his buffer zone (1945–48), sparking the 1946 Long and Novikov Telegrams.

Option B (4/4): Economic Reparations Split Across Germany:

One consequence of the Potsdam Conference in July–August 1945 was the economic division of occupied Germany along superpower lines. Because President Truman blocked Joseph Stalin’s demand for crippling reparations from Western Germany—fearing poverty would breed communism—each power extracted reparations mainly from its own zone, stripping the Soviet east of machinery while Western zones rebuilt separately.

Page 3 • Question 2 • Analytical Narrative Account (12 mins) 8 Marks
Write a narrative account analysing the key events of the Tehran, Yalta and Potsdam conferences in the years 1943–45. [8 marks]
Stimulus bullets printed: • the Tehran Conference (1943) • the development of the atomic bomb (1945)
Edexcel Paper 2 Q2 Narrative Tariff (8 Marks — AO1: 4, AO2: 4): Level 1 (1–2m): Simple statements • Level 2 (3–5m): Chronological description with implicit links (capped at 4/8 without own knowledge beyond stimulus) • Level 3 (6–8m): Organised beginning–middle–outcome sequence with explicit causal links & own knowledge beyond the two stimulus points.
Grade 9 Model Answer (8 / 8 Marks):

First, at the Tehran Conference in November 1943, the shared imperative of defeating Hitler drove Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin toward wartime compromise. Britain and the USA promised to launch a Second Front in Nazi-occupied France by May 1944 rather than invading via the Balkans; in return, Stalin pledged to fight Japan once Germany surrendered and backed a post-war United Nations, while securing Western acceptance of Soviet gains in eastern Poland.

This pragmatic unity carried into the Yalta Conference in February 1945, when Nazi defeat was imminent and Red Army forces occupied Poland. The Big Three agreed to divide Germany and Berlin into four occupation zones, set reparations provisionally at $20 billion, and sign the Declaration on Liberated Europe promising free elections, yet sharp tension surfaced as Stalin backed the Communist Lublin Poles against the democratic London Poles.

Consequently, once Hitler fell and anti-communist Harry S. Truman replaced Roosevelt, cooperation unravelled at Potsdam in July 1945. Emboldened by the 16 July New Mexico atomic test—followed by the Hiroshima and Nagasaki bombings in August—Truman no longer needed Soviet help against Japan and challenged Stalin over Poland and reparations, driving Stalin to tighten his Eastern European buffer zone.

Telegrams, Satellite States, Truman Doctrine & Marshall Plan, 1946–48
Enquiry Question (Page 4): How did mutual suspicion in 1946 harden into rival American and Soviet economic blocs by 1948?
Page 5 • Question 1 [4 Marks] Level 1: 1–2m | Level 2: 3–4m (AO1 + AO2)
Explain one consequence of the Truman Doctrine (1947). [4 marks]
Option A (4/4): Containment Policy → Creation of Cominform (Sept 1947):

One consequence of the Truman Doctrine in March 1947 was Joseph Stalin’s decision to tighten ideological control across European communism through Cominform. Viewing Truman’s $400 million containment aid to Greece and Turkey as aggressive American encirclement, Stalin established Cominform in September 1947 to direct satellite Communist parties from Moscow and block them from accepting Western influence.

Option B (4/4): Containment Policy → Launch of the $13bn Marshall Plan (June 1947):

One consequence of the Truman Doctrine in March 1947 was America’s commitment to active containment, backed immediately by the June 1947 Marshall Plan. Having pledged $400 million to defeat communism in Greece and Turkey after Britain withdrew, Washington reinforced containment economically with over $13 billion in Marshall Aid to rebuild Western Europe and prevent Communist electoral gains.

Page 5 • Question 3 • Explain Importance (12 mins) 8 Marks
Explain the importance of the Marshall Plan (1947) for US-Soviet relations. [8 marks]
Edexcel Paper 2 Q3 Importance Tariff (8 Marks — AO1: 4, AO2: 4): Level 1 (1–2m): Generalised assertions • Level 2 (3–5m): Descriptive account with limited link to importance • Level 3 (6–8m): Two distinct, factually supported analytical paragraphs directly explaining why X mattered for Y.
Grade 9 Model Answer (8 / 8 Marks):

First, the Marshall Plan was important for US-Soviet relations because it economically stabilised Western Europe into a prosperous pro-American bloc and halted communism’s westward appeal. Approved rapidly after the February 1948 Czechoslovak coup, Secretary of State George Marshall’s programme channelled over $13 billion in food, fuel, machinery, and loans across 16 Western European nations by 1952. Eliminating post-war hunger and unemployment undercut Communist parties in France and Italy while requiring recipients to trade freely with the United States, cementing American economic leadership against Soviet influence.

Second, the Marshall Plan was also important for US-Soviet relations because it provoked Joseph Stalin into sealing Eastern Europe inside a rival political and economic bloc. Viewing open financial records and American loans as "dollar imperialism" designed to pry satellite states out of Moscow’s buffer zone, Stalin forbade Poland and Czechoslovakia from attending the Paris talks. In direct retaliation, he founded Cominform in September 1947—expelling Tito’s Yugoslavia in 1948—and Comecon in January 1949, cutting East–West trade and locking Europe into two rival camps.

The Berlin Crisis (1948–49), NATO, Arms Race & Warsaw Pact
Enquiry Question (Page 6): How did the 1948–49 Berlin Crisis divide Germany and create rival military alliances?
Page 7 • Question 1 [4 Marks] Level 1: 1–2m | Level 2: 3–4m (AO1 + AO2)
Explain one consequence of the Berlin Crisis of 1948–49. [4 marks]
Option A (4/4): End of Four-Power Rule → Creation of FRG & GDR (1949):

One consequence of the Berlin Crisis of 1948–49 was the permanent division of Germany into two rival states. Because Stalin’s blockade destroyed any remaining prospect of four-power reunification, the Western Allies merged Trizonia into the democratic Federal Republic of Germany (FRG) in May 1949, prompting Stalin to turn the Soviet zone into the Communist German Democratic Republic (GDR) in October 1949.

Option B (4/4): Blockade Alarm → Formation of NATO (April 1949):

One consequence of the Berlin Crisis of 1948–49 was the creation of NATO as a peacetime Western military alliance. Alarmed by Stalin’s blockade of West Berlin, the USA, Britain, France, and nine allies signed the North Atlantic Treaty on 4 April 1949 around Article 5 collective security, stationing American troops and bombers permanently in Western Europe.

Page 7 • Question 2 • Analytical Narrative Account (12 mins) 8 Marks
Write a narrative account analysing the key events of the Berlin Crisis in the years 1948–49. [8 marks]
Stimulus bullets printed: • the division of Berlin into zones • the Berlin airlift (1948–49)
Edexcel Paper 2 Q2 Narrative Tariff (8 Marks — AO1: 4, AO2: 4): Level 1 (1–2m): Simple statements • Level 2 (3–5m): Chronological description with implicit links (capped at 4/8 without own knowledge beyond stimulus) • Level 3 (6–8m): Organised beginning–middle–outcome sequence with explicit causal links & own knowledge beyond the two stimulus points.
Grade 9 Model Answer (8 / 8 Marks):

First, the Yalta and Potsdam agreements divided both Germany and Berlin—lying 100 miles inside the Soviet zone—into four occupation zones. While Stalin stripped eastern reparations, Britain, the USA, and France merged their zones into Bizonia (1947) and Trizonia (1948) and introduced a new currency, the Deutschmark, in June 1948; fearing a prosperous capitalist West Germany, Stalin severed all road, rail, and canal links into West Berlin on 24 June 1948.

Refusing either to abandon 2.1 million West Berliners or risk World War Three by forcing armed convoys across East German roads, President Truman and Britain launched the Berlin Airlift (Operation Vittles and Operation Plainfare) on 26 June 1948. Flying down three agreed 20-mile air corridors—which Stalin dared not shoot down—Allied crews completed over 275,000 flights into Tempelhof, Gatow, and Tegel, delivering up to 8,000–13,000 tons of coal and food daily.

Consequently, realising his blockade had failed and alienated world opinion, Stalin reopened land routes on 12 May 1949. Far from expelling the Allies, the crisis spurred twelve Western nations to form NATO in April 1949 and destroyed four-power reunification, splitting Germany into West Germany (FRG) in May 1949 and East Germany (GDR) in October 1949.

The Hungarian Uprising & International Reaction, 1956
Enquiry Question (Page 10): Why did Khrushchev crush the 1956 Hungarian Uprising—and why didn’t the West help?
Page 11 • Question 1 [4 Marks] Level 1: 1–2m | Level 2: 3–4m (AO1 + AO2)
Explain one consequence of the Soviet invasion of Hungary in 1956. [4 marks]
Option A (4/4): Tightened Warsaw Pact Discipline:

One consequence of the Soviet invasion of Hungary in November 1956 was the ruthless tightening of Soviet control across the Warsaw Pact. After over 1,000 tanks crushed Budapest, Khrushchev installed loyalist János Kádár, arrested 35,000 activists, and hanged Imre Nagy in June 1958, deterring any other Eastern European satellite state from attempting to leave the Soviet bloc.

Option B (4/4): Refugee Crisis & Western Restraint:

One consequence of the Soviet invasion of Hungary on 4 November 1956 was a major humanitarian crisis that exposed the limits of Western intervention. Two weeks of street fighting killed around 2,500 Hungarians and drove 200,000 refugees into Austria, while President Eisenhower restricted US support to $20 million in aid rather than risking nuclear war.

Page 11 • Question 3 • Explain Importance (12 mins) 8 Marks
Explain the importance of the Hungarian Uprising (1956) for US-Soviet relations. [8 marks]
Edexcel Paper 2 Q3 Importance Tariff (8 Marks — AO1: 4, AO2: 4): Level 1 (1–2m): Generalised assertions • Level 2 (3–5m): Descriptive account with limited link to importance • Level 3 (6–8m): Two distinct, factually supported analytical paragraphs directly explaining why X mattered for Y.
Grade 9 Model Answer (8 / 8 Marks):

First, the Hungarian Uprising in 1956 was important for US-Soviet relations because it exposed the practical limits of American containment inside Eastern Europe and emboldened Nikita Khrushchev. Although US-funded Radio Free Europe broadcasts and John Foster Dulles had spoken of "rolling back" communism, President Eisenhower refused to send troops to save Imre Nagy because intervening inside an existing Warsaw Pact state risked thermonuclear hydrogen-bomb war—especially while Britain and France distracted NATO in the Suez Crisis. Seeing Washington restrict itself to UN condemnation and $20 million in relief convinced Khrushchev that the West dared not challenge Soviet control militarily.

Second, the Hungarian Uprising was also important for US-Soviet relations because its brutal suppression shattered the mid-1950s thaw and deepened Cold War hostility. Watching over 1,000 Soviet tanks kill around 2,500 Hungarians, drive 200,000 refugees into Austria, and install János Kádár exposed Khrushchev’s "peaceful coexistence" slogan as hollow. Western outrage—including 1956 Melbourne Olympics boycotts and mass resignations from Western Communist parties—poisoned diplomatic trust and set up a sharper superpower confrontation over Berlin in 1958.

Explain one consequence of the Long and Novikov telegrams (1946). [4 marks]
Grade 9 Model Answer (4 / 4 Marks):

One consequence of the Long and Novikov telegrams in 1946 was a sharp hardening of mutual superpower distrust that ended wartime cooperation. Kennan’s Long Telegram (February 1946) convinced President Truman that the USSR sought to destroy rival capitalist power and had to be met with firm containment, while Novikov’s September 1946 telegram convinced Stalin that America sought "world supremacy", accelerating Moscow’s creation of Eastern European satellite states.

Explain one consequence of Cominform (1947) and Comecon (1949). [4 marks]
Grade 9 Model Answer (4 / 4 Marks):

One consequence of establishing Cominform (September 1947) and Comecon (January 1949) was the strict political and economic binding of Eastern Europe’s satellite states to Moscow. Stalin used Cominform to enforce ideological obedience—expelling Tito’s independent Yugoslavia in 1948—while Comecon barred satellite governments from accepting American Marshall Aid and redirected their trade exclusively around Soviet industrial priorities.

Page 14 • Question 1: Explain Two Consequences [8 Marks — 2 × 4m] Mark Each Consequence Independently out of 4
1  Explain two consequences of the formation of the Warsaw Pact (1955). [8 marks]
Pearson Edexcel Paper 2 Q1 Marking Rule: Mark Consequence 1 (0–4 marks) and Consequence 2 (0–4 marks) separately. Level 1 (1–2m): Simple/general statement • Level 2 (3–4m): Specific factual detail + explained causal outcome.
Consequence 1 — Grade 9 Model Answer (4 / 4 Marks):

One consequence of the formation of the Warsaw Pact in May 1955 was the permanent division of Europe into two armed military blocs facing each other across the Iron Curtain. Formed in direct response to West Germany joining NATO, the treaty placed the armed forces of seven Eastern European satellite states under unified Soviet military command, increasing tension and accelerating the conventional and nuclear arms race.

Consequence 2 — Grade 9 Model Answer (4 / 4 Marks):

A second consequence was that the Warsaw Pact gave Moscow a legal and military mechanism to enforce Communist control inside Eastern Europe itself. When Hungarian Prime Minister Imre Nagy announced on 1 November 1956 that Hungary would withdraw from the Warsaw Pact and become neutral, Khrushchev used the alliance’s strategic security to justify sending over 1,000 tanks into Budapest on 4 November to crush the uprising.

Page 15 • Question 2 • Analytical Narrative Account [8 Marks] (Unscaffolded) 8 Marks (AO1: 4 + AO2: 4)
2  Write a narrative account analysing the key events of the Hungarian Uprising in 1956. [8 marks]
Stimulus bullets printed: • the Hungarian Uprising (October 1956) • the Soviet invasion of Hungary (November 1956)
Level 3 (6–8 Marks): Organised beginning–developments–outcome chronological sequence linked by explicit causal connectives, integrating accurate own knowledge beyond the two stimulus bullets.
Grade 9 Model Answer (8 / 8 Marks):

Following years of brutal Stalinist repression and falling living standards under Mátyás Rákosi and the hated ÁVH secret police, Nikita Khrushchev’s February 1956 "Secret Speech" denouncing Stalin’s tyranny raised Hungarian hopes for reform. When student protesters in Budapest demanded free elections, a free press, and the withdrawal of Soviet troops on 23 October 1956—toppling Stalin’s giant statue—clashes with ÁVH police escalated into a nationwide uprising that forced Moscow to appoint reformist Communist Imre Nagy as Prime Minister.

Initially, Khrushchev hoped concessions would calm Budapest and pulled Red Army tanks out of the capital while Nagy abolished one-party rule and released Cardinal Mindszenty. However, on 1 November 1956 Nagy crossed Moscow’s strategic red line by announcing that Hungary would leave the Warsaw Pact and appeal to the United Nations as a neutral state, prompting Mao Zedong and Warsaw Pact hardliners to warn Khrushchev that losing Hungary would shatter the entire Eastern Bloc.

Consequently, on 4 November 1956 Khrushchev launched Operation Whirlwind, sending over 1,000 Soviet tanks back into Budapest while Western powers were distracted by the Suez Crisis and unwilling to risk nuclear war. Around 2,500 Hungarians were killed and 200,000 fled west as refugees; Moscow installed pro-Soviet leader János Kádár, executed Nagy in 1958, and proved that de-Stalinisation would never permit leaving the Warsaw Pact.
