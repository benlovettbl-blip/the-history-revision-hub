Pearson Edexcel 1HI0/B4 Q1 Marking Rule: Award 1 mark for identifying a valid feature and 1 mark for specific supporting factual detail. Do not award 2 marks for two bare assertions without supporting detail.
1 (a)  Describe one feature of the drive to expand trade in the Elizabethan era.
Question 1(a) — Grade 9 Model Answer (2 / 2 Marks):

One feature of the drive to expand trade was establishing royal chartered joint-stock companies to bypass Spanish and Antwerp markets. Merchants pooled capital in the Muscovy Company (Russia) and Levant Company (1581, Ottoman Empire) while explorers like Martin Frobisher searched for a Northwest Passage to Asia.

1 (b)  Describe one feature of the first settlement in Virginia.
Question 1(b) — Grade 9 Model Answer (2 / 2 Marks):

One feature of the first Virginia settlement at Roanoke (1585–86) was its flawed recruitment of 107 all-male colonists under Ralph Lane and Richard Grenville. Roughly half were gentlemen soldiers seeking gold rather than skilled farmers capable of growing crops to make the colony self-sufficient.

Pages 14–15 • Question 2: Explain Why [12 Marks] (Unscaffolded) 12 Marks (AO1: 6 + AO2: 6)
2  Explain why the first settlement in Virginia failed.
Stimulus bullets printed: • Supplies and the voyage • Relations with Native Americans (Candidates must also include own knowledge beyond the stimulus to progress above Level 2 / 6 marks.)
Level 4 (10–12 Marks): Three analytical causation paragraphs consistently directed at the conceptual focus of the question, supported by precise dates/terminology and own knowledge beyond the two stimulus bullets.
Grade 9 Model Answer (12 / 12 Marks):

First, disastrous problems with the 1585 voyage and lost supplies crippled the Roanoke settlement from the moment it reached North America. Setting sail from Plymouth in April 1585—already too late in the agricultural year to plant spring crops—flagship Tiger struck a shallow sandbar at Ocracoke Inlet on 29 June 1585. Flooding seawater ruined almost all the expedition’s seed corn, salt, and gunpowder, leaving the settlers unable to grow harvests for the coming winter or preserve meat and fish.

Second, flawed recruitment and weak colonial planning compounded these supply shortages. Sir Walter Raleigh’s 107 all-male colonists lacked essential farming families, stone-masons, and fishermen: roughly half were soldiers and status-conscious gentlemen expecting easy gold or Native labour rather than manual agricultural toil, while military governor Ralph Lane clashed bitterly with naval commander Sir Richard Grenville. Without seed grain or farming skills, the garrison could never achieve self-sufficiency before Grenville sailed back to England for resupply.

Finally, deteriorating relations with local Algonquian Native Americans destroyed the settlers’ only remaining source of food and forced total evacuation in June 1586. Initially reliant on Chief Wingina’s Secotan people for corn and fish traps, Grenville torched the village of Aquascogoc over a missing silver cup, and as winter hunger bit, Wingina (renamed Pemisapan) refused to hand over scarce tribal grain stores and planned resistance. When Ralph Lane launched a preemptive ambush on 1 June 1586 and beheaded Wingina, surrounding tribes cut off all food supplies; facing starvation and retaliation, the surviving colonists abandoned Virginia aboard Francis Drake’s passing fleet on 18 June 1586.
