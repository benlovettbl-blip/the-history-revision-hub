Page 14 • Question 1(a) & 1(b): Describe One Feature [4 Marks — 2 × 2m] Mark 1(a) out of 2 and 1(b) out of 2 Independently
Pearson Edexcel 1HI0/B4 Q1 Marking Rule: Award 1 mark for identifying a valid feature and 1 mark for specific supporting factual detail. Do not award 2 marks for two bare assertions without supporting detail.
1 (a)  Describe one feature of Elizabeth’s religious settlement (1559).
Question 1(a) — Grade 9 Model Answer (2 / 2 Marks):

One feature of Elizabeth’s religious settlement (1559) was the Act of Supremacy, which ended papal authority in England and named Elizabeth ‘Supreme Governor’ of the Church of England. All clergy and royal officials had to swear an Oath of Supremacy accepting her authority or lose their posts.

1 (b)  Describe one feature of the problem of marriage for Elizabeth.
Question 1(b) — Grade 9 Model Answer (2 / 2 Marks):

One feature of the problem of marriage was the no-win political choice between domestic and foreign suitors. Marrying an English courtier like Robert Dudley risked violent noble jealousy and faction, whereas marrying a foreign Catholic prince like Philip II risked foreign domination and Protestant anger.

Pages 14–15 • Question 2: Explain Why [12 Marks] (Unscaffolded) 12 Marks (AO1: 6 + AO2: 6)
2  Explain why Elizabeth faced challenges to the religious settlement in the years 1559–69.
Stimulus bullets printed: • The Puritan challenge • Foreign powers (Candidates must also include own knowledge beyond the stimulus to progress above Level 2 / 6 marks.)
Level 4 (10–12 Marks): Three analytical causation paragraphs consistently directed at the conceptual focus of the question, supported by precise dates/terminology and own knowledge beyond the two stimulus bullets.
Grade 9 Model Answer (12 / 12 Marks):

First, Elizabeth faced vocal domestic challenges from radical Puritans who believed the 1559 settlement was an incomplete ‘half-reformed’ compromise that retained popish superstitions. Influenced by Calvinist Geneva during Mary I’s reign, returning Marian exiles objected fiercely to crucifixes, organs, saint days, and clerical vestments, viewing the white linen surplice as ‘rags of Rome’. This provoked the 1562 Crucifix Controversy in the Royal Chapel, a narrow one-vote battle in the 1563 Convocation, and open clerical defiance in 1566 when 37 London ministers refused Archbishop Parker’s Book of Advertisements and had to be sacked.

Second, deep-rooted Catholic loyalty among England’s northern nobility and rural gentry created persistent resistance to Protestant uniformity. Roughly one-third of peers and a majority of northern JPs remained sympathetic to Catholicism; thousands of ‘Church Papists’ conformed only outwardly on Sundays while sheltering former Marian priests to celebrate Latin Mass in manor houses. Once Pope Pius V instructed Catholics in 1566 to boycott Anglican worship—and especially after Catholic claimant Mary, Queen of Scots arrived in England in May 1568—northern earls like Northumberland and Westmorland rallied around restoring Catholicism, culminating in the 1569 Revolt of the Northern Earls.

Finally, shifting actions by Catholic foreign powers steadily intensified pressure on the settlement by the late 1560s. Although France was distracted by its Wars of Religion (1562) and Philip II initially preserved friendship to counter French influence, the Counter-Reformation sharpened international hostility: William Allen founded the Douai seminary in 1568 to train missionary priests, while Philip II dispatched the Duke of Alba with 10,000 ruthless Spanish troops to the Netherlands in 1567 right across the Channel. Combined with the 1568 Genoese Loan crisis, Alba’s nearby Catholic army emboldened English Catholic conspirators to believe foreign military help might back a rebellion against Elizabeth’s Church.
