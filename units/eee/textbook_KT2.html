Key Historical Actors & Essential Concepts (1569–1588)

1569–1588
KEY HISTORICAL INDIVIDUALS & ACTORS
Sir Francis Walsingham (c.1532–1590) Elizabeth’s "Spymaster"

Built a European intelligence network of decipherers (Thomas Phelippes) and double agents that trapped Babington and Mary Stuart.

Sir Francis Drake (c.1540–1596) Privateer & Vice-Admiral

Circumnavigated the globe (1577–80), raided Cadiz (1587), and commanded fast race-built galleons against the Armada at Gravelines.

Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester Royal Favourite & Commander

Led the 1585–87 English expedition to the Netherlands, though hampered by underfunding and quarrels after accepting "Governor-General".

Duke of Medina Sidonia (1550–1615) Spanish Armada Commander

Aristocrat appointed after Santa Cruz died in Feb 1588; courageous administrator but lacked naval combat experience.

Duke of Parma (1545–1592) Spanish Commander in Flanders

Commanded 27,000 veteran troops in the Netherlands, but lacked a deep-water port while Dutch Sea Beggars blockaded his barges.

Earls of Northumberland & Westmorland Northern Catholic Rebels

Resented losing border influence to Protestant newcomers like Forster; led the failed November 1569 northern rebellion.

ESSENTIAL DISCIPLINARY CONCEPTS & TERMINOLOGY
Council of the North (1572) Reorganised royal council at York under the Puritan Earl of Huntingdon to govern the Catholic north.
Seminary Priests & Jesuits Douai-trained priests and Society of Jesus missionaries (Campion, Parsons) sent secretly to sustain Catholicism.
Bond of Association (1584) Pledge made law in 1585 that anyone in whose name a plot was hatched against Elizabeth would be executed.
Pacification of Ghent (1576) United all 17 Dutch provinces demanding expulsion of mutinous Spanish troops after the Spanish Fury.
Treaty of Joinville (Dec 1584) Secret alliance between Philip II and the French Catholic League that forced Elizabeth to intervene in 1585.
Treaty of Nonsuch (Aug 1585) Committed Elizabeth to fund 7,400 English soldiers in the Netherlands, effectively starting war with Spain.
Race-Built Galleons & Culverins John Hawkins’ lower, faster English warships firing long-range broadsides without letting Spain board.
"Protestant Wind" (1588) Elizabethan commemorative medal ("Flavit Deus et Dissipati Sunt") crediting God’s gales for scattering Spain.

KEY ENQUIRY “Why did Catholic rebellions and plots finally persuade Elizabeth to execute Mary in 1587?”
STAGE 1 The Revolt of the Northern Earls NOV 1569–JAN 1570
“Why did the Revolt of the Northern Earls fail to depose Elizabeth?”

Following Norfolk’s arrest, the Northern Earls — Thomas Percy, Earl of Northumberland, and Charles Neville, Earl of Westmorland — rose in November 1569. They aimed to rescue Mary Stuart from Tutbury and restore Catholicism. Marching 4,600 men into Durham Cathedral, rebels tore up the English Bible and prayer book and celebrated a defiant Latin Mass. However, promised Spanish troops from Alba never landed at Hartlepool, and most northern gentry stayed loyal to the Queen. Elizabeth moved Mary south to Coventry and sent a royal army north under the Earl of Sussex. As the earls fled into Scotland, Elizabeth ordered 450 rebels executed under martial law and reshaped the Council of the North.

STAGE 2 Papal Bull & The Ridolfi Plot 1570–1572
“How did the 1570 Papal Bull and Ridolfi Plot endanger the Queen?”

Too late to save the northern rebels, Pope Pius V issued the papal bull Regnans in Excelsis in February 1570. It excommunicated Elizabeth as a heretic and ordered English Catholics to end their allegiance to her. Overnight, devout English Catholics looked like potential traitors, prompting the harsh 1571 Treasons Act outlawing papal bulls. In 1571, Florentine banker Roberto Ridolfi organised the international Ridolfi Plot. Philip II promised 10,000 Spanish troops under Alba to invade England, assassinate Elizabeth, and put Mary Stuart on the throne married to Norfolk. Cecil and Walsingham intercepted Ridolfi’s coded letters, leading to the Duke of Norfolk’s execution for treason in June 1572.

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 The Throckmorton Plot & Bond of Association 1583–1584
“Why did the Throckmorton Plot produce the Bond of Association?”

After 1574, Douai seminary and Jesuit priests entered England in secret, bringing Edmund Campion’s execution and £20 monthly recusancy fines in 1581. In November 1583, Secretary of State Sir Francis Walsingham uncovered the Throckmorton Plot. Under torture, courier Francis Throckmorton confessed a plan for Mary’s cousin, the French Catholic Duke of Guise, to invade Sussex. Spain’s London ambassador Bernardino de Mendoza was funding the plot with Spanish gold, so Elizabeth expelled him and broke diplomatic ties with Madrid. In 1584, Cecil and Walsingham drew up the Bond of Association (made law in 1585). Signatories pledged to execute anyone on whose behalf an assassination of Elizabeth was attempted.

STAGE 4 The Babington Plot & Walsingham’s Spies JULY 1586
“How did Walsingham’s beer-barrel cipher trap expose Babington and Mary?”

In 1586, Catholic gentleman Anthony Babington organised the Babington Plot to murder Elizabeth, rescue Mary from Chartley Hall, and trigger a Franco-Spanish invasion of 60,000 troops backed by the Pope. Unknown to Mary, Walsingham’s double agent Gilbert Gifford routed every coded letter hidden inside beer barrels straight to Walsingham’s desk. Codebreaker Thomas Phelippes deciphered every message until, on 17 July 1586, Mary dictated a reply backing Elizabeth’s ‘dispatch’ (assassination) before foreign troops landed. Phelippes forged a postscript asking for the six assassins’ names, letting Walsingham arrest Babington, execute the plotters, and seize Mary’s papers.

STAGE 5 The Execution of Mary, Queen of Scots 8 FEB 1587
“Why was Mary’s 1587 execution at Fotheringhay Castle so significant?”

Tried before thirty-six royal commissioners at Fotheringhay Castle in October 1586 under the 1585 Act for the Preservation of the Queen’s Safety, Mary, Queen of Scots was convicted of high treason. Agonising over executing an anointed monarch and provoking foreign war, Elizabeth delayed until February 1587, when her Privy Council secretly dispatched the signed warrant. On 8 February 1587, Mary, Queen of Scots’ execution took place in the Great Hall at Fotheringhay, where forty-four-year-old Mary wore bright crimson martyrdom petticoats as a Catholic martyr. Although Scottish King James VI accepted Elizabeth’s apology, Mary bequeathed her English claim to Philip II, removing the domestic Catholic figurehead while spurring Spain toward invasion.

KEY ENQUIRY “Why did rivalry with Spain slide toward open war between 1569 and 1585?”
STAGE 1 Political and Religious Rivalry with Spain 1570–1577
“Why did Spanish rule in the Netherlands threaten English security?”

Throughout the 1570s, Anglo-Spanish relations deteriorated sharply over political, religious, and commercial rivalry centred on the Spanish Netherlands. Low Countries Channel ports lay directly opposite the Thames estuary, while Antwerp handled most English woollen cloth exports. Elizabeth feared King Philip II would crush Dutch provincial liberties and station a permanent Catholic invasion army facing southern England. When Dutch Protestants led by William of Orange rebelled against the Duke of Alba’s harsh taxes and Catholic persecution, Elizabeth carefully avoided open war. Instead, she sent quiet financial loans, allowed English Protestant volunteers to fight, and expelled Dutch Sea Beggars from English ports in 1572, triggering their capture of Brielle and widening the Dutch Revolt.

STAGE 2 Commercial Rivalry in the New World 1570–1579
“How did Francis Drake’s privateering challenge Philip II’s trade monopoly?”

At the same time, fierce commercial rivalry erupted across the Atlantic as English privateers challenged Philip II’s closed monopoly over New World trade. Spanish warships had ambushed John Hawkins and Francis Drake at San Juan de Ulúa in 1568. In response, Drake launched daring privateering raids across the Spanish Main, seizing silver trains at Nombre de Dios in Panama. Rather than punishing her captains, Elizabeth secretly invested ships and money in these raids while issuing polite denials to Madrid. After unpaid Spanish troops sacked Antwerp in the brutal Spanish Fury of November 1576, Elizabeth lent the Dutch Estates £100,000 under the Pacification of Ghent.

Relations with Spain — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 Drake’s Circumnavigation 1577–1580
“How did Drake’s voyage in the Golden Hind raid Spanish Pacific wealth?”

In December 1577, Elizabeth secretly backed Drake’s five-ship voyage from Plymouth to sail through the Strait of Magellan and raid Spanish Pacific ports, while claiming he was bound for Alexandria. Surviving fierce gales in his renamed flagship Golden Hind, Drake raided unguarded Spanish ports along Chile and Peru. In March 1579, Drake captured Spain’s rich Pacific treasure galleon Cacafuego, seizing over £140,000 in silver, gold, and jewels before crossing the Pacific and Indian oceans. Returning to Plymouth in September 1580 with roughly £400,000 in total plunder, Drake gave Elizabeth enough Crown share to clear England’s entire national debt.

STAGE 4 The Deptford Knighting & Royal Defiance APRIL 1581
“Why did knighting Drake at Deptford infuriate King Philip II?”

Furious at Drake’s Pacific raids, Philip II grew bolder after annexing Portugal and its Atlantic fleet in 1580. Through ambassador Mendoza, he demanded Drake’s execution as a pirate and the return of the treasure. Instead, in April 1581 Elizabeth boarded the Golden Hind at Deptford on the Thames and publicly awarded Drake’s knighthood on deck. Handing the gilded sword to a French envoy while courting the Catholic Duke of Alençon and funding his Dutch campaign, Elizabeth signalled an anti-Spanish alignment with France. Publicly honouring ‘El Draque’ humiliated Philip II before Europe and proved the English Crown backed attacks on Spain’s empire.

STAGE 5 The Treaties of Joinville & Nonsuch 1584–AUG 1585
“Why did William of Orange’s death and Joinville force Nonsuch (1585)?”

By 1584, indirect diplomacy collapsed. Under Spain’s able general, the Duke of Parma, Spanish forces steadily retook southern Dutch towns, and on 10 July 1584 a Catholic assassin shot dead William of Orange at Delft. Worse still, in December 1584 Philip II signed the Treaty of Joinville with the French Catholic League against Protestantism. With France tied down by civil war and Antwerp falling to Parma, Elizabeth could no longer rely on indirect aid. On 10 August 1585, she signed the Treaty of Nonsuch with the Dutch Estates. England garrisoned Brill and Flushing and sent 7,400 soldiers under Robert Dudley — an act Philip II treated as open war.

KEY ENQUIRY “How did war in the Netherlands and Drake’s raid on Cadiz trigger the Armada?”
STAGE 1 Dudley’s Campaign in the Netherlands DEC 1585–1586
“Why did Dudley’s title of Governor-General anger both Queen and Dutch?”

Landing in the Netherlands in December 1585 with 7,400 men after England garrisoned Flushing and Brielle, Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester, enraged Elizabeth in January 1586. Without consulting her, he accepted the supreme title Governor-General of the United Provinces. Furious that this openly rejected Philip II’s rule, Elizabeth humiliated Dudley before the Dutch. Throughout 1586–87, Elizabeth starved the campaign of money, siege guns, and troops while still hoping for a compromise peace with Madrid. Unpaid English soldiers suffered hunger and desertion. Even so, Dudley’s garrisons slowed the Duke of Parma’s advance by a year and held deep-water Ostend, denying Spain a Channel harbour.

STAGE 2 Treachery at Deventer and Zutphen 1586–JAN 1587
“How did betrayals at Deventer and Zutphen undermine Dudley’s campaign?”

Leicester’s Dutch campaign soon suffered heavy blows. Outside Zutphen in autumn 1586, Protestant courtier-poet Sir Philip Sidney was mortally wounded. Worse followed in January 1587 when two Catholic English officers appointed by Leicester — Sir William Stanley and Rowland Yorke — switched sides. They betrayed Deventer and Zutphen fort directly to Parma’s Spanish army. Stanley and Yorke’s defection wrecked Dutch trust in English loyalty, especially amid rumours of secret Anglo-Spanish peace talks. Unable to work smoothly with the Dutch States-General or match Parma’s 30,000 veteran soldiers, a discredited Dudley was permanently recalled to England in late 1587.

The Outbreak of War with Spain — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 Drake’s Raid on Cadiz 1587
“How did Drake’s raid on Cadiz disrupt Spain’s invasion preparations?”

Early in 1587, Walsingham’s spies reported that Philip II was assembling a vast invasion Armada following Nonsuch and Mary Stuart’s execution. Elizabeth ordered Francis Drake to strike pre-emptively at Spanish ports. On 19 April 1587, Drake sailed twenty-four warships straight into Cadiz harbour in a daring raid nicknamed ‘Singeing the King of Spain’s Beard’. Over three days of bombardment, Drake’s gunners burned or sank 30 Spanish ships and destroyed thousands of tonnes of supplies. Sailing toward the Azores, Drake also captured the Portuguese spice carrack San Felipe (£108,000). Diverting Spanish warships to hunt Drake delayed the Armada’s launch by a vital year until 1588.

STAGE 4 Spanish Plans & Preparations for the Armada 1587–EARLY 1588
“Why did burned barrel staves and Santa Cruz’s death weaken the fleet?”

Off Cape St Vincent, Drake’s raids inflicted hidden logistical damage by burning huge stockpiles of seasoned oak barrel staves and hoops meant for the Armada’s food and water casks. Forced to make hasty replacements from damp green pine, Spanish crews loaded leaky barrels that warped at sea and turned water and salted meat foul. Spanish command weakened further in February 1588 when veteran admiral Santa Cruz died suddenly in Lisbon. Hoping his fleet would still meet the Duke of Parma, Philip II appointed the reluctant Duke of Medina Sidonia. Though an able administrator, Sidonia lacked sea battle experience, suffered chronic seasickness, and had to obey rigid royal orders.

STAGE 5 The Armada Sets Sail MAY–JULY 1588
“What fatal flaw undermined Philip II’s plan to link Sidonia and Parma?”

After spring storms forced hull repairs at Corunna, the 130 ships of Spain’s Armada — carrying 30,000 sailors and soldiers — entered the English Channel in late July 1588. Philip II ordered Medina Sidonia to sail up the Channel to Calais and Flanders, meet Parma’s 27,000 troops, and guard their barges across to Kent. Yet Philip’s plan had a fatal flaw: Parma controlled no deep-water port in Flanders. His unarmed, flat-bottomed barges needed forty-eight calm hours to emerge from shallow canals watched by nimble Dutch flyboats under Justin of Nassau. With slow courier boats, Medina Sidonia and Parma could never time their meeting.

KEY ENQUIRY “Why did Philip II’s Spanish Armada fail to conquer England in 1588?”
STAGE 1 Channel Battles: Plymouth to Isle of Wight 29 JUL–4 AUG 1588
“Why did the Spanish crescent hold firm during Channel skirmishes?”

Sighted off the Lizard in Cornwall on 29 July 1588, Medina Sidonia’s 130 Spanish ships sailed up the English Channel in a tight seven-mile crescent formation. Heavy fighting galleons guarded the wings while slower supply ships stayed in the centre. Warping out of Plymouth against the wind, Lord Howard of Effingham, Drake, and Hawkins seized the windward gauge behind Spain. Over six days of running skirmishes past Plymouth, Portland Bill, and the Isle of Wight, agile English ships avoided grappling hooks and fired stand-off cannon salvos. Although few Spanish ships sank in the Channel, Howard blocked Medina Sidonia from anchoring in the Solent and herded the Armada to exposed Calais Roads on 6 August.

STAGE 2 Calais Roads & The Fireships 7–8 AUG 1588
“How did eight midnight fireships at Calais shatter the Spanish fleet?”

Anchored off exposed Calais Roads on 6 August, the Duke of Medina Sidonia received bad news. Parma’s army at Dunkirk needed another week to embark and lacked warships to clear Dutch blockaders. Around midnight on 7 August 1588, Howard and Drake filled eight blazing fireships with pitch, brimstone, and gunpowder and drifted them toward Spain’s fleet. Fearing giant explosive ‘hell-burners’ like those used at Antwerp, panicked Spanish captains cut anchor cables in the darkness and scattered into the open North Sea. Although no Spanish galleon burned that night, the Armada lost its heavy main anchors and broke its tight crescent formation.

The Spanish Armada: Strategy, Conflict & Defeat — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 The Battle of Gravelines 8 AUG 1588
“How did race-built galleons and broadsides win at Gravelines?”

At dawn on 8 August 1588, the nine-hour Battle of Gravelines erupted off the Flemish sandbanks. Sailing fast, low race-built galleons (race-built warships) designed by John Hawkins, English squadrons closed within a hundred yards and out-turned tall Spanish ships. Meanwhile, shallow waters and Dutch flyboats kept Parma’s barges pinned ashore. Mounted on compact four-wheeled gun carriages, long-range English bronze culverins fired three times faster than clumsy Spanish guns manned by soldiers trained to fire once and board. Fierce English broadsides sank or wrecked five Spanish warships, inflicted over 1,000 casualties, and ruined any chance of invading Kent.

STAGE 4 The Winds & Atlantic Retreat AUG–SEPT 1588
“Why did gales and cut anchors wreck retreating ships off Ireland?”

Driven off the coast of Flanders (Calais and Dunkirk) after passing the Isle of Wight earlier, the battered Armada faced a strong south-westerly gale on 9 August. Hailed in England as the ‘Protestant Wind’, it blew Spain’s fleet into the North Sea. Short of shot and drinking foul water, Medina Sidonia had to sail north around the stormy coasts of Scotland and western Ireland. Fierce autumn storms turned Spain’s retreat along the Irish coast into disaster. Lacking the heavy anchors cut at Calais and misled by poor sea charts, over twenty-five Spanish ships wrecked on rocky Scottish and Irish shores, where thousands died. Barely 67 ships limped home to Spain, with over 15,000 men lost.

STAGE 5 Consequences of the English Victory AUG 1588–1589
“How did Tilbury and the Armada victory secure Elizabeth’s prestige?”

Even as the shattered Armada fled north on the ‘Protestant Wind’, England stayed braced against Parma’s army in Flanders. On 18 August 1588, Elizabeth rode in a silver breastplate before 4,000 militia mustered at Tilbury in Essex, declaring she possessed ‘the heart and stomach of a king, and of a king of England too’. Commemorative royal medals proclaimed Flavit Jehovah et Dissipati Sunt — ‘God blew and they were scattered’ — celebrating the English victory as proof of God’s favour toward Protestant England. Defeating the Armada through agile galleons, rapid gunnery, Calais fireships, and Atlantic gales preserved Dutch independence, boosted English naval prestige, and ended immediate invasion danger in 1588.
