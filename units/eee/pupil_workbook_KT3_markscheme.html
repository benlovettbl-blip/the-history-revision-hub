Education and Leisure, 1558–88
Enquiry Question (Page 2): How did educational expansion and popular theatrical culture reflect both social change and strict Tudor hierarchy?
Page 2 Word Bank (10 Core Terms): groundlings • grammar schools • Great Chain of Being • The Theatre • petty schools • Inns of Court • bear-baiting • James Burbage • dame schools • Oxford
Page 3 • Question 1: Describe One Feature [2 Marks] 1m Valid Feature + 1m Supporting Factual Detail (AO1: 2)
Describe one feature of sport and pastimes in early Elizabethan England.
Grade 9 Model Answer (2 / 2 Marks):

One feature of sport and pastimes in early Elizabethan England was sharp division by social class alongside shared blood sports. While nobles practised expensive pursuits like hunting deer, hawking, and real tennis, all social ranks attended Southwark arenas such as the Paris Garden to gamble on bear-baiting and cockfighting.

Page 3 • Question 3: Evaluative Essay [16 Marks — Zero SPaG] 16 Marks (0 SPaG)
‘The growth of the theatre was the most significant feature of sport, pastimes and the theatre in early Elizabethan England.’ How far do you agree? Explain your answer.
Stimulus bullets printed: • The theatre • Pastimes of the nobility and lower classes (+ mandatory own knowledge)
Edexcel Paper 2 (1HI0/B4) Q3/Q4 Evaluative Essay Tariff (16 Marks — AO1: 6, AO2: 10, ZERO SPaG): Level 1 (1–4m): Simple/generalised assertions • Level 2 (5–8m): Descriptive or one-sided explanation • Level 3 (9–12m): Balanced analysis for & against using own knowledge beyond the stimulus • Level 4 (13–16m): Sustained evaluative argument weighing factors against explicit criteria to reach a justified overall verdict.
Grade 9 Model Answer (16 / 16 Marks):

The dramatic rise of permanent commercial playhouses after 1576 was indeed the most innovative and culturally significant feature of Elizabethan leisure, even though traditional class-divided sports and blood sports continued to occupy the everyday lives of far more rural English people.

On the one hand, the transformation of the theatre between 1576 and 1588 revolutionized English entertainment. Whereas early Elizabethan strolling players performed in temporary inn-yards and risked arrest as vagabonds under the 1572 Act unless licensed by noble patrons such as the Earl of Leicester’s Men or the Queen’s Men (1583), actor James Burbage built England’s first purpose-built playhouse—‘The Theatre’ in Shoreditch—in 1576, swiftly followed by the Curtain (1577) and the Rose (1587). Uniquely, playhouses brought opposing social classes under one roof while preserving visible hierarchy: labourer ‘groundlings’ paid just one penny to stand in the open pit watching gripping secular dramas by Christopher Marlowe and Thomas Kyd, while gentry and nobles paid extra for cushioned seats in covered upper galleries, despite fierce Puritan opposition over plague and immorality.

On the other hand, traditional pastimes of the nobility and lower classes remained central to reinforcing the social order across every county outside London. Aristocratic leisure intentionally displayed wealth and chivalric exclusivity: peers maintained private deer parks for hunting on horseback, practised hawking and fencing, and competed in royal Accession Day tilts. Conversely, working labourers played rough, rule-free parish folk football where whole villages brawled across fields, often resulting in broken bones or deaths.

Furthermore, spectator blood sports and tavern pastimes were far more widespread nationally than London playgoing. Across towns and Southwark arenas like the Paris Garden, rich and poor alike gambled heavily on chained bear-baiting, bull-baiting, and cockfighting, while archery, dice, ballads, and alehouse drinking structured weekly parish life across rural shires.

Overall, I agree with the statement. Although blood sports and class-based country pastimes involved larger rural numbers and reinforced traditional hierarchy, they represented centuries-old continuity; the creation of permanent, affordable commercial theatres after 1576 was the defining new cultural breakthrough of Elizabeth’s reign.

The ‘Problem’ of the Poor, 1558–88
Enquiry Question (Page 4): Why did poverty and vagabondage surge, and why did Tudor policy shift from harsh punishment toward parish work relief?
Page 4 Word Bank (10 Core Terms): enclosure • sturdy beggars • 1572 Vagabonds Act • impotent poor • bad harvests • Bridewell • sheep farming • Thomas Harman • poor rate • inflation
Page 5 • Question 1: Describe One Feature [2 Marks] 1m Valid Feature + 1m Supporting Factual Detail (AO1: 2)
Describe one feature of policies towards the poor in early Elizabethan England.
Grade 9 Model Answer (2 / 2 Marks):

One feature of Elizabethan policies towards the poor was combining harsh physical punishment for idle vagrants with compulsory local taxation for genuine relief. Under the 1572 Vagabonds Act, JPs levied a compulsory parish poor rate to support the impotent poor while ordering unlicensed sturdy beggars to be whipped and bored through the ear with a hot iron.

Page 5 • Question 3: Evaluative Essay [16 Marks — Zero SPaG] 16 Marks (0 SPaG)
‘Economic pressures were the main reason for the increase in poverty and vagabondage in the years 1558–88.’ How far do you agree? Explain your answer.
Stimulus bullets printed: • Sheep farming and enclosure • Population growth (+ mandatory own knowledge)
Edexcel Paper 2 (1HI0/B4) Q3/Q4 Evaluative Essay Tariff (16 Marks — AO1: 6, AO2: 10, ZERO SPaG): Level 1 (1–4m): Simple/generalised assertions • Level 2 (5–8m): Descriptive or one-sided explanation • Level 3 (9–12m): Balanced analysis for & against using own knowledge beyond the stimulus • Level 4 (13–16m): Sustained evaluative argument weighing factors against explicit criteria to reach a justified overall verdict.
Grade 9 Model Answer (16 / 16 Marks):

Economic pressures—such as price inflation, falling real wages, cloth trade slumps, and agricultural enclosure for sheep farming—played a major role in impoverishing Elizabethan labourers, yet sustained demographic population growth was the underlying root catalyst that made every economic shock so severe between 1558 and 1588.

On the one hand, economic and agricultural pressures directly destroyed rural jobs and purchasing power. Because raw wool and cloth accounted for roughly 80% of England’s exports, profit-seeking landowners enclosed open village strip-fields with hedges and converted arable crop land into pasture for sheep farming, which required a tiny fraction of the workforce. Evicted tenant farmers and landless cottagers lost common grazing rights while rack-renting landlords sharply raised entry fines and rents. Simultaneously, sudden foreign trade disruptions—such as the Duke of Alba’s 1568 Antwerp cloth embargo—threw thousands of East Anglian and West Country spinners and weavers out of work overnight, while earlier Tudor coin debasement fuelled price inflation as real wages stagnated.

On the other hand, rapid population growth from roughly 3 million in 1551 to nearly 4 million by 1588 was the fundamental structural driver behind rising poverty and vagabondage. With hundreds of thousands more mouths to feed and stagnant farming yields, demand for grain and housing permanently outstripped supply—quadrupling bread prices whenever consecutive bad harvests struck in the mid-1550s, 1573–77, and 1586. Surplus rural youths unable to inherit land flooded into towns like London and Norwich looking for work, swelling the ranks of wandering ‘masterless men’ who terrified magistrates.

Furthermore, long-term institutional changes left destitute families without a safety net: Henry VIII’s earlier Dissolution of the Monasteries had abolished traditional church charity and hospital alms, forcing jobless families onto the roads until Parliament introduced compulsory poor rates in 1572 and wool work-stocks in 1576.

In conclusion, I agree to a large extent because inflation, rack-renting, cloth embargoes, and sheep enclosure were the immediate mechanisms that evicted tenants and crushed wages. However, those economic pressures were inseparable from relentless population growth: without a million extra people competing for scarce jobs, land, and grain, neither bad harvests nor enclosure would have produced mass vagabondage on such a national scale.

Enquiry Question (Page 6): How did navigational science, galleon engineering, and Drake’s circumnavigation turn England into an oceanic sea power?
Page 6 Word Bank (10 Core Terms): Mercator • culverins • astrolabe • Nova Albion • Muscovy Company • lateen sails • Strait of Magellan • quadrant • Northwest Passage • Antwerp
Page 7 • Question 1: Describe One Feature [2 Marks] 1m Valid Feature + 1m Supporting Factual Detail (AO1: 2)
Describe one feature of the impact of new technology on ships and sailing.
Grade 9 Model Answer (2 / 2 Marks):

One feature of the impact of new technology on sailing was accurate open-ocean navigation using brass astrolabes, quadrants, and Gerardus Mercator’s 1569 world map projection. By plotting true compass bearings as straight lines and measuring star latitude accurately, English captains could sail safely across vast oceans out of sight of coastlines.

Page 7 • Question 2: Explain Why [12 Marks] 12 Marks
Explain why Drake’s circumnavigation of the globe (1577–80) was significant.
Stimulus bullets printed: • Spanish treasure • England’s reputation as a sea power (+ mandatory own knowledge)
Edexcel Paper 2 (1HI0/B4) Q2 Explain Why Tariff (12 Marks — AO1: 6, AO2: 6): Level 1 (1–3m): Simple statements • Level 2 (4–6m): Descriptive or capped at 6m if confined to stimulus • Level 3 (7–9m): Explained causation with specific detail & own knowledge • Level 4 (10–12m): Sustained analytical explanation across 3 coherent reasons integrating accurate own knowledge beyond the two stimulus bullets.
Grade 9 Model Answer (12 / 12 Marks):

First, Drake’s circumnavigation (1577–80) was financially and commercially transformative because of the vast haul of captured Spanish treasure brought back to Plymouth in September 1580. Having raided Spanish Pacific ports like Valparaiso and captured the treasure galleon Cacafuego carrying £140,000 of silver and jewels alone, the Golden Hind returned roughly £400,000 in bullion—delivering a staggering 4,700% profit to court syndicate investors. Elizabeth’s personal royal share exceeded her entire annual Crown income, enabling her to pay off England’s foreign debt while encouraging wealthy London merchants to fund joint-stock companies such as the Levant Company (1581).

Second, the voyage dramatically elevated England’s reputation as a global sea power and inspired early colonial ambition. Despite losing four of his five ships, surviving violent gales in the Strait of Magellan, and crossing the uncharted Pacific and Indian Oceans, Drake returned as only the second captain in history—and the first Englishman—to sail around the world. Furthermore, in June 1579 Drake landed on the Californian coast and claimed ‘Nova Albion’ (New England) for Queen Elizabeth, demonstrating that Philip II’s Pacific monopoly could be breached and inspiring Sir Walter Raleigh’s subsequent Virginia expeditions.

Finally, the circumnavigation had major diplomatic significance by sharply escalating Anglo-Spanish hostility toward war. Furious at English plunder in waters claimed exclusively by Spain, ambassador Mendoza demanded Drake’s execution as a pirate; instead, Elizabeth publicly boarded the Golden Hind at Deptford in April 1581 and knighted Drake on deck. This calculated royal defiance humiliated Philip II, proved Elizabeth backed maritime aggression against Spain’s empire, and deepened the strategic rivalry that culminated in war in 1585.

Page 11 • Question 1: Describe One Feature [2 Marks] 1m Valid Feature + 1m Supporting Factual Detail (AO1: 2)
Describe one feature of the significance of Raleigh in the attempted colonisation of Virginia.
Grade 9 Model Answer (2 / 2 Marks):

One feature of Raleigh’s significance was masterminding, funding, and promoting the entire Virginia venture despite Elizabeth forbidding him to sail in person. Armed with his 1584 royal patent, Raleigh sent Barlowe’s reconnaissance voyage, named ‘Virginia’ to honour the Queen, commissioned Richard Hakluyt’s propaganda, and recruited court investors and colonists.

Page 11 • Question 3: Evaluative Essay [16 Marks — Zero SPaG] 16 Marks (0 SPaG)
‘The drive to expand trade was the main reason for the attempted colonisation of Virginia.’ How far do you agree? Explain your answer.
Stimulus bullets printed: • The significance of Raleigh • Rivalry with Spain (+ mandatory own knowledge)
Edexcel Paper 2 (1HI0/B4) Q3/Q4 Evaluative Essay Tariff (16 Marks — AO1: 6, AO2: 10, ZERO SPaG): Level 1 (1–4m): Simple/generalised assertions • Level 2 (5–8m): Descriptive or one-sided explanation • Level 3 (9–12m): Balanced analysis for & against using own knowledge beyond the stimulus • Level 4 (13–16m): Sustained evaluative argument weighing factors against explicit criteria to reach a justified overall verdict.
Grade 9 Model Answer (16 / 16 Marks):

The drive to expand trade and acquire raw materials untied to European rivals was a powerful economic motive behind the attempted colonisation of Virginia in the 1580s, yet strategic rivalry with Catholic Spain—championed by Sir Walter Raleigh and Richard Hakluyt—was equally crucial in winning royal backing from Queen Elizabeth.

On the one hand, commercial pressures strongly encouraged English merchants and courtiers to establish an American colony. Since the collapse of the Antwerp cloth market and Spanish trade embargoes had exposed England’s dangerous over-reliance on European wool exports, geographer Richard Hakluyt’s 1584 Discourse on Western Planting argued that North America offered both a captive market for English woollens and a direct source of exotic commodities—sugar, wine, naval timber, pitch, hemp, and hoped-for gold or copper—without paying high duties to Spanish, Mediterranean, or Baltic middlemen. Colonisation also promised employment for England’s growing population of landless poor while friendly Algonquian trade scouted in 1584 suggested rich commercial rewards.

On the other hand, geopolitical and religious rivalry with Philip II’s Spain gave Virginia urgent strategic purpose by 1584–85. As Anglo-Spanish tension slid toward open war under the Treaty of Nonsuch (1585), Elizabeth and Walsingham backed Virginia primarily because Roanoke offered a permanent North American naval base from which English privateers could ambush Spanish treasure fleets sailing up the Florida Channel from the Caribbean—draining Philip II’s war chest while challenging Catholic domination of the New World.

Finally, neither commercial nor anti-Spanish motives would have produced an expedition in 1584–87 without the personal ambition and organisation of Sir Walter Raleigh. Granted a six-year royal patent by Elizabeth in March 1584 after his half-brother Humphrey Gilbert drowned, Raleigh invested £40,000 of his own and court backers’ wealth, flattered Elizabeth by naming the territory ‘Virginia’, brought Manteo and Wanchese to London so Thomas Harriot could learn Algonquian, and organised both the 1585 Grenville/Lane military venture and John White’s 1587 family colony.

In conclusion, I agree to a substantial extent, because long-term trade independence from Europe underpinned merchant investment and Hakluyt’s blueprint. However, trade cannot be separated from anti-Spanish strategy: in the crisis years of 1584–88, Elizabeth approved Raleigh’s venture above all because Virginia promised to enrich English commerce while simultaneously striking at Spain’s Atlantic treasure lifeline.

Pages 14–15 • Question 2: Explain Why [12 Marks] (Unscaffolded) 12 Marks (AO1: 6 + AO2: 6)
2  Explain why the first settlement in Virginia failed.
Stimulus bullets printed: • Supplies and the voyage • Relations with Native Americans (Candidates must also include own knowledge beyond the stimulus to progress above Level 2 / 6 marks.)
Level 4 (10–12 Marks): Three analytical causation paragraphs consistently directed at the conceptual focus of the question, supported by precise dates/terminology and own knowledge beyond the two stimulus bullets.
Grade 9 Model Answer (12 / 12 Marks):

First, disastrous problems with the 1585 voyage and lost supplies crippled the Roanoke settlement from the moment it reached North America. Setting sail from Plymouth in April 1585—already too late in the agricultural year to plant spring crops—flagship Tiger struck a shallow sandbar at Ocracoke Inlet on 29 June 1585. Flooding seawater ruined almost all the expedition’s seed corn, salt, and gunpowder, leaving the settlers unable to grow harvests for the coming winter or preserve meat and fish.

Second, flawed recruitment and weak colonial planning compounded these supply shortages. Sir Walter Raleigh’s 107 all-male colonists lacked essential farming families, stone-masons, and fishermen: roughly half were soldiers and status-conscious gentlemen expecting easy gold or Native labour rather than manual agricultural toil, while military governor Ralph Lane clashed bitterly with naval commander Sir Richard Grenville. Without seed grain or farming skills, the garrison could never achieve self-sufficiency before Grenville sailed back to England for resupply.

Finally, deteriorating relations with local Algonquian Native Americans destroyed the settlers’ only remaining source of food and forced total evacuation in June 1586. Initially reliant on Chief Wingina’s Secotan people for corn and fish traps, Grenville torched the village of Aquascogoc over a missing silver cup, and as winter hunger bit, Wingina (renamed Pemisapan) refused to hand over scarce tribal grain stores and planned resistance. When Ralph Lane launched a preemptive ambush on 1 June 1586 and beheaded Wingina, surrounding tribes cut off all food supplies; facing starvation and retaliation, the surviving colonists abandoned Virginia aboard Francis Drake’s passing fleet on 18 June 1586.
