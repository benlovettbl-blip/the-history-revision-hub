Born amid military collapse and hunger in November 1918, Germany’s new Weimar Republic faced immediate "stab-in-the-back" myths, the hated Treaty of Versailles, armed uprisings from Left and Right, and the 1923 Ruhr invasion and hyperinflation. Assess how Gustav Stresemann stabilised currency and foreign relations (1924–29) and how far Weimar’s cultural "Golden Age" masked fragile foundations.

KEY HISTORICAL INDIVIDUALS & ACTORS
Friedrich Ebert (1871–1925) First Weimar President

Moderate SPD leader who signed the Ebert-Groener Pact with the army and used Freikorps to defeat communist uprisings.

Rosa Luxemburg & Karl Liebknecht Spartacist Leaders

Revolutionary communists who led the January 1919 Berlin rising before being captured and murdered by Freikorps officers.

Wolfgang Kapp (1858–1922) Right-Wing Putschist

Nationalist civil servant backed by General Lüttwitz’s Freikorps in March 1920 until Berlin workers paralysed the city.

Gustav Stresemann (1878–1929) Chancellor & Foreign Minister

Architect of Weimar’s 1923–29 recovery (Rentenmark, Dawes/Young Plans, Locarno, League); died weeks before the Wall Street Crash.

Paul von Hindenburg (1847–1934) President from 1925

Revered monarchist WWI Field Marshal whose 1925 election reassured conservatives initially, though he distrusted parliamentary rule.

Otto Dix, George Grosz & Fritz Lang Weimar Cultural Pioneers

Led Neue Sachlichkeit ("New Objectivity") painting and Expressionist cinema (Metropolis, 1927), denounced by Nazis as "degenerate".

ESSENTIAL DISCIPLINARY CONCEPTS & TERMINOLOGY
Dolchstoss ("Stab in the Back") Nationalist myth claiming the unbeaten German army was betrayed in 1918 by socialists, democrats, and Jews.
Proportional Representation & Art. 48 Voting system splintering the Reichstag into 28+ parties, tempting the President to rule by emergency decree.
Freikorps Heavily armed paramilitary brigades of demobilised, fiercely anti-communist WWI veterans.
Hyperinflation (1923) Runaway money-printing that wiped out middle-class life savings and fixed pensions while debtors paid off loans cheaply.
Rentenmark (Nov 1923) Strictly limited temporary currency backed by German agricultural land and industrial mortgages.
Dawes (1924) & Young (1929) Plans US-brokered agreements scaling reparations to economic capacity and injecting short-term American loans.
Locarno Treaties (1925) Stresemann’s guarantee of Germany’s western borders (unlike Versailles, negotiated voluntarily on equal terms).
Neue Frau ("New Woman") Urban 1920s women voting, entering professions, and wearing bobbed hair—resented by traditional conservatives.

KEY ENQUIRY “How did defeat in 1918 create the Weimar Republic—and weaken it from birth?”
STAGE 1 Mutiny on the High Seas: The Kiel Sailors' Revolt and Imperial Collapse 1918
“How did naval mutiny and civilian starvation force military leaders to surrender?”

By autumn 1918, Imperial Germany faced military collapse on the Western Front and bitter discontent at home. Four hard years of the British Allied naval blockade had cut vital food and fuel imports, causing Turnip Winter starvation that killed over 750,000 German civilians. When admirals ordered a suicidal final battle against the British fleet, exhausted sailors refused to sail. Open revolt erupted in the Kiel naval mutiny on 3 November 1918, as forty thousand armed sailors and dockworkers seized naval bases and raised red socialist banners. Within days, mass strikes and political unrest swept across German cities as workers' and soldiers' councils seized town halls, proving imperial leaders had lost control of the armed forces and sparking revolution.

STAGE 2 Flight to the Border: The Abdication of Kaiser Wilhelm II and the Birth of the Republic 9 Nov 1918
“Why did Scheidemann rush to proclaim a republic on 9 November 1918?”

On 9 November 1918, general strikes brought central Berlin to a standstill as crowds surrounded government ministries. At army headquarters in Spa, Field Marshal Hindenburg warned the Emperor that soldiers would no longer obey his orders. That warning forced Kaiser Wilhelm's abdication, ending five centuries of Hohenzollern rule. Wilhelm slipped into Dutch border exile aboard a royal train next morning. Meanwhile in Berlin, keen to stop communists declaring a Soviet republic first, Social Democratic leader Philipp Scheidemann proclaimed the new German Republic from a Reichstag window. He handed provisional power to SPD Chancellor Friedrich Ebert to restore order.

STAGE 3 The Armistice & The Ebert-Groener Pact: Forging a Secret Alliance with the Old Army Nov 1918
“Why did Ebert form a secret pact with the Imperial Army leadership?”

Taking power amid street fighting in Berlin, Social Democratic Chancellor Friedrich Ebert moved fast to steady the state. On 10 November 1918, Ebert struck the telephone Ebert-Groener army pact with General Wilhelm Groener of the Imperial Army. The officer corps promised to keep order and crush communist risings if Ebert protected army independence from radical reform. Next morning, civilian politician Matthias Erzberger signed the Compiègne armistice in a railway carriage, ending the First World War. While peace returned at once, generals like Hindenburg and Ludendorff dodged blame for defeat, leaving right-wing nationalists to brand civilian republican leaders the "November Criminals".

STAGE 4 Flight from Berlin: The National Assembly Convened in Weimar Jan–Feb 1919
“Why was the constitutional assembly held in Weimar rather than Berlin?”

In January 1919, nationwide elections drew an eighty-three percent voter turnout across Germany. Moderate pro-republican parties won a clear majority, forming a stable democratic coalition government uniting the SPD, the Catholic Centre Party, and the German Democratic Party. Delegates then elected moderate socialist Friedrich Ebert first President of the new German Republic. Because continuing street warfare and communist strikes made Berlin too dangerous for politicians, deputies met 150 miles south at the Weimar National Assembly in February 1919. Inside the National Theatre, delegates drafted a democratic constitution linked to the peaceful culture of Goethe and Schiller rather than Prussian militarism.

STAGE 5 The Weimar Constitution: Progressive Universal Suffrage and Fatal Emergency Powers Aug 1919
“Why did proportional representation and Article 48 weaken Weimar?”

Signed into law on 11 August 1919, the Weimar Constitution created one of the freest democracies in the world. It protected free speech, assembly, and worship while granting universal suffrage rights to all men and women aged twenty and over. Ordinary citizens gained equal votes across Germany's eighteen federal states. However, two deep flaws weakened its stability. Under proportional representation, small fringe parties multiplied in parliament, making single-party majorities impossible and causing unstable coalitions. Worst of all, Article 48 emergency powers let the President suspend civil rights and rule by decree without Reichstag consent — a loophole future dictators exploited.

KEY ENQUIRY “How did the Weimar Republic survive Versailles, armed revolts, and hyperinflation by 1923?”
STAGE 1 Red Flags in Berlin: The Spartacist Uprising and the Freikorps Bloodbath Jan 1919
“Why did the government rely on right-wing paramilitaries to crush the Spartacists?”

In January 1919, radical left-wing revolutionaries launched an armed rising against Ebert's republican government. Led by Karl Liebknecht and Rosa Luxemburg, the Spartacist Communist uprising brought over one hundred thousand armed workers onto Berlin's streets. Rebels seized rail junctions, newspaper offices, and ministries, hoping to topple Ebert and set up workers' councils. Lacking loyal police, Defence Minister Gustav Noske ordered Freikorps volunteer units of demobilized imperial soldiers into Berlin with machine guns and flamethrowers. Over one hundred workers died in street fighting, and right-wing soldiers beat and murdered Rosa Luxemburg alongside Liebknecht, leaving a permanent rift between Social Democrats and Communists.

STAGE 2 The Diktat of Versailles: War Guilt, Reparations and the 'Stab in the Back' Legend Jun 1919
“How did the Versailles Treaty foster deep nationalist hatred of the Republic?”

On 28 June 1919, threats of renewed Allied invasion forced German envoys to sign the Treaty of Versailles. Hated across Germany as a humiliating Diktat (dictated peace), its harsh terms became a primary cause of the early unpopularity of the Republic. Under Article 231 War Guilt, Germany had to accept sole blame for the war and pay £6.6 billion reparations. Germany also lost thirteen percent of its European territory and all overseas colonies, had to demilitarise the Rhineland, and saw its army capped at 100,000 volunteers without tanks or aircraft. Right-wing nationalists blamed democratic leaders for signing and spread the 'stab in the back' theory (Dolchstoss), falsely claiming socialists and Jews had betrayed an unbeaten German army at home.

STAGE 3 The Ehrhardt Brigade Marches: The Kapp Putsch and the General Strike Mar 1920
“Why did the army refuse to defend Ebert against the right-wing putsch?”

In March 1920, anger erupted on the far right when the government tried to disband the Freikorps under Versailles rules. Nationalists launched the Wolfgang Kapp putsch, marching five thousand armed troops into Berlin and seizing ministries. Ebert and his cabinet fled south to Stuttgart to escape arrest. Ebert ordered regular soldiers to crush the rebels, yet army chief General von Seeckt refused: "Reichswehr does not fire upon Reichswehr." Instead, trade unions rescued the republic by calling a Berlin general strike. It shut off gas, water, and trams, defeating the putsch in four days.

STAGE 4 Bullets from the Shadows: The Assassinations of Erzberger and Rathenau 1921–1922
“How did judicial bias protect right-wing nationalist assassins?”

Defeated in open coups after January 1919, far-right extremists turned to political murder through secret Organisation Consul hit-squads. In August 1921, nationalist gunmen assassinated Matthias Erzberger in the Black Forest, hating him as a "traitor" for signing the 1918 surrender and raising taxes on the rich. In June 1922, Foreign Minister Walther Rathenau was murdered with submachine guns and hand grenades in his open car in Berlin. Anti-Semitic nationalists despised this wealthy Jewish minister for seeking peace with the West. Over 350 political assassinations took place between 1919 and 1922, yet right-wing judges gave nationalist killers light sentences.

STAGE 5 French Troops in the Ruhr & The Mark Collapses: The Nightmare of Hyperinflation 1923
“How did passive resistance in the Ruhr trigger runaway hyperinflation?”

In January 1923, Germany failed to pay its scheduled £6.6 billion reparations in coal and timber. In response, sixty thousand French and Belgian troops carried out the Ruhr occupation. Chancellor Wilhelm Cuno ordered passive resistance, telling Ruhr miners and rail workers to strike while the government printed billions of paper marks. Reckless money-printing triggered runaway hyperinflation, making the mark worthless by autumn 1923 when one loaf cost 201 billion marks. Shoppers pushed wheelbarrows of cash for bread, while the middle classes saw their life savings and fixed pensions wiped out, shattering faith in Weimar democracy.

KEY ENQUIRY “Did Gustav Stresemann truly solve Weimar Germany’s problems between 1924 and 1929?”
STAGE 1 Burning the Trillion-Mark Notes: Stresemann and the Miracle of the Rentenmark Nov 1923
“How did the Rentenmark restore basic confidence in the monetary system?”

In August 1923, at the height of the inflation crisis, moderate statesman Gustav Stresemann became Chancellor of a broad "Great Coalition". Acting boldly, Stresemann called off passive resistance in the occupied Ruhr to stop ruinous state payouts. He also cut civil service jobs and government spending across ministries to balance the budget. In November 1923, Stresemann introduced the temporary Rentenmark, a strictly limited currency backed by German agricultural and industrial land values. Public trust returned at once, and in 1924 the Reichsbank stabilized the currency under the permanent Reichsmark, ending hyperinflation and launching five years of recovery.

STAGE 2 Wall Street Gold: The Dawes Plan and the Influx of American Loans 1924
“Why did American loans provide temporary relief but leave Germany vulnerable?”

Following a pragmatic policy of Erfüllungspolitik (fulfilment) to drive economic recovery, Stresemann agreed the 1924 Dawes Plan with Allied banker Charles Dawes while leaving final debt cuts to the later Young Plan. Dawes lowered annual reparations to an affordable one billion gold marks at first, rising over five years only in step with German industrial output and tax growth. Crucially, the agreement unlocked 800 million gold marks (800m) in Wall Street credit alongside vital American loans and investment across German industry. Foreign capital built modern factories, municipal railways, power stations, and public housing, cutting unemployment and boosting exports. Yet this reliance created a dangerous weakness, tying German prosperity directly to the health of American banks.

STAGE 3 The Spirit of Reconciliation: The Locarno Treaties and Demilitarising the Rhineland 1925
“How did the Locarno Treaties break Germany’s post-war diplomatic isolation?”

Seeking peaceful reconciliation abroad, Foreign Minister Gustav Stresemann signed the Locarno Pact (Locarno Treaties) in October 1925 alongside Britain, France, Belgium, and Italy. Unlike the hated dictated peace of Versailles, Germany signed voluntarily as an equal partner, recognizing its western borders as permanent and formally giving up claims to Alsace-Lorraine for France and Eupen-Malmedy for Belgium. In return, France promised never to invade the industrial Ruhr again, while all powers backed permanent Rhineland demilitarization to safeguard European peace. This warm "Spirit of Locarno" proved decisive in gaining international acceptance of Germany abroad after years of bitter post-war isolation, reassuring Western democracies of Weimar's peaceful aims and securing Allied troop withdrawals from Cologne.

STAGE 4 Return to the World Stage: Germany Joins the League of Nations Sep 1926
“Why was admission to the League of Nations a major victory for Stresemann?”

A major diplomatic triumph followed in September 1926, when Stresemann won Germany's official League of Nations admission, ending post-war isolation. Better still, Germany immediately gained a permanent seat on the League of Nations Council in Geneva alongside Britain and France, restoring equal voting power as a great power. Using this restored standing, Stresemann spoke up peacefully for German minorities in Eastern Europe, negotiated early Allied troop withdrawals from the Rhineland, and laid groundwork for the Young Plan. In December 1926, he shared the Nobel Peace Prize with French Foreign Minister Aristide Briand.

STAGE 5 Dancing on a Volcano: The Kellogg-Briand Pact, the Young Plan and Hidden Vulnerabilities 1928–1929
“Why did right-wing nationalists fiercely oppose the Young Plan?”

In August 1928, Germany and sixty-four nations signed the Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928), renouncing war as an instrument of national policy. Building on this goodwill, Stresemann negotiated the Young Plan (1929), reducing total reparations from £6.6 billion to £2 billion (£2bn) and spreading payments over fifty-nine years until 1988. Despite these major cuts and lower taxes, right-wing nationalists led by press baron Alfred Hugenberg and Adolf Hitler campaigned fiercely against the plan. Weeks before the October 1929 crash, Stresemann died of a stroke after warning: "Germany is dancing on a volcano."

KEY ENQUIRY “How far did everyday life and culture improve in Weimar’s ‘Golden Twenties’?”
STAGE 1 Wages, Tenements and Pensions: Standard of Living and Unemployment Insurance in the Twenties 1924–1928
“How did welfare legislation and real wage growth raise living standards?”

Between 1924 and 1928, Weimar Germany improved social welfare and workers' living standards. As factory output rebounded after currency reform, real wages rose by over 10% (reaching 25% gains by 1928) alongside a maximum eight-hour working day. Even so, debt-hit farmers and the middle classes felt left behind. In 1927, parliament passed the Unemployment Insurance Act, using 3% wage contributions to guarantee weekly jobless benefits for over seventeen million workers. Meanwhile, town councils built over 2 million modern homes fitted with indoor plumbing and electricity, easing city housing shortages across Germany.

STAGE 2 The Bubikopf and the Vote: The Emancipation and Clashes of the 'New Woman' 1924–1929
“Why did urban women experience new freedoms while traditional roles persisted?”

Weimar Germany brought major changes in the position of women in work, politics, and leisure under Article 109 of the 1919 Constitution, which guaranteed equal voting rights and civil service access. High female turnout elected dozens of female Reichstag deputies, giving Weimar Germany a higher share of female lawmakers than Britain or the United States during the 1920s. In big cities, young wage-earning women embraced the image of the 'New Woman' (Neue Frau) across office, retail, medical, and teaching jobs, although married women still faced lower pay and traditional pressures. Enjoying independent urban leisure, young women wore short Bubikopf bobbed hair, cosmetics, and shorter skirts while visiting cafés, dance halls, and cinemas unchaperoned, angering rural conservatives.

STAGE 3 Glass, Steel and Functional Design: Walter Gropius and the Bauhaus Revolution 1925–1929
“How did the Bauhaus school embody modern functionalist design?”

Weimar Germany also stood at the heart of modernist art and design. In 1919, visionary architect Walter Gropius founded the Bauhaus school in Weimar. Bridging fine art with modern factory craft, Gropius taught young designers that everyday form must follow practical, everyday function. Rejecting heavy nineteenth-century carving, Bauhaus designers used clean geometric lines, steel frames, reinforced concrete, and wide glass curtain walls. Moving into its famous Dessau campus in 1925, its steel-tube chairs, clean lettering, and bright flats shaped modern architecture across Europe.

STAGE 4 Neue Sachlichkeit & Weimar Cinema: Otto Dix, The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari and Modernist Irony 1924–1929
“Why did Neue Sachlichkeit painters reject traditional romantic art?”

Disillusioned by First World War slaughter and harsh city poverty, German painters pioneered New Objectivity (Neue Sachlichkeit) across major cities in the 1920s. Rather than romanticizing German life, avant-garde artists used blunt realism and sharp irony to critique Weimar society in unsparing detail. Leading painters Otto Dix and George Grosz drove this bold movement. Dix painted mutilated trench amputees and wartime trauma, while Grosz mocked corrupt industrialists and greedy generals in Berlin streets, making Weimar art the sharpest social commentary in Europe.

STAGE 5 The Conservative Backlash: Degeneracy Accusations and the Cultural Fault Lines of the Republic 1924–1929
“Why did conservative Germans view Berlin culture as degenerate?”

Weimar cinema won world fame too, led by the giant UFA film studios in Babelsberg outside Berlin. Directors pushed camera and lighting design in landmark expressionist films like Fritz Lang's sci-fi epic Metropolis (1927), The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari, and Marlene Dietrich's cabaret drama The Blue Angel. However, Berlin's bold cabaret nightlife, sexual openness, and avant-garde art sparked a fierce conservative cultural backlash across rural towns and churches. Right-wing nationalists denounced Weimar culture as un-German, decadent, and morally degenerate, claiming democracy was poisoning traditional German values.
