LESSON 1 • Evidence & Everyday Life

Celtic Britain Before the Romans (c. 500 BC – AD 43)

Key Enquiry: Who were the Iron Age Celts, and how did they live before the Roman legions arrived?
[1.1] Tribes, Warriors & Giant Earth Hillforts

Long before Roman soldiers marched into Britain, the island was home to Iron Age Celts. Britain was not one united country with a single ruler. Instead, dozens of proud tribes ruled their own lands, including the Iceni in the east, the Catuvellauni in the south, and the Brigantes in the north.

Rival tribes sometimes raided neighbours for cattle, so families built giant hillforts like Maiden Castle in Dorset, ringed with steep earth ramparts and wooden fences. When the Roman general Julius Caesar landed in 55 BC and 54 BC, fierce Celtic warriors in rattling chariots fought bravely before he sailed back to Gaul.

[1.2] Inside a Smoky Thatched Roundhouse

Most Celtic families were hardworking farmers who lived inside circular homes called roundhouses. Builders wove hazel branches into wattle-and-daub walls plastered with sticky clay, mud, and straw. Overhead rose a tall cone-shaped thatched roof of reeds or straw, cleverly built with no chimney so howling winds could not blow sparks inside.

Smoke from the glowing central hearth fire drifted slowly through the thatch, curing meat hung in the rafters and driving away biting insects. Inside the warm, dim room, parents wove brightly patterned woollen cloaks on tall wooden looms, while children turned heavy stone quern stones to grind wheat into flour.

[1.3] Master Craftworkers, Druids & Sea Traders

Far from being uncivilised, the Celts were dazzling metalworkers and wise storytellers. Smiths twisted ropes of pure gold into heavy neck rings called torcs, and decorated bronze shields with swirling red glass before offering them to water gods in sacred rivers. Religious leaders called Druids never wrote books, instead memorising thousands of laws, poems, and prayers.

Celtic Britons were also busy international sea traders. Ships crossed the English Channel carrying Cornish tin, iron, cattle hides, hunting dogs, and golden wheat to mainland Europe. Wealthy tribal rulers even minted shiny gold stater coins, bringing news of Britain's rich treasures straight to greedy ears in Rome.

LESSON 2 • Cause & Consequence

Why Did Emperor Claudius Invade Britain in AD 43?

Key Enquiry: Why did Emperor Claudius send 40,000 Roman soldiers and war elephants across the sea in AD 43?
[2.1] Britain's Riches & Claudius's Need for Glory

Nearly ninety years after Julius Caesar sailed away, a nervous new ruler named Emperor Claudius took power in Rome in AD 41. Powerful Roman senators doubted Claudius because he walked with a limp, spoke with a stutter, and had never led an army. Claudius desperately needed a spectacular military triumph to win respect.

Conquering misty island Britain promised both military glory and dazzling wealth. Rome wanted Britain's underground mines of silver, Cornish tin, lead, and iron to make coins, water pipes, and weapons. Fertile southern farms also grew abundant wheat to feed soldiers, while conquered tribes would have to pay heavy Roman taxes.

[2.2] The Unstoppable Roman War Machine

Unlike Celtic farmers who fought part-time, Roman legionaries were full-time professional soldiers. Wearing hobnailed leather sandals called caligae, they marched 20 miles a day carrying heavy packs and flexible iron strip armour called lorica segmentata. When arrows rained down, soldiers locked their curved rectangular scutum shields overhead and in front like a giant testudo ("tortoise") shell.

Every legionary trained daily with deadly teamwork. First, soldiers hurled a heavy iron-tipped javelin called a pilum, cleverly designed to bend on impact so enemies could not throw it back. Then they stepped close behind their shield wall, using a short stabbing sword called a gladius with calm, unstoppable discipline.

[2.3] Landing at Richborough & Elephants at Camulodunum

In AD 43, General Aulus Plautius sailed across the Channel with four legions and auxiliary troops — around 40,000 soldiers in total. Landing at Richborough in Kent, the Romans built strong defensive ditches and marched inland. After a fierce two-day battle at the River Medway, they defeated the brave Celtic leader Caratacus.

Plautius halted near the River Thames and sent word to Rome. Emperor Claudius arrived in person bringing towering war elephants that terrified Celtic horses with their strange smell and trumpeting roars. Claudius marched triumphantly into the tribal capital Camulodunum (Colchester), winning eleven kings' surrender and turning southern Britain into a Roman province.

LESSON 3 • Cause, Consequence & Perspectives

Boudicca's Rebellion — Why Did the Iceni Rise Up? (AD 60–61)

Key Enquiry: Why did Queen Boudicca lead a huge rebellion against Rome in AD 60, and why did it fail?
[3.1] Broken Roman Promises to the Iceni

At first, Rome let some friendly British rulers keep their thrones in peace. In East Anglia, King Prasutagus of the Iceni tribe stayed an ally of Rome. When Prasutagus died in AD 60, he left half his wealth to Emperor Nero and half to his two daughters, trusting Rome to protect his family.

Greedy Roman officials cruelly broke that promise. Soldiers marched into Iceni lands, seized noble families' farms, and demanded that heavy Roman loans and taxes be paid back at once. When Queen Boudicca protested against this injustice, Roman officers had her flogged with whips and mistreated her daughters, sparking fury across East Anglia.

[3.2] Three Roman Towns Burn to Red Clay

Riding tall in her wooden war chariot, Queen Boudicca united the Iceni, the neighbouring Trinovantes, and other angry tribes. Better still for the rebels, Roman Governor Suetonius Paulinus was marching hundreds of miles away in Anglesey in north Wales. Boudicca struck first at Camulodunum (Colchester), burning the hated Roman temple to ashes.

Next, Boudicca's huge rebel army swept south and torched the bustling port of Londinium (London) before destroying Verulamium (St Albans). Timber shops and clay-walled houses burned so fiercely that they baked into a scorching red clay "destruction layer". Even today, archaeologists digging deep beneath modern London streets still uncover blackened coins and melted pottery!

[3.3] Showdown at the Battle of Watling Street (AD 61)

In AD 61, Governor Suetonius Paulinus raced back along the Roman road called Watling Street with 10,000 disciplined legionaries. Although heavily outnumbered, Suetonius cleverly chose a narrow wooded valley that protected his sides. Confident of victory, Iceni families parked a half-circle of wooden wagons behind the battlefield to watch.

When the Britons charged, the narrow funnel squashed Boudicca's warriors together so they could barely swing their long swords. Advancing in tight wedges, Roman soldiers hurled javelins and pushed the rebels backward until they were trapped against their own ring of wagons. Rome won, Boudicca died soon after, and Londinium was rebuilt inside mighty stone walls.

LESSON 4 • Evidence & Diversity

Hadrian's Wall & Diverse Soldiers on the Frontier (AD 122–400)

Key Enquiry: Why did Emperor Hadrian build a 73-mile stone wall across northern Britain, and who guarded it?
[4.1] 73 Miles of Stone From Sea to Sea (AD 122)

In AD 122, Emperor Hadrian visited Britain and ordered his legions to build a mighty stone frontier wall across the north. Stretching 73 miles (80 Roman miles) from Wallsend on the River Tyne to Bowness-on-Solway, Hadrian's Wall separated Roman Britain from Caledonia (modern Scotland) and showed Rome's power from sea to sea.

At every Roman mile, soldiers built a guarded gatehouse called a milecastle to stop surprise cattle raids and collect taxes from peaceful traders crossing the border. Two stone watchtowers stood between each milecastle, backed by 16 large forts such as Housesteads, where flowing rainwater channels flushed communal multi-seat stone latrines.

[4.2] Postcards in the Mud: The Vindolanda Tablets

Just south of the Wall stood Vindolanda Fort, where deep, wet, oxygen-free mud stopped buried wood and leather from rotting away. Archaeologists dug up hundreds of wafer-thin wooden ink tablets preserved underground since around AD 100, capturing real family voices from the chilly northern frontier.

On one famous tablet, a fort commander's wife named Claudia Severa warmly invited her dearest friend Sulpicia Lepidina to her birthday party on 11 September — the oldest surviving Latin handwriting by a woman! Another tablet lists a parcel sent to keep a cold soldier cosy: warm woollen socks, two pairs of sandals, and two pairs of underpants.

[4.3] Soldiers & Families From Across the Roman Empire

Hadrian's Wall was guarded by auxiliary regiments recruited from three continents — Europe, the Middle East, and North Africa. Batavian troops from the Netherlands served at Vindolanda, Syrian archers watched the hills from Carvoran, and North African Aurelian Moors stood guard near the western coast at Burgh-by-Sands.

Bustling civilian settlements called vici grew outside the fort gates where soldiers' families, craftspeople, and traders lived together. At Arbeia Fort (South Shields), a Syrian flag-trader named Barates married Regina, a freedwoman from the British Catuvellauni tribe, while another carved tombstone honours Victor, a young freedman from Mauretania in North Africa.

LESSON 5 • Change & Continuity

Life in Roman Towns — Roads, Baths & Villas

Key Enquiry: How did Roman roads, bathhouses, and stone towns transform everyday life in Britain?
[5.1] Straight Stone Roads & Bustling Market Forums

Before Rome arrived, Britons travelled slowly along muddy dirt tracks. Roman surveyors used a wooden sighting tool called a groma to lay 10,000 miles of dead-straight stone highways across Britain. Soldiers stacked rammed gravel and cambered (gently curved) paving stones between deep side ditches so rainwater drained quickly away and heavy carts moved in all weathers.

Where major highways met, Romans built planned grid towns protected by tall stone walls. At each town's heart stood the forum — a bustling open marketplace edged by busy shops and a grand town hall called a basilica — while oval stone amphitheatres like Caerleon in South Wales seated thousands of cheering spectators for gladiator shows.

[5.2] Aqueducts, Steam Baths & Underfloor Heating

Stone water channels called aqueducts and underground lead pipes carried fresh spring water straight into Roman towns to feed street fountains, flushing sewers, and public bathhouses like Aquae Sulis (modern Bath). Visiting the noisy baths was a favourite daily routine where townspeople went to exercise, chat with friends, and wash clean.

Bathhouses and rich homes used hypocaust underfloor central heating. Enslaved workers stoked a roaring wood furnace so hot air blew beneath floors raised on neat brick tile stacks called pilae. Bathers relaxed in warm and steaming hot rooms before scraping perfumed olive oil, sweat, and dirt from their skin with a curved bronze strigil.

[5.3] Dolphin Mosaics vs Countryside Roundhouses

In peaceful southern Britain, wealthy Roman officials and friendly Celtic tribal leaders built grand stone country villas like Fishbourne Palace in Sussex. Skilled craftspeople pressed thousands of tiny coloured stone cubes called tesserae into wet mortar to create mosaic floors, while diners feasted on new Roman foods including grapes, cherries, walnuts, and salty fish sauce called garum.

Yet Roman rule did not change everyone's home equally across the island. Out in the countryside, almost nine out of ten Britons carried on farming the land inside smoky thatched roundhouses, speaking Celtic languages while working hard to pay Roman taxes.

LESSON 6 • Historical Significance & Legacy

Farewell to the Legions — What Was Rome's Lasting Legacy? (AD 410 & Beyond)

Key Enquiry: Why did the Roman army leave Britain around AD 410, and what did the Romans leave behind for us today?
[6.1] Raiders on Every Coast & Leaving in AD 410

After nearly four centuries of rule, the mighty Roman Empire came under fierce attack everywhere at once. Around AD 400, Picts from Scotland, Scotti from Ireland, and seafaring Angles and Saxons from across the North Sea raided Britain's coasts and villages, while Visigoth armies invaded Italy and threatened Rome itself.

One by one, Roman legions marched away from Britain and sailed across the Channel to defend the heart of the Empire. When worried British leaders wrote begging for military help in AD 410, Emperor Honorius sent a famous reply warning that Rome had no spare soldiers left and British towns must defend themselves.

[6.2] Buried Silver Treasure & Crumbling Bathhouses

Once the Roman army left, fresh shipments of Roman coins stopped arriving to pay soldiers' wages. Busy town markets shrank, potters' workshops closed down, and nobody repaired leaky stone aqueducts, hypocausts, or public bathhouses, which slowly crumbled into silent, overgrown ruins.

Fearing coastal raiders and dangerous times, anxious wealthy families packed chests of gold coins, silver spoons, pepper pots, and heavy banquet plates underground as a secret hoard, hoping to dig them up when peace returned. Many owners never came back: centuries later, farmers ploughing fields in Suffolk unearthed glittering Roman treasures like the Hoxne Hoard and the Mildenhall Treasure.

[6.3] Roman Footprints Still Shaping Britain Today

Even though the legions marched away, Rome's lasting legacy still shapes Britain all around us today. Bustling Londinium grew into our capital city London, towns ending in -chester or -caster — like Manchester and Lancaster — mark old Roman forts (castra), and modern A-roads still follow dead-straight Roman highways.

Every single day we write using the Latin ABC alphabet, read Roman numerals (I, V, X) on clockfaces, and track the year with the 12-month calendar naming July (Julius Caesar) and August (Emperor Augustus) after Roman leaders. Rome even brought Christianity, written laws, sweet apples, cabbages, rabbits, and stinging nettles to Britain!

MASTER HISTORICAL VOCABULARY BANK 24 CORE ROMAN & CELTIC TERMS WITH SYLLABLES
Roundhouse Round-house
A circular Iron Age family home with woven clay walls, a central fire, and a tall cone-shaped thatched roof with no chimney.
Hillfort Hill-fort
A settlement built high on a hilltop and ringed with steep earth banks (ramparts), deep ditches, and wooden fences for protection.
Wattle and Daub Wat-tle and Daub
Woven strips of bendy hazel wood (wattle) plastered with a sticky mixture of clay, mud, animal dung, and straw (daub) to make warm walls.
Torc Torc
A heavy, twisted neck ring made of gold, silver, or bronze worn by wealthy Celtic rulers and warriors to show high status.
Emperor Em-per-or
The supreme ruler of the vast Roman Empire, commanding all Roman armies and provinces.
Legionary Le-gion-a-ry
A full-time, heavily armoured Roman citizen soldier trained to march 20 miles a day and fight in tight formations.
Testudo Tes-tu-do
Latin for "tortoise" — a battle formation where Roman soldiers locked shields in front and overhead to block enemy arrows.
Scutum Scu-tum
A tall, curved rectangular Roman shield made of layered wood, leather, and an iron boss in the centre.
Rebellion Re-bel-lion
An armed uprising where angry people fight back to overthrow the rulers controlling them.
Ally Al-ly
A friendly king, queen, or country that agrees to help and stay at peace with another power.
Chariot Char-i-ot
A fast, two-wheeled wooden war cart pulled by ponies, ridden into battle by Celtic leaders like Boudicca.
Destruction Layer De-struc-tion Lay-er
A band of scorched red clay, black ash, and melted objects buried underground where an ancient town burned down.
Frontier Fron-tier
The guarded border line at the edge of an empire separating Roman land from unconquered territory beyond.
Milecastle Mile-cas-tle
A small stone fortlet and guarded gateway built at every single Roman mile along Hadrian's Wall.
Latrine La-trine
A communal Roman stone toilet block flushed clean by flowing rainwater and drainage channels.
Preserved Pre-served
Kept safe from rotting or crumbling over hundreds of years, such as wooden letters trapped in oxygen-free mud.
Forum Fo-rum
The busy open-air marketplace and meeting square right in the middle of a Roman town.
Aqueduct Aq-ue-duct
A stone channel or bridge built by Roman engineers to carry fresh spring water into a town.
Hypocaust Hy-po-caust
Roman underfloor central heating where hot air from a furnace flowed between brick pillars beneath the floor.
Strigil Stri-gil
A curved bronze scraper used at Roman bathhouses to wipe olive oil, sweat, and dirt off the skin.
Hoard Hoard
A secret collection of valuable coins, silver, or jewellery buried underground to keep it safe in dangerous times.
Legacy Leg-a-cy
Something important handed down from the past — such as roads, cities, laws, or words — that still shapes our lives today.
Castra Cas-tra
The Latin word for a Roman fort, which survives today inside British town names ending in -chester or -caster.
Latin Lat-in
The language spoken and written by the Romans, which gave us our ABC alphabet and thousands of English words.
