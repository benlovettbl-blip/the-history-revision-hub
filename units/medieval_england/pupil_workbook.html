Medieval England: 1066–1485

Conquest, Castles, Church & The Shattering of Feudalism
Primary Visual Evidence • Anglo-Norman Embroidery The Bayeux Tapestry (c. 1070s)

Commissioned by Bishop Odo of Bayeux within a decade of 1066, this 70-metre embroidered linen masterpiece records the Norman Conquest as both triumphant statecraft and a profound human catastrophe:

1. The Saxon Shield Wall at Senlac:
Interlocking wooden round shields and Danish two-handed battleaxes held the ridge against hours of Norman cavalry charges and arrow storms.
2. Norman Heavy Cavalry with Couched Lances:
Iron stirrups and high pommel saddles allowed Norman knights to deliver devastating shock charges, transforming European medieval battle tactics.
3. Duke William Unmasks His Helmet:
When panic spread that Duke William was dead, he pushed back his nasal-helmet shouting: ‘Look at me! I live and by God’s grace shall conquer!’
4. The Slain King & The Marginal Dead:
King Harold is struck down beside his golden dragon standard; the bottom frieze shows stripped armor, severed limbs, and dead warriors.
Couched Lance
Shock cavalry breaking infantry shield walls.
Papal Banner
Holy crusade moral blessing from Rome.
Marginal Frieze
Brutal human carnage of Norman conquest.
Disciplinary Significance: Embroidered by English seamstresses under Norman rule, the tapestry serves both as Norman triumphal propaganda and a poignant Anglo-Saxon testimony to national defeat.
Disciplinary Hinge Question: Historical Evaluation
Does the Bayeux Tapestry celebrate William’s conquest as the righteous will of God, or does its vivid marginal art subtly subvert Norman triumph by mourning the destruction of Saxon England?

Question 3(b) & 3(c): Contrasting Interpretations [8 marks • 10 mins]

HISTORIOGRAPHICAL ANALYSIS
Interpretation 1 • Edward Grim, Eyewitness Monk & Chronicler, Life of St Thomas (c. 1175)
“The glorious martyr Thomas stood like a rock before the altar, refusing to yield the sacred liberties of Christ’s holy Church to earthly tyrants. He offered his neck to the wicked swords of the four knights, dying willingly so that God’s law might triumph over royal pride.”
Interpretation 2 • Historian Frank Barlow, Thomas Becket (1986)
“Becket was vain, stubborn, and obsessively confrontational. Having served Henry faithfully as chancellor, his dramatic conversion into an unbending champion of clerical privilege was theatrical extremism. By shielding rapists and murderers in holy orders from secular law, Becket provoked a needless crisis.”
Explain how Interpretations 1 and 2 differ on Thomas Becket’s motives and character. [4 marks]

Question 5: Evaluative Statement Essay [12 marks • 15 mins]

Extended Writing Practice
‘The Black Death of 1348 was the main cause of the breakdown of the feudal system in medieval England.’ How far do you agree? [12 marks]
Stimulus: The demographic collapse and labour shortage caused by the Black Death (1348–1350) The 1351 Statute of Labourers and the Peasants’ Revolt of 1381 (Support each factor with your own precise historical evidence)
1. PLAGUE & ECONOMIC LEVERAGE
Explain how the death of up to 50% of the population created a desperate shortage of agricultural labour, forcing lords to pay wages.
2. REPRESSION & SOCIAL REBELLION
Explain how parliamentary laws (Statute of Labourers) and poll taxes failed to stop peasant mobility, culminating in the 1381 Revolt.
3. COMPARATIVE VERDICT
Weigh whether biological demographic collapse or political peasant resistance ultimately dealt the fatal blow to feudal serfdom.
