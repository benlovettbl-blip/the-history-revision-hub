Enquiry 1 True Historical Story True Historical Story (14 October 1066)

Lifting the Iron Helmet: Duke William & the Feigned Retreat at Hastings

The History Revision Hub (Grounded in the Bayeux Tapestry & William of Poitiers)
Historical Context: On Saturday 14 October 1066, King Harold Godwinson’s tired Anglo-Saxon army locked shields atop steep Senlac Hill near Hastings. For hours Duke William’s Norman charges bounced off the English shield-wall—until a moment of battlefield panic inspired a deadly cavalry trap.
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Shield-Wall[SHEELD-wawl]
A tightly locked defensive line where Anglo-Saxon warriors overlapped wooden shields along the top of a ridge.
Huscarls[HUS-karlz]
Elite, heavily armoured bodyguards of the Anglo-Saxon king armed with fearsome two-handed Danish battleaxes.
Feigned Retreat[FAYND rih-TREET]
A clever cavalry trick of pretending to flee in panic to tempt defenders out of a strong hilltop line.
Senlac Hill[SEN-lak HIL]
The steep ridge north of Hastings where King Harold’s English army stood firm on 14 October 1066.

By mid-morning on Saturday 14 October 1066, Duke William’s invasion of England hung by a thread. High atop steep Senlac Hill stood King Harold’s warriors, tired from their long march south from Stamford Bridge yet locked tightly behind a wall of wooden shields. Every time Norman horsemen charged up the muddy slope, English huscarls swung heavy two-handed axes that cleaved straight through mail coats and shields.

Suddenly, panic rippled along the left wing of William’s army. Foot-soldiers stumbled backward down the slope, and a terrifying shout echoed across the valley: "The Duke is dead!"

Spurring his warhorse directly across the path of his fleeing knights, William pushed back his iron helmet so every soldier could see his bare, furious face. "Look at me well!" he roared, according to his chaplain William of Poitiers. "I live, and by God’s grace I shall conquer!"

That real panic gave William a cunning idea. Twice that afternoon, Norman knights charged uphill and then deliberately pretended to flee. Tempted by victory, hundreds of English fighters broke ranks to chase them downhill—where Norman cavalry wheeled sharply round, surrounded the scattered pursuers, and shattered Harold’s shield-wall before dusk.

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. Why couldn’t Duke William’s cavalry break King Harold’s army during the morning fighting at Hastings?
2. How did Duke William stop his own soldiers running away, and how did that moment inspire the "feigned retreat"?
3. Why did lifting his iron helmet to show his bare face matter so much—and why was the feigned retreat trap so tempting for Harold’s men?

Enquiry 2 True Historical Story True Historical Story (1085–1086)

"Not One Ox, Nor One Pig Left Out!": King William’s Great Domesday Survey

The History Revision Hub (Grounded in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle 1085 & Domesday Book)
Historical Context: After crushing rebellions with timber castles and the brutal Harrying of the North (1069–1070), King William spent Christmas 1085 at Gloucester ordering a giant survey of England. Within months, royal commissioners counted every manor, watermill, plough, and pig across the realm.
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Domesday Book[DOOMZ-day BOOK]
King William’s great 1086 land and tax survey of England, named after God’s final Day of Judgment.
Commissioners[kuh-MISH-uhn-erz]
Senior Norman bishops and barons sent out across England’s shires in 1086 to question witnesses under oath.
Harrying[HAR-ee-ing]
King William’s scorched-earth burning of Yorkshire crops, barns, and cattle in 1069–1070 to starve northern rebels.
Vasta (Wasteland)[VAS-tuh]
The chilling Latin word written across northern Domesday pages where ruined villages still lay empty sixteen years after 1070.

Twenty years after winning Hastings, King William wanted to know what his conquered kingdom was worth. He also wanted to know how much tax he could squeeze from it. At Gloucester during Christmas 1085, the King sent royal officials riding into every shire across England.

In each county town, Norman lords sat beside scribes armed with goose-quill pens and sheepskin parchment. Standing nervously before them were the sheriff, the village priest, the reeve, and six English peasants from every village. Swearing an oath on holy relics, they had to answer strict questions. Who held this land in 1066, and which Norman holds it now? How many ploughs, fishponds, and watermills stand here?

An angry English monk writing the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle gasped at William’s thoroughness. "Not one ox, nor one cow, nor one pig was left out!" he complained.

Across Yorkshire, William had burned crops and barns during the brutal Harrying of the North sixteen years earlier. Beside village after village, scribes wrote one grim Latin word—vasta, meaning "wasteland." English folk called the finished volumes Domesday Book. Like God’s Judgment Day, nobody could argue against it.

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. How did King William’s commissioners make sure local lords and villagers couldn’t lie or hide farm animals in 1086?
2. Why was the English monk writing the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle so angry that William counted every ox, cow, and pig?
3. Sixteen years after the Harrying of the North, scribes still wrote the Latin word vasta ("wasteland") beside Yorkshire village after village. What does that single word tell us?

Vita Sancti Thomae: Edward Grim’s Wounded Arm in Canterbury Cathedral

Edward Grim (c. 1172 • British Library Cotton MS Claudius B II)
Historical Context: Edward Grim was a visiting clerk from Cambridge standing right beside Archbishop Thomas Becket in Canterbury Cathedral on the evening of 29 December 1170. When four household knights of King Henry II burst in with swords, Grim threw up his own arm to shield Becket from the first blow.
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Archbishop[arch-BISH-uhp]
The Archbishop of Canterbury—the highest church leader in England, answering spiritually to the Pope in Rome.
Excommunication[eks-kuh-myoo-nih-KAY-shun]
Officially cutting someone off from the Catholic Church and its sacraments—a terrifying spiritual punishment.
Sanctuary[SANK-choo-er-ee]
Sacred church ground where medieval law forbade bloodshed or violent arrest.
Martyr[MAR-ter]
Someone killed because they refuse to give up their religious faith or principles.

"Twilight was falling on 29 December 1170 when frightened monks hurried Archbishop Thomas Becket into Canterbury Cathedral for evening prayers. Begging him to bolt the heavy oak doors against King Henry II’s men, they tried to slide the iron bars shut. Thomas stopped them calmly upon the steps: 'It is not right to turn the house of prayer into a military fortress. Even unlocked, God’s Church can shield its own.'"

"Moments later, four mailed knights burst through the cloister door brandishing drawn swords and axes. 'Where is Thomas Becket, traitor to the King and realm?' they shouted into the dim candlelight. Stepping out beside a stone pillar, Thomas answered clearly: 'Here I am—no traitor to the King, but a priest of God. What do you seek?'"

"The knights demanded that he lift the Church ban placed upon the King’s bishops, yet Thomas stood firm. When they tried to drag him outside, I wrapped my arms tight around him. As the first knight swung his blade down toward the Archbishop’s head, I threw my own arm forward to block the blow. Steel sliced deep into my arm, and Thomas bowed his head in prayer as their swords struck the stone floor."

3. Why do you think the four knights tried to drag Becket outside before striking him, and what made the narrator’s own action so brave?

Enquiry 4 True Historical Story True Historical Story (15 June 1215)

Cornered in the Water-Meadow: King John & Magna Carta at Runnymede

The History Revision Hub (Grounded in Roger of Wendover & Magna Carta 1215)
Historical Context: After losing Normandy to France, quarrelling with the Pope, and demanding heavy scutage taxes eleven times, King John lost London to rebel barons in May 1215. Cornered beside the Thames at Runnymede on 15 June 1215, John had to authorize the Great Seal on Magna Carta.
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Runnymede[RUN-ee-meed]
The boggy water-meadow beside the River Thames near Windsor where King John agreed to Magna Carta in June 1215.
Scutage[SKOO-tij]
"Shield money"—a heavy feudal tax King John demanded eleven times to pay for failed foreign wars.
Magna Carta[MAG-nuh KAR-tuh]
Latin for "The Great Charter" (1215), placing the English king beneath the rule of law.
Free Man[FREE MAN]
In 1215, barons, knights, and free townsmen—leaving out the unfree peasant villeins who farmed most manors.

On the morning of 15 June 1215, bright silk tents fluttered across the wet meadow of Runnymede beside the River Thames. Inside the royal tent sat King John, hiding his anger behind a tight smile. For once in his reign, the King of England was trapped.

Over sixteen bitter years, John had lost Normandy to France and quarrelled with the Pope. Worse still, he starved noble hostages in dungeons and demanded "scutage" shield-taxes eleven times. That May, rebel lords calling themselves the "Army of God" marched straight into London after citizens threw open the city gates. Deserted by nearly all his knights, John had to bargain.

Watched by Archbishop Stephen Langton and ranks of mailed barons, royal clerks pressed the heavy Great Seal into beeswax. Those sixty-three Latin rules became known as Magna Carta. Clause 39 laid down a famous promise. "No free man shall be seized or imprisoned, or stripped of his rights, except by the lawful judgment of his equals or by the law of the land."

In 1215, that promise left out unfree peasant serfs, and John soon asked the Pope to cancel the charter. Even so, for the first time in writing, England’s king stood beneath the law.

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. Which dramatic event in May 1215 finally forced King John to meet the rebel barons at Runnymede?
2. How did royal clerks make Magna Carta official at Runnymede, and why was King John hiding his anger behind a tight smile?
3. Why didn’t Clause 39 protect everyone in England in 1215—and why did Magna Carta still matter so much?

"When Adam Delved and Eve Span": Chaucer’s Ploughman & John Ball’s Rebel Verse

John Ball (1381 Blackheath Couplet) & Geoffrey Chaucer (The Canterbury Tales, c. 1387)
Historical Context: After the 1348 Black Death halved England’s workforce, lords froze wages by law and demanded three flat-rate Poll Taxes between 1377 and 1381. While poet Geoffrey Chaucer praised hard-working ploughmen, radical priest John Ball turned a two-line rhyme into dynamite during the June 1381 Peasants’ Revolt!
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Swinker[SWINK-er]
Chaucer’s Middle English word for an honest, hard-working farm labourer.
Delved[DELVD]
Dug and turned the soil with a spade—just as biblical Adam farmed the earth.
Villeinage[VIL-uh-nij]
Unfree serfdom tying peasant families to a lord’s manor for unpaid labour and fines.
Poll Tax[POHL TAKS]
The hated 1381 "head tax" forcing a poor villager to pay the exact same twelve pence as a rich duke.

After the Black Death wiped out nearly half of England’s people, surviving villagers knew their farm work was scarce and valuable. Yet Parliament froze wages by law and demanded three unfair Poll Taxes, charging poor labourers the exact same twelve pence as wealthy lords!

In fourteenth-century poetry, Geoffrey Chaucer honoured the quiet dignity of ordinary country workers in The Canterbury Tales.

"With him there was a Ploughman, was his brother,
That had hauled of dung full many a cartload.
A true swinker and a good was he,
Living in peace and perfect charity."

By June 1381, however, patience snapped across Essex and Kent. After rebels broke open Maidstone prison to free radical priest John Ball, thousands gathered on Blackheath hill overlooking London. Before the roaring crowd, Ball cried out the two-line rebel rhyme that echoed from village to village.

"When Adam delved and Eve span,
Who was then the gentleman?"

In Eden, Ball preached, God made no lords and no serfs! Led by Wat Tyler, the rebels crossed London Bridge, burned the manor rolls recording their serfdom, and demanded freedom from fourteen-year-old King Richard II.

2. What clever argument did John Ball pack into his two-line rhyme, "When Adam delved and Eve span, Who was then the gentleman?"?
3. Why did Wat Tyler’s rebels deliberately burn the manor rolls instead of just smashing things at random?

Enquiry 1 (p. 2): William’s Helmet (1066)
Q1: Harold held the steep top of Senlac Hill in a locked shield-wall, where Norman horses struggled uphill against two-handed huscarl axes.
Q2: He pushed back his helmet to show his face alive; seeing English fighters chase his retreating wing gave him the idea to fake two cavalry retreats.
Q3: Showing his bare face proved instantly that the rumour of his death was false; later, seeing Normans appear to flee again tempted tired English fighters to break their shield-wall and chase them downhill into a cavalry trap.
Enquiry 2 (p. 3): Not One Ox Left Out!
Q1: They questioned the sheriff, the village priest, the reeve, and six peasants from every village in public court under a sacred religious oath.
Q2: Conquered English people felt outraged that a foreign king recorded every last farm animal so he could squeeze as much tax out of them as possible.
Q3: It shows that William’s burning of Yorkshire’s crops, barns, and cattle in 1069–1070 was so brutal that whole villages still lay ruined and empty sixteen years later.
Enquiry 3 (p. 4): Grim at Canterbury (1170)
Q1: He insisted a church is a house of prayer, not a military fortress, believing God’s Church could shield its own even with unlocked doors.
Q2: He stepped out calmly saying he was "no traitor to the King, but a priest of God," and stood firm when they demanded he lift the Church ban placed on the King’s bishops.
Q3: Cathedral ground was sacred sanctuary where bloodshed was forbidden; when the knights attacked anyway, the narrator wrapped his arms around Becket and blocked the first sword blow with his own arm!
Enquiry 4 (p. 5): John at Runnymede (1215)
Q1: London citizens threw open the city gates to the rebel "Army of God," and nearly all of John’s knights deserted him, leaving him trapped.
Q2: Royal clerks pressed the heavy Great Seal into beeswax while armed barons watched; John was furious at being forced to bargain, and he soon asked the Pope to cancel the charter!
Q3: Clause 39 only protected "free men" and left out unfree peasant serfs, yet for the first time in writing it proved that even the king stood beneath the law.
Enquiry 5 (p. 6): When Adam Delved (1381)
Q1: Even though he hauled heavy cartloads of manure in the fields, Chaucer praises him as an honest hard worker ("true swinker") living in peace and Christian kindness.
Q2: When Adam dug and Eve spun wool at Creation, no nobles existed—proving serfdom and class inequality were invented by greedy men, not God.
Q3: The manor rolls held the written records proving which villagers were unfree serfs—burning them destroyed the proof of their serfdom as they demanded freedom!
