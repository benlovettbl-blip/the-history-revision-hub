Hymn to the Nile (Praise to Hapi)

Middle Kingdom Egyptian Scribe Tradition (c. 2100 BC)
Historical Context: Without rain in the scorching Sahara Desert, all of Ancient Egypt depended on one giant river bursting its banks every July. Read this 4,000-year-old hymn aloud to hear how villagers celebrated the rising water.
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Inundation[in-un-DAY-shun]
The life-giving summer flood when the River Nile burst its banks and spread rich black mud over the fields.
Kemet[KEM-et]
Ancient Egypt's name for itself, meaning 'Black Land', after the dark fertile river silt left behind by floods.
Papyrus[puh-PY-rus]
Tall marsh reeds growing along the Nile that Egyptians pressed into smooth writing sheets and plaited into sandals.
Silt[SILT]
Fine, nutrient-rich dark river mud carried downstream by floodwater to help wheat and barley grow.

Every summer across dry Egypt, families watched the southern sky and waited eagerly for the river to rise. Four thousand years ago, Egyptian pupils copied a joyful song onto papyrus rolls to thank the annual Nile inundation.

Hail to you, O Nile, rising out of the earth,
Coming in peace to keep all Egypt alive!
You water the green orchards created by Ra,
And give cool drink to every thirsty flock.
When your flood delays, every breath grows weak,
And rich and poor cry out together in fear!
Yet when you rise, the Black Land shouts for joy,
Every belly is glad and every back shakes with laughter!
You bring sweet bread and fill the storehouses wide,
Remembering the poor family waiting at the gate.
Sweet harps ring out to praise your dark mud,
And children clap their hands along the river bank!

Without that yearly gift of black silt, golden wheat could never sprout beside scorching desert sand.

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. In the first verse of the hymn, what happened across Egypt whenever the Nile flood was late?
2. Why does the poet say 'every belly is glad and every back shakes with laughter' as soon as the floodwaters rise?
3. Usually people are frightened when a river floods—why did Egyptian children clap their hands and adults play harps to welcome this flood?

Garlic, Bread and Wet Sand at Giza

The History Revision Hub Narrative Archive
Historical Context: For centuries, people guessed that whipped slaves built the Great Pyramid of Giza. Then archaeologists dug up the builders' real town and even found Boat Inspector Merer's 4,500-year-old papyrus diary!
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Giza[GEE-zuh]
The rocky desert plateau near modern Cairo where Pharaoh Khufu raised the 146-metre Great Pyramid.
Tura Limestone[TOO-ruh LYM-stohn]
Fine, gleaming white stone quarried across the Nile and floated by barge to case the outside of Khufu's pyramid.
Sledge[SLEJ]
A heavy wooden frame on smooth runners pulled with ropes over wet desert sand to move two-tonne stone blocks.
Archaeology[ar-kee-OL-uh-jee]
Studying human history by digging up and examining buildings, bones, tools, and food remains left underground.

Dawn broke pink over Giza around 2560 BC. Warm woodsmoke drifted from rows of dome-shaped mud ovens. Inside the walled builders' village, bakers tipped hot loaves of fresh bread out of clay moulds. Nobody stood cracking whips over starving slaves here.

Down at the canal harbour, Boat Inspector Merer opened his papyrus logbook and dipped his reed pen in ink. His forty-man crew had just floated shining white limestone blocks across the flooded Nile from Tura quarry. Proud teams painted bold gang names onto blocks, calling themselves "Friends of Khufu" and "Drunkards of Menkaure".

Out on the ramp, a young worker sloshed river water onto the sand ahead of a wooden sledge. Wet sand halved rubbing friction! Haulers pulled the two-tonne stone steadily skyward. At sunset, tired crews shared roasted Nile fish, beef, sweet onions, and crisp garlic. Four thousand years later, ash-filled bakeries and healed bones proved well-fed Egyptian teams built the Great Pyramid.

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. What clever trick did Giza workers use to stop heavy wooden sledges from digging into dry desert sand?
2. What do team names like 'Friends of Khufu' tell us about how the pyramid builders felt about their work?
3. Many people used to think starving, whipped slaves built the Great Pyramid—what details in this story would surprise them most?

The Instruction of Dua-Kheti (The Satire of the Trades)

Dua-Kheti (Middle Kingdom Egypt, c. 1900 BC)
Historical Context: Almost four thousand years ago, an Egyptian father named Dua-Kheti sailed south up the Nile to drop his young son Pepi at scribe school. Listen to the funny warnings he gave Pepi to make him practise his 700 hieroglyphs!
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Scribe[SKRYB]
A respected Ancient Egyptian official trained for years to read and write hieroglyphs, count taxes, and keep royal records.
Hieroglyphs[HY-ruh-glifs]
Ancient Egypt's sacred writing system combining over 700 picture signs and sound symbols.
Satire[SAT-yr]
Clever, funny writing that exaggerates everyday problems to make readers laugh and think.
Ostraca[OS-truh-kuh]
Broken pottery shards or flat limestone flakes used like cheap scrap notebooks by trainee scribes.

Nearly four thousand years ago, an Egyptian father named Dua-Kheti sailed south up the Nile with his young son Pepi. Pepi was starting at the royal school for scribes, so his father recited a witty poem to stop him daydreaming.

Set your whole heart on books, my son,
For nothing beats learning how to write!
I have watched the smith beside his roaring furnace,
With fingers wrinkled like rough crocodile skin.
The reed-cutter toils until his bare arms ache,
While hungry mosquitoes bite his face at dusk!
The potter kneels smeared in wet river clay,
Dirtier than pigs snuffling round the yard.
The cobbler chews tough leather strips all day,
And the washerman scrubs beside lurking crocodiles!
Look round: no job on Earth escapes a boss,
Except the scribe—for he gives out the orders!

Trainee scribes laughed at Kheti's jokes for centuries, copying his verses onto pottery scraps until their reed brushes flew neatly across papyrus.

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. Which unpleasant dangers or discomforts does Dua-Kheti describe for the smith, the reed-cutter, and the washerman?
2. Do you think Dua-Kheti was being completely fair to Egypt's skilled potters and cobblers, or exaggerating on purpose?
3. If you were young Pepi listening to your dad's funny poem on the boat, would it persuade you to work hard at scribe school? Why?

One Feather Against a Lifetime: Scribe Ani's Book of the Dead

The History Revision Hub Narrative Archive
Historical Context: Around 1250 BC in Thebes, a real royal scribe named Ani paid master artists to paint a twenty-four-metre papyrus scroll for his tomb. Step inside the scene painted on that scroll as Ani faces the golden scales!
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Anubis[uh-NYOO-bis]
The jackal-headed Egyptian god of embalming and tombs who checked the golden scales in the underworld.
Ma'at[mah-AHT]
The Egyptian goddess of truth, fairness, and cosmic balance, represented by a single light ostrich feather.
Ammit[AM-it]
The fearsome underworld monster—part crocodile, lion, and hippopotamus—waiting to gobble up heavy, selfish hearts.
Field of Reeds[FEELD ov REEDZ]
Ancient Egypt's sunny afterlife paradise of green canals and rich harvests ruled by green-skinned Osiris.

Around 1250 BC in Thebes, artists painted a long papyrus scroll in blue, red, and gold for a real scribe named Ani. Rolled inside Ani's tomb, this Book of the Dead showed the toughest afterlife test in the Hall of Two Truths.

In the scene, Ani bows in clean white linen beside his wife Tutu while forty-two gods watch from above. Kneeling before tall golden scales, jackal-headed Anubis steadies the brass pointer. On one pan sits Ani's heart. Priests left it inside his mummy chest because Egyptians believed the heart kept every good and bad memory. On the other pan rests one light white feather from Ma'at, goddess of truth.

Right below crouches Ammit the Devourer, snapping crocodile jaws on a lion-and-hippo body! Holding his breath, Ani whispers his plea. "O my heart, do not stand as a witness against me!" Ibis-headed Thoth marks his tablet. The pans stay level, and Osiris welcomes kind Ani into the sunny Field of Reeds.

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. Why did priests leave Ani's heart inside his mummy chest instead of taking it out?
2. Why was an ostrich feather the perfect symbol to place on the opposite pan of the scales?
3. Ani whispered, 'O my heart, do not stand as a witness against me!' What does this afterlife test tell us about what Ancient Egyptians thought made a good person?

Enquiry 5 Eyewitness Diary, Letter & Speech Royal Obelisk Inscription

Pharaoh Hatshepsut's Karnak Obelisk & Punt Inscriptions

Pharaoh Hatshepsut (Carved at Thebes, c. 1460 BC)
Historical Context: Around 1479 BC, royal woman Hatshepsut crowned herself full Pharaoh of Egypt, complete with the striped royal headcloth and ceremonial false beard! Read the proud message she carved into stone for people living thousands of years after her.
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Hatshepsut[hat-SHEP-soot]
One of Ancient Egypt's most successful female pharaohs, famous for peaceful sea trade and grand stone temples.
Obelisk[OB-uh-lisk]
A tall, four-sided stone pillar tapering to a pointed pyramid tip, carved from a single block of red granite.
Land of Punt[LAND ov PUNT]
A distant trading land south along the Red Sea coast, rich in gold, ebony wood, and sweet frankincense trees.
Deir el-Bahari[dair el-bah-HAH-ree]
Hatshepsut's breathtaking terraced cliff temple near Thebes where carvers pictured her ships sailing to Punt.

Around 1460 BC, female Pharaoh Hatshepsut had stonemasons carve her own proud words onto tall granite obelisks at Karnak. More carvings lined her bright cliff temple at Deir el-Bahari. Speaking across the ages, she addressed everyone who would ever stand below her stones.

"Listen closely, all you people yet unborn who shall see this monument in future years! Do not say, 'I do not know why this was made.' Truly, I sat quiet inside my palace and remembered great Amun who shaped me. My heart led me to raise two tall red granite obelisks from Aswan. Their gleaming gold tips shine across both banks of the Nile!"

"I also sent five great wooden ships south down the Red Sea to the Land of Punt. No ruler before me brought back living frankincense trees with baskets of earth round their roots! Now sweet smoke fills Karnak's halls, and my name shall live forever in stone."

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. Who was Pharaoh Hatshepsut speaking to when she carved 'all you people yet unborn' onto her tall granite obelisks?
2. Why was Hatshepsut so proud of bringing back living frankincense trees with baskets of earth round their roots?
3. Hatshepsut wanted her name to 'live forever in stone'—what achievements did she choose to be remembered for instead of fighting wars?

Enquiry 6 True Historical Story True Discovery Story

Sixteen Buried Steps: Uncovering Tutankhamun's Tomb

The History Revision Hub Narrative Archive
Historical Context: For years, experts insisted nothing remained left to find in Egypt's Valley of the Kings. Discover how a young Egyptian water-boy's digging stick in November 1922 led to the most dazzling archaeological moment in history!
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Tutankhamun[too-tahn-KAH-moon]
The New Kingdom boy-pharaoh crowned aged nine around 1332 BC whose hidden cliff tomb escaped ancient grave robbers.
Valley of the Kings[VAL-ee ov thuh KINGZ]
A hot, secret desert canyon opposite Thebes where New Kingdom pharaohs cut deep rock tombs underground.
Excavation[eks-kuh-VAY-shun]
Carefully digging, brushing, photographing, and recording buried historical objects without damaging clues.
Sarcophagus[sar-KOF-uh-gus]
A heavy carved stone coffin holding royal nested coffins and Tutankhamun's eleven-kilogram solid gold mask.

On 4 November 1922, fierce sun baked the rocky floor in Egypt's Valley of the Kings. Near old stone huts, young water-boy Hussein Abdel Rassoul scooped away loose sand for heavy clay water jars. Suddenly, his stick struck flat, cut rock!

Egyptian diggers brushed away gravel and found sixteen steep stone steps going down into the cliff. At the foot stood a plastered door stamped with royal jackal seals three thousand years old! British archaeologist Howard Carter wired his backer, Lord Carnarvon, to sail out from England at once.

On 26 November, Carter chipped a small hole in the inner door and held a lit candle inside. Warm ancient air rushed out and made the tiny flame dance wildly. Slowly, gold couches, statues, chariots, and a shining throne glinted through the gloom! Behind him, Carnarvon whispered tensely. "Can you see anything?" Carter breathed back in awe. "Yes, wonderful things!"

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. How did young water-boy Hussein Abdel Rassoul accidentally uncover the hidden stone steps on 4 November 1922?
2. Why did Howard Carter wait from 4 November until 26 November before chipping a tiny hole in the inner door?
3. Imagine standing right behind Howard Carter in the dark tunnel as his candle flame danced—what must it have felt like to hear him whisper, 'Yes, wonderful things!'?

Parent, Carer & Tutor Conversation Guide & Answer Key Ancient Egypt
No Specialist History Knowledge Needed: Pupils do not need word-for-word answers—praise curiosity and listen for these core historical ideas during your 3-minute chat!
Enquiry 1 (p. 2): Hymn to the River Nile
Q1: Every breath grew weak, and rich and poor people cried out together in fear because the orchards and flocks needed the rising water.
Q2: Rising waters left behind damp black Kemet mud, guaranteeing full grain storehouses, fresh bread, and relief for every family.
Q3: In dry desert Egypt, the yearly flood brought cool drinking water and rich black silt so golden wheat could grow, filling storehouses with sweet bread for rich and poor alike.
Enquiry 2 (p. 3): Bread for Pyramid Crews
Q1: A worker poured river water onto the sand right in front of the sledge runners, halving friction so blocks slid smoothly.
Q2: They worked in proud teams who enjoyed naming their gangs and took pride in building for Pharaoh Khufu, rather than being whipped, starving slaves.
Q3: The builders lived in a walled village with fresh bread ovens, ate roasted fish, beef, onions, and garlic, gave themselves proud team names, and left behind healed bones and ash-filled bakeries.
Enquiry 3 (p. 4): Satire of the Trades
Q1: The smith gets fingers rough as crocodile skin by his hot furnace, mosquitoes bite the reed-cutter, and crocodiles lurk near the washerman.
Q2: He was exaggerating on purpose for a laugh! Like all satire, he made other jobs sound messy and dangerous so his son Pepi would focus on learning to write.
Q3: Learning over 700 hieroglyphs took years of practice, yet becoming a scribe meant counting taxes, keeping royal records, and giving out orders instead of dodging crocodiles or chewing leather!
Enquiry 4 (p. 5): Weighing the Heart
Q1: Egyptians believed the heart kept every good and bad memory from a person's life, so Anubis needed to weigh it on the golden scales.
Q2: A single feather is almost weightless, showing that a person's heart had to be completely free of heavy lies, cruelty, and greed.
Q3: It shows that kindness, truth, and fairness mattered most—only a light, honest heart could balance Ma'at's feather and reach the sunny Field of Reeds.
Enquiry 5 (p. 6): Hatshepsut's Bold Boast
Q1: She was speaking directly to future people standing below her stones centuries later—including us reading her words today!
Q2: Because no ruler before her had brought back whole living trees to plant in Egypt, so their sweet incense smoke could fill Karnak's halls year after year.
Q3: She chose to be remembered for peaceful sea trade to the Land of Punt, raising gleaming gold-tipped red granite obelisks at Karnak, and building her grand cliff temple at Deir el-Bahari.
Enquiry 6 (p. 7): Candlelight in Tomb KV62
Q1: He was using his stick to scoop away loose sand for heavy clay water jars near old stone huts when he suddenly struck flat, cut rock.
Q2: He waited for his backer Lord Carnarvon to sail all the way out from England, and he opened the door carefully so no buried clues or treasures were damaged.
Q3: It would have felt breathless and magical, as gold couches, statues, chariots, and a shining throne slowly glinted out of a tomb sealed for three thousand years.
