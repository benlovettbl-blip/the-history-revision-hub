WALK-IN LESSON PLANS • ENQUIRIES 1 & 2 NON-SPECIALIST PRIMARY TEACHER GUIDE
Enquiry 1: Four River Cradles & The Gift of the Nile — Why Did Ancient Egypt Flourish? (c. 3500–2700 BC) TB p.4 • WB pp.3–4
Key Enquiry: Where did the earliest river civilisations grow, and why did Ancient Egypt flourish beside the River Nile?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This opening Key Stage 2 enquiry places Ancient Egypt alongside the three other earliest river valley civilisations required by the National Curriculum: Ancient Sumer (Tigris/Euphrates), the Indus Valley, and the Shang Dynasty (Yellow River). Primary pupils investigate how the annual Nile inundation created fertile Kemet (Black Land) beside arid Deshret (Red Land), how farmers used shaduf levers to grow wheat, barley, flax, and papyrus, and how King Narmer united Upper and Lower Egypt around 3100 BC.
Learning Objectives:
  • Locate Ancient Egypt alongside Ancient Sumer, the Indus Valley, and the Shang Dynasty as the four earliest river civilisations.
  • Explain how the Nile inundation, Kemet silt, and wooden shaduf irrigation allowed Egyptian farming to flourish.
  • Describe why Upper Egypt lay in the south and Lower Egypt in the north, and how King Narmer united them around 3100 BC.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Calling Ancient Egypt "the Gift of the Nile" makes complete historical sense because without the river Egypt would have been empty Sahara desert! Each summer, the inundation left rich black Kemet silt where villagers swung shadufs to grow wheat, barley, flax, and papyrus reeds. Just as importantly, the Nile acted as a liquid highway for wooden boats travelling north with the current and south with the wind, allowing King Narmer to unite Upper and Lower Egypt around 3100 BC under the Double Crown.”
Enquiry 2: Pyramids of Giza & The Great Sphinx — How Were Giant Stone Tombs Built? (c. 2670–2500 BC) TB p.5 • WB pp.5–6
Key Enquiry: Why and how did Old Kingdom Egyptians build giant stone pyramids and the Great Sphinx at Giza?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This second Key Stage 2 enquiry explores Old Kingdom pyramid building (c. 2670–2500 BC). Primary pupils trace why Pharaohs were revered as living gods, how architect Imhotep pioneered Pharaoh Djoser’s Step Pyramid at Saqqara, and how Pharaoh Khufu raised the 146-metre Great Pyramid at Giza using copper chisels, flood-season Nile barges, and wet-sand sledges. Crucially, pupils weigh archaeological evidence from builders’ villages (bakeries, fish rations, healed bones) to bust the myth that slaves built the pyramids, finishing at Pharaoh Khafre’s Great Sphinx.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain why Old Kingdom Pharaohs built giant stone tombs and trace the leap from Imhotep’s Step Pyramid to Khufu’s Great Pyramid.
  • Describe how Egyptian masons quarried, floated, and hauled 2.3 million limestone blocks without wheels, cranes, or iron.
  • Use archaeological clues to refute the myth that slaves built the pyramids and explain the purpose of Khafre’s Great Sphinx.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Old Kingdom Egyptians built giant stone tombs and the Great Sphinx through clever ideas and teamwork. After architect Imhotep stacked six tiers for Djoser’s Step Pyramid at Saqqara around 2670 BC, Pharaoh Khufu raised the 146-metre Great Pyramid at Giza from 2.3 million limestone blocks. Masons shaped rocks with copper chisels, floated white Tura limestone on Nile barges during the summer flood, and pulled wooden sledges over wet sand up ramps — all powered by well-fed paid workers who also carved Khafre’s Great Sphinx!”

Enquiry 3: Scribes, Hieroglyphs & Everyday Life — How Did Ordinary Egyptians Live? (c. 2500–1200 BC) TB p.6 • WB pp.7–8
Key Enquiry: How did everyday life differ across Egypt’s social pyramid, and how did historians finally crack hieroglyphs?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This third Key Stage 2 enquiry explores social hierarchy, everyday family life, and literacy in Ancient Egypt. Primary pupils compare roles on the Egyptian social pyramid (Pharaoh, viziers/priests/scribes, craftworkers, peasant farmers), step inside whitewashed flat-roofed mud-brick homes with white linen clothes, woven reed sandals, and Senet boards, and trace how scribes wrote 700+ hieroglyphs before Jean-François Champollion decoded the Rosetta Stone (196 BC / found 1799) in 1822 using Ancient Greek and royal cartouches.
Learning Objectives:
  • Compare the roles and responsibilities of Pharaohs, viziers, priests, scribes, craftworkers, and peasant farmers on Egypt’s social pyramid.
  • Explain how whitewashed mud-brick houses, flat roofs, white linen, reed sandals, and Senet shaped everyday Egyptian life.
  • Describe scribe school training and explain how Jean-François Champollion used the Rosetta Stone to decode hieroglyphs in 1822.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Across Egypt’s social pyramid, rich officials and peasant farmers lived very different working lives, yet shared clever desert habits. Near the top beneath the Pharaoh, viziers, priests, and trained scribes studied over 700 hieroglyphs on papyrus scrolls to run taxes and temples, while skilled craftworkers glazed treasures and millions of peasant farmers toiled in muddy fields without learning to read. Even so, families rich and poor wore cool white linen and reed sandals, sheltered in whitewashed mud-brick rooms, slept on flat roofs, and loved playing Senet!”
Enquiry 4: Gods, Mummies & Weighing the Heart — What Did Egyptians Believe About Death? (c. 2000–1000 BC) TB p.7 • WB pp.9–10
Key Enquiry: What did Ancient Egyptians believe happened after death, and why did they make mummies?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This fourth Key Stage 2 enquiry explores Ancient Egyptian religion and funerary beliefs (c. 2000–1000 BC). Primary pupils meet major deities (Ra, Osiris, Isis, Horus, Anubis, Thoth, Ma'at) and discover why preserving the physical body mattered for the twin spirit parts (ka and ba). Pupils trace 70-day mummification with natron salt, Canopic jars, heart scarabs, and shabti figurines before stepping into the Book of the Dead's Weighing of the Heart before monster Ammit and Osiris's Field of Reeds.
Learning Objectives:
  • Identify key Ancient Egyptian gods and explain why preserving the body mattered for the soul (ka and ba).
  • Describe how priests preserved mummies over seventy days using natron salt, Canopic jars, amulets, and shabtis.
  • Explain what the Book of the Dead's Weighing of the Heart reveals about Egyptian values of truth and kindness.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Mummification and the Weighing of the Heart reveal a huge amount about Egyptian values because they show Egyptians loved Nile life so dearly they wanted it to carry on forever in the sunny Field of Reeds. Spending seventy days drying bodies in natron, carving Canopic jars, and moulding shabti servants proves how deeply families cared for loved ones. Most importantly, balancing every heart — whether pharaoh or farmer — against Ma'at's feather of truth proves Egyptians prized honesty, fairness, and kindness above gold!”

Enquiry 5: Hatshepsut, Nefertiti & Ramesses II — Who Were Egypt's Mightiest New Kingdom Rulers? (c. 1500–1213 BC) TB p.8 • WB pp.11–12
Key Enquiry: What made Hatshepsut, Nefertiti, and Ramesses II three of Ancient Egypt's most remarkable New Kingdom rulers?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This fifth Key Stage 2 enquiry investigates power, trade, religion, and diplomacy during Egypt's New Kingdom Golden Age (c. 1550–1213 BC), centred on Thebes and Karnak. Primary pupils compare female Pharaoh Hatshepsut (her royal false beard, Red Sea trading expedition to Punt for frankincense trees, and cliff temple at Deir el-Bahari), Akhenaten and Queen Nefertiti's short-lived worship of the sun-disc Aten at Amarna, and Ramesses II's 66-year reign, chariot battle at Kadesh, c. 1259 BC peace treaty, and 20-metre cliff statues at Abu Simbel.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain how female Pharaoh Hatshepsut showed royal power and enriched Egypt through peaceful trade with Punt.
  • Describe how Akhenaten and Queen Nefertiti briefly changed Egyptian religion at their desert capital of Amarna.
  • Evaluate why Ramesses II ("Ramesses the Great") is remembered for both Kadesh peace diplomacy and giant monuments like Abu Simbel.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Both Pharaoh Hatshepsut and Pharaoh Ramesses II achieved remarkable things during the New Kingdom, however each showed greatness in different ways. Hatshepsut proved a woman could govern Egypt peacefully as full pharaoh, sending Red Sea ships to Punt for frankincense trees and building graceful Deir el-Bahari. Meanwhile, Ramesses II ruled for sixty-six years, carved four twenty-metre statues into the cliffs at Abu Simbel, and wisely signed the world's oldest surviving peace treaty after Kadesh around 1259 BC — making both unforgettable leaders!”
Enquiry 6: Tutankhamun's Tomb & Cleopatra VII — How Did Ancient Egypt End & Live On? (1332–30 BC & AD 1922) TB p.9 • WB pp.13–14
Key Enquiry: Why did Tutankhamun's hidden tomb survive until 1922, and how did Cleopatra VII's reign end pharaonic Egypt?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This final Key Stage 2 enquiry brings together archaeological discovery and the end of pharaonic rule. Primary pupils investigate why New Kingdom rulers switched from pyramids to rock-cut tombs in the Valley of the Kings, and how boy-pharaoh Tutankhamun (died 1323 BC, aged 19) lay hidden under rubble until Howard Carter, Hussein Abdel Rassoul, and Egyptian excavators opened KV62 in November 1922 ("wonderful things!" — over 5,000 artefacts including the 11kg gold mask). Pupils then jump forward to Alexander the Great (332 BC), Ptolemaic Alexandria, multi-lingual Queen Cleopatra VII, and Rome's conquest in 30 BC.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain why New Kingdom tombs moved to the Valley of the Kings and why Tutankhamun's tomb KV62 escaped ancient robbers.
  • Describe how Howard Carter and Egyptian excavators discovered and recorded over 5,000 treasures inside KV62 in November 1922.
  • Explain who Queen Cleopatra VII was and how Roman victory in 30 BC ended rule by the pharaohs.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Across more than three thousand years beside the River Nile, Ancient Egypt achieved astonishing wonders! Farmers turned flooded Kemet silt into rich harvests using shadufs, while Imhotep and Khufu engineered towering limestone pyramids so old that Queen Cleopatra VII lived closer in time to us today than to Giza's builders! Scribes recorded maths and medicine in hieroglyphs on papyrus, Hatshepsut traded peacefully with Punt, Ramesses II signed the oldest surviving peace treaty, and Tutankhamun's 5,000 KV62 treasures still dazzle the world!”
