WORLD WAR II & THE HOME FRONT

Evacuation, Kindertransport, The Blitz, Rationing & Rebuilding Lives
BIG QUESTION “How did World War II change the lives of children and families across Britain and Europe?”
Primary Photograph: British schoolchildren carrying cardboard gas mask boxes and wearing luggage labels as they walk along a London railway platform during wartime evacuation, 1940 (Imperial War Museum HU 36871).
ABOUT THIS UNIT YEARS 5 & 6 HISTORY

Between 1939 and 1945, the Second World War changed everyday life for millions of families. In Britain, children carried gas masks in cardboard boxes, left cities with brown luggage labels pinned to their coats, sheltered from night air raids, and grew vegetables in back gardens. Across Europe, Jewish children lost their rights step by step before brave rescue missions brought thousands to safety in Britain—both before the war on the Kindertransport and afterwards beside Lake Windermere.

Your Journey Through World War II

Eleven Historical Enquiries into How War Changed Children’s Lives
Lesson 1: Outbreak 1939 & Home Front Mobilisation Page 4
“Why did ordinary British families carry cardboard boxes and tape their windows in September 1939?”

On a sunny Sunday morning in September 1939, millions of British families gathered around their radios to hear that Britain was at war. Within minutes, air raid sirens wailed across London, and every person — even babies — had a gas mask to carry. Why did ordinary families have to cover every window and paint street kerbs white?

Lesson 2: Operation Pied Piper & British Evacuation Page 5
“What was it like for 1.5 million British children to be sent away from home with a brown paper luggage label?”

Starting on 1 September 1939, 1.5 million children marched to city railway stations with cardboard name labels pinned to their coats and gas mask boxes over their shoulders. Sent away to stay with strangers in the countryside, some found kindness and green fields, while others felt deeply homesick.

Lesson 3: Pre-War Life & Rights Lost (1933–1938) Page 6
“What was everyday life like for Jewish children in Europe before 1933, and how were their rights taken away step by step?”

Before World War II, millions of Jewish children across Europe played in football teams, went on seaside holidays, and carried sweet cones on their first day of school. After 1933, Nazi laws in Germany took away their everyday rights step by step — until Jewish children were even banned from their own classrooms.

Lesson 4: Vera's Journey & The Kindertransport (1938–1939) Page 7
“How did 10,000 refugee children travel alone across Europe to safety in Britain in 1938–1939?”

After the violent attacks of November 1938, Britain agreed to welcome nearly 10,000 unaccompanied refugee children on rescue trains and ferries called the Kindertransport. Nine-year-old Vera Löwyová left Prague with one small suitcase, her diary, and her porcelain doll to begin a new life in England.

Lesson 5: Who Were the Rescuers? (1938–1939) Page 8
“How did ordinary volunteers like Nicholas Winton and Trevor Chadwick rescue 669 children from Prague before war broke out?”

In December 1938, a young London stockbroker named Nicholas Winton cancelled his skiing holiday to join brave volunteers in Prague. Working in a race against time before war broke out, their team organised eight trains that rescued 669 children — and Winton kept his scrapbook secret in his attic for nearly fifty years.

Lesson 6: The Blitz & Air Raids (1940–1941) Page 9
“How did ordinary families protect themselves when bombs fell night after night during the Blitz?”

From September 1940, German bombers attacked London for 57 nights in a row and bombed ports and factory cities across Britain. Families sheltered in garden Anderson shelters, indoor steel Morrison cages, and deep Underground stations while firefighters and volunteers risked their lives above ground.

Lesson 7: Dig for Victory & Wartime Rationing (1940–1945) Page 10
“Why did the British government ration butter, sugar, and meat, and ask families to "Dig for Victory"?”

Before 1939, island Britain brought in over 70% of its food by ship. When German submarines attacked food convoys in the Atlantic Ocean, Britain introduced ration books so rich and poor received fair shares — and turned flower gardens, school fields, and parks into vegetable allotments!

Lesson 8: Women at War (1941–1945) Page 11
“How did women on farms, in forests, and in factories keep Britain fighting during the Second World War?”

With millions of men away in the armed forces, Britain became the first country in the world to call up women by law for war work in December 1941. Women drove farm tractors, felled forest timber, filled explosive shells, welded bombers, and even flew brand-new Spitfires to RAF airfields!

Lesson 9: Secret Codebreakers & Bletchley Park (1941–1945) Page 12
“How did secret codebreakers working in wooden huts at Bletchley Park help shorten the Second World War?”

Deep in the Buckinghamshire countryside at a Victorian house codenamed "Station X", 10,000 mathematicians, puzzle-solvers, and Wrens worked in total secrecy. By building clever machines to crack Nazi Germany's Enigma codes, they saved countless ships and shortened World War II by at least two years.

Lesson 10: VE Day, Rebuilding Britain & Remembrance (1945) Page 13
“Why was VE Day on 8 May 1945 a day of both joyful street parties and quiet sadness for British families?”

On 8 May 1945 — Victory in Europe (VE) Day — church bells rang out, blackout curtains came down, and children sat at long street-party tables decked in bunting. Yet with bombed homes to rebuild and loved ones lost across Britain and the Commonwealth, peace also brought a lasting duty to remember.

Lesson 11: After the War — The Windermere Children (1945) Page 14
“How did 300 child survivors begin to rebuild their lives by Lake Windermere in August 1945?”

In August 1945, ten converted Royal Air Force bombers flew 300 orphaned Jewish child survivors out of war-torn Europe to wooden chalets beside Lake Windermere in Cumbria. Surrounded by mountain air, patient teachers, and shiny red bicycles, they slowly healed and built remarkable lives in Britain.

LESSON 1 • READING CHAPTER 1939–1945

Outbreak 1939 & Home Front Mobilisation

Enquiry Question “Why did ordinary British families carry cardboard boxes and tape their windows in September 1939?”
Part 1 The Sunday Morning Radio Broadcast (3 September 1939)

On the sunny morning of Sunday 3 September 1939, millions of British families gathered quietly around wooden wireless radios in their living rooms. Two days earlier, on 1 September, Adolf Hitler's German army had invaded Poland. At 11:15am, Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain spoke from 10 Downing Street: "This country is at war with Germany."

Just fifteen minutes after Chamberlain finished speaking, a loud wailing air raid siren echoed across London. Families hurried into cellars and streets while staring nervously at the sky. Although that first warning turned out to be a false alarm caused by a friendly aircraft, everyone now knew that modern war would reach ordinary homes.

Part 2 38 Million Gas Masks on Shoulder Strings

Remembering the deadly poison gas used during the First World War, the British government feared enemy aircraft might drop gas bombs on crowded towns. Before war was even declared, officials handed out 38 million free gas masks so every civilian was protected.

Adults and schoolchildren carried their black rubber respirators everywhere inside square cardboard boxes slung over their shoulders on string. Toddlers were given bright red-and-blue "Mickey Mouse" gas masks to make them less frightening, while babies were placed inside enclosed protective gas helmets with a hand pump. At school, children practised putting on their masks in seconds despite the hot rubber smell and misting eyepieces.

Part 3 The Blackout & Volunteer ARP Wardens

Two nights before war was declared — on 1 September 1939 — Britain switched off its lights. Under strict Blackout rules, every streetlight was turned off, car headlamps were fitted with narrow slotted hoods, and families hung thick black curtains over every window so German night bombers could not spot glowing cities from above.

Because pitch-dark streets caused bumps and road accidents, councils painted white stripes along kerbs, tree trunks, lampposts, and gateposts to guide pedestrians. Over 1.4 million volunteer Air Raid Precautions (ARP) wardens, wearing black steel helmets painted with a white "W", patrolled neighbourhoods each night calling out, "Put that light out!" whenever a gap shone through a curtain.

LESSON 2 • READING CHAPTER 1939–1945

Operation Pied Piper & British Evacuation

Enquiry Question “What was it like for 1.5 million British children to be sent away from home with a brown paper luggage label?”
Part 1 Operation Pied Piper Begins (1 September 1939)

Two days before Britain declared war, the government launched Operation Pied Piper — the biggest movement of people in British history. Over three busy days starting on 1 September 1939, around 1.5 million schoolchildren, teachers, and mothers with very young infants were evacuated from crowded industrial cities that bombers might target.

Children walked in school lines to railway stations carrying a gas mask box, a packet of sandwiches, and one small bag of spare clothes. Each child wore an official cardboard luggage label pinned to their coat showing their name, school, and home address. To stop platforms becoming dangerously overcrowded and panicked, parents had to kiss their children goodbye outside the station barriers.

Part 2 The Village Hall & The Billeting Officer

Steam trains carried tired children for hours without knowing their exact destination until they arrived in quieter rural counties such as Devon, Cornwall, Norfolk, or Wales. From country stations, evacuees were led to the local church or village hall for drinks and biscuits.

There, a local volunteer called the Billeting Officer had the difficult job of matching every child to a host family. Country householders were legally required to take in evacuees if they had spare bedrooms, receiving a small weekly government payment for food. Many children remembered feeling nervous and unwanted as strangers chose who to take home, especially brothers and sisters hoping not to be split up.

Part 3 Countryside Shocks & Mixed Experiences

Life in the countryside brought huge surprises for both city children and country hosts. Many children from smoky city terraces had never seen live cows being milked, pigs in muddy pens, or apples growing on trees rather than stacked in shop crates. Some hosts were shocked by how thin poorer inner-city children looked.

Experiences varied greatly. Thousands of evacuees were treated like family, enjoying fresh air, country walks, and warm beds, and stayed in touch with their hosts for life. Others felt lonely, missed their parents terribly, or faced strict households. When no bombs fell during the quiet "Phoney War" of late 1939, thousands returned home — only to be evacuated again when heavy bombing began in 1940.

LESSON 3 • READING CHAPTER 1939–1945

Pre-War Life & Rights Lost (1933–1938)

Enquiry Question “What was everyday life like for Jewish children in Europe before 1933, and how were their rights taken away step by step?”
Part 1 Ordinary Childhoods Across Europe Before 1933

Before the Second World War, over 9 million Jewish people lived across Europe in busy cities, market towns, and villages. In Germany, where Jewish families formed less than one percent of the population, many had lived as proud citizens for centuries — working as shopkeepers, doctors, teachers, musicians, and factory workers.

Jewish children enjoyed the same everyday childhoods as their classmates. They played in local football teams, rode bicycles, joined scout groups, and went on family holidays. On their very first day of primary school, six-year-olds proudly carried a Schultüte — a tall, brightly decorated cardboard cone filled with sweets, fruit, and new pencils.

Part 2 Unfair Laws Step by Step (1933–1937)

Everything began to change in January 1933, when Adolf Hitler and the Nazi Party came to power in Germany. Spreading racist lies that blamed Jewish people for Germany's problems, the Nazi government passed over 400 anti-Jewish laws that took away basic civil rights one small step at a time.

In April 1933, Nazi stormtroopers stood outside Jewish-owned shops ordering shoppers to stay away. In 1935, the Nuremberg Laws stripped German Jews of their citizenship. Soon, cruel rules crept into children's daily lives: Jewish families were banned from public parks, swimming pools, and cinemas, forced to hand over bicycles and radios, and even forbidden from keeping family pets.

Part 3 Banned from School & November 1938

Discrimination reached inside classrooms too. Jewish teachers were sacked from state schools, and some former friends stopped speaking to Jewish classmates out of fear or Nazi propaganda. Children were sometimes forced to sit on separate benches or excluded from school sports.

In November 1938, Nazi gangs launched the violent November Pogrom (Kristallnacht) across Germany and Austria, burning synagogues, smashing thousands of shop windows, and arresting 30,000 Jewish men. Days later, on 15 November 1938, the Nazi Education Minister completely banned all Jewish children from attending German state schools. Terrified parents realised they had to find a way to get their children out of danger.

LESSON 4 • READING CHAPTER 1939–1945

Vera's Journey & The Kindertransport (1938–1939)

Enquiry Question “How did 10,000 refugee children travel alone across Europe to safety in Britain in 1938–1939?”
Part 1 Britain Agrees to a Rescue (November 1938)

British newspapers and radio broadcasts reported shocking accounts of the November Pogrom (Kristallnacht) in November 1938. Deeply moved by appeals from Jewish, Quaker, and Christian charities, the British Parliament agreed to speed up travel permits so endangered refugee children under seventeen could enter Britain.

Between December 1938 and September 1939, this rescue operation — known as the Kindertransport (German for "Children's Transport") — brought nearly 10,000 mostly Jewish children to safety from Germany, Austria, Czechoslovakia, and Poland. However, parents were not allowed to come with them, and every child needed a British sponsor or charity to guarantee £50 towards their care.

Part 2 Nine-Year-Old Vera's One Small Suitcase

One of those children was nine-year-old Vera Löwyová (later Vera Schaufeld), who grew up in a loving family in Czechoslovakia. After Nazi troops marched into Prague in March 1939, Vera's parents made the agonising decision to put her name down for a rescue train to England so she would be safe.

Each child was allowed only one small suitcase, ten Reichsmarks, and no jewellery or valuables. Vera packed warm clothes, her little autograph diary signed by her parents, and her treasured English porcelain doll. In May 1939, wearing a numbered card around her neck, Vera waved through the train window at Prague's Wilson Station, bravely promising her mother and father not to cry.

Part 3 Across the Sea to Liverpool Street Station

For two days Vera's train rolled across Germany. When the train finally crossed the border into the Netherlands, Dutch women met the children at the station with trays of hot chocolate, white bread, and smiles — the first moment the children felt safe from Nazi guards. From the Hook of Holland, they sailed overnight by ferry to Harwich in Essex.

Steam trains carried almost all Kindertransport arrivals into London's Liverpool Street Station, where kind British foster families waited to take them home. Although Britain gave them safety, education, and care, war slammed borders shut in September 1939. Tragically, like many Kindertransport refugees, Vera never saw her parents again.

LESSON 5 • READING CHAPTER 1939–1945

Who Were the Rescuers? (1938–1939)

Enquiry Question “How did ordinary volunteers like Nicholas Winton and Trevor Chadwick rescue 669 children from Prague before war broke out?”
Part 1 A Cancelled Skiing Holiday (December 1938)

In December 1938, 29-year-old London stockbroker Nicholas Winton packed his bags for a skiing holiday in Switzerland. Just before leaving, he received an urgent phone call from his friend Martin Blake in Prague, Czechoslovakia, asking him to come and see a growing refugee crisis instead.

Tens of thousands of Jewish and anti-Nazi families had fled into Prague after Germany seized the borderlands, living in freezing camps as winter set in. Refusing to remain a bystander who looked away, Winton chose to become an upstander. Using his dining table in a Prague hotel as an office, he began taking down the names and photographs of desperate children whose parents begged him to get them out.

Part 2 The Rescue Chain in Prague & London

Saving hundreds of children was never the work of one person alone — it relied on a courageous team working for the British Committee for Refugees from Czechoslovakia. British economist Doreen Warriner coordinated dangerous relief work across Prague, while Trevor Chadwick, a Latin schoolteacher from Dorset, stayed in Prague after Nazi troops occupied the city to escort frightened children past armed guards onto the trains.

Back in London, Winton worked at the Stock Exchange by day and ran the rescue campaign every evening with his determined mother Babi. Together they pestered the Home Office for visas, printed children's photo cards in British newspapers, and found hundreds of generous British foster families and £50 sponsors.

Part 3 669 Children Saved & The Attic Scrapbook

Between March and August 1939, eight trains organised by the Prague rescue team — including Vera's May 1939 train — brought 669 children safely to Liverpool Street Station in London. Heartbreakingly, the largest ninth train, due to leave Prague with 250 children on 1 September 1939, was stopped when Germany invaded Poland and borders slammed shut.

After the war, Winton quietly got on with family life and hardly ever mentioned Prague. Nearly fifty years later, in 1988, his wife Grete discovered his dusty 1939 scrapbook of all 669 children's names and foster addresses in their attic. Invited onto the BBC programme That's Life!, Winton was astonished when dozens of grown-up men and women in the audience stood up around him — all alive because of his team's courage.

Key Words (pp. 17–18): Upstander (Up-stand-er)Bystander (By-stand-er)Rescue Chain (Res-cue Chain)Visa (Vi-sa)

LESSON 6 • READING CHAPTER 1939–1945

The Blitz & Air Raids (1940–1941)

Enquiry Question “How did ordinary families protect themselves when bombs fell night after night during the Blitz?”
Part 1 Black Saturday & 57 Nights of Bombing (September 1940)

On the afternoon of Saturday 7 September 1940 ("Black Saturday"), over 300 German Luftwaffe bombers roared up the River Thames and dropped high-explosive and incendiary bombs onto London's East End docks. This began The Blitz (short for the German word Blitzkrieg, meaning "lightning war"), as Hitler tried to force Britain to surrender.

London was bombed for 57 nights in a row, while heavy raids also struck ports and factory cities across the UK, including Coventry, Liverpool, Plymouth, Birmingham, Manchester, Bristol, Hull, Clydebank, and Belfast. Through thick smoke and flames, St Paul's Cathedral survived thanks to volunteer firewatchers guarding its great dome.

Part 2 Anderson & Morrison Shelters at Home

To protect civilians at home, the government supplied over 2 million corrugated steel Anderson shelters free to lower-income families with gardens. Families dug a pit 3 feet deep in the lawn, bolted six curved steel sheets together, and piled 15 inches of soil on top — often planting lettuces, marrows, and flowers over the roof! Although damp and freezing on winter nights, Anderson shelters absorbed deadly bomb blast waves.

Millions of city families lived in terraced streets or flats with no garden. From early 1941, they were issued indoor Morrison shelters — heavy steel-mesh cages shaped like large dining tables. Families ate meals on top by day and crawled inside the strong cage at night in case their house roof collapsed.

Part 3 Tube Station Platforms & Rooftop Volunteers

In London, over 150,000 people sheltered deep underground every night on Tube station platforms. Families queued with blankets, pillows, and thermos flasks, turning curved platforms into busy underground dormitories fitted with triple bunk beds, first-aid posts, and even lending libraries.

Meanwhile, ordinary citizens worked bravely above ground. Men and women of the Auxiliary Fire Service (AFS) fought raging dockland blazes, rooftop firewatchers doused burning incendiary bombs using sandbags and hand-pumped stirrup pumps, and the Women's Voluntary Services (WVS) drove mobile canteens into bombed streets each morning to serve hot sweet tea and find temporary homes for bombed-out neighbours.

LESSON 7 • READING CHAPTER 1939–1945

Dig for Victory & Wartime Rationing (1940–1945)

Enquiry Question “Why did the British government ration butter, sugar, and meat, and ask families to "Dig for Victory"?”
Part 1 The Atlantic Lifeline & Why Rationing Started

In 1939, island Britain imported over 70% (more than two-thirds) of its food — including wheat, sugar, butter, bacon, tea, and fruit — on merchant cargo ships sailing across the Atlantic Ocean. As soon as war began, packs of German U-boat submarines hunted Allied convoys, sinking hundreds of ships in an attempt to starve Britain into surrender.

If scarce food had simply risen in price, wealthy shoppers could have bought everything while poorer families went hungry. Instead, in January 1940, the Ministry of Food introduced rationing under the motto "Fair Shares for All", starting with bacon, butter, and sugar and soon covering meat, tea, jam, cheese, sweets, soap, and clothes.

Part 2 Ration Books, Weekly Allowances & Fair Shares

Every man, woman, and child was given a personal paper ration book filled with coupons and had to register with a local butcher, baker, and grocer. Shopkeepers snipped or stamped a coupon alongside the cash payment. A typical adult weekly ration allowed just one fresh egg, 50g of butter, 50g of cheese, and two rashers of bacon, supplemented with dried powdered egg and tinned Spam.

Expectant mothers and children under five received special green ration books granting extra milk, free cod liver oil, and concentrated orange juice for vitamins. Deliberately wasting edible food became a crime punishable by a heavy fine or prison, and inspectors cracked down on the illegal Black Market, where cheats traded goods secretly without coupons.

Part 3 Dig for Victory, Potato Pete & Healthier Children

Because home-grown fruit and vegetables were never rationed, the government launched the famous "Dig for Victory" campaign. Families dug up rose gardens, village greens, railway embankments, school playing fields, and even the dry moat around the Tower of London, creating 1.4 million allotments by 1943.

Cheerful cartoon characters Potato Pete and Doctor Carrot encouraged children to eat filling British potatoes and carrots instead of imported wheat bread (even serving carrot fudge on sticks instead of rationed sweets!). Families cooked meat-free Lord Woolton Pie — named after Food Minister Lord Woolton — out of root vegetables and oatmeal crust. Remarkably, eating less sugar and fat and far more fresh vegetables meant British children grew taller and healthier during the war than ever before!

LESSON 8 • READING CHAPTER 1939–1945

Women at War (1941–1945)

Enquiry Question “How did women on farms, in forests, and in factories keep Britain fighting during the Second World War?”
Part 1 Calling Up Women for War Work (December 1941)

Before 1939, most married women in Britain were expected to give up paid jobs to look after the home. Yet as millions of men left to fight overseas, farms, railways, shipyards, and aircraft factories faced an urgent shortage of workers.

In December 1941, Parliament passed the National Service Act, making conscription compulsory for unmarried women aged 20 to 30 — the first time in British history women were legally called up for war service. Women could choose between farming, factory engineering, or joining the uniformed women's branches of the armed forces. In 1945, even 18-year-old Princess Elizabeth (later Queen Elizabeth II) trained in overalls as an ATS ambulance driver and lorry mechanic.

Part 2 Land Girls & Lumberjills in Fields and Forests

To stop Britain starving while U-boats prowled the seas, over 80,000 women joined the Women's Land Army (WLA). Wearing green jumpers, brown corduroy breeches, and heavy Wellington boots, "Land Girls" worked from dawn to dusk in all weathers — driving tractors, ploughing fields, milking cows, digging drainage ditches, harvesting wheat, and catching barn rats.

Thousands more joined a tough forestry branch called the Women's Timber Corps, nicknamed the "Lumberjills". Swinging heavy axes and two-person saws in Scottish, Welsh, and English woods, Lumberjills measured and felled tall pine trees needed for coal-mine pit props, telegraph poles, and military crates.

Part 3 Canary Girls & Spitfire Ferry Pilots

Nearly a million women worked in noisy munitions factories making bullets, bombs, and artillery shells. Workers packing toxic TNT and cordite explosive powder were nicknamed "Canary Girls" because the chemicals stained their skin and hair bright yellow! Every shift carried real danger: a single metal spark could trigger an explosion, so workers removed hairpins and wore wooden-soled shoes.

High in the clouds, 168 women pilots flew for the Air Transport Auxiliary (ATA), including famous pilot Maureen Dunlop. Navigating only by paper maps and landmarks without radios or gunners, ATA women delivered newly built Spitfires, Hurricanes, and four-engine Lancaster bombers straight from factory runways to frontline RAF squadrons — and from 1943 earned the same pay as male ATA pilots.

LESSON 9 • READING CHAPTER 1939–1945

Secret Codebreakers & Bletchley Park (1941–1945)

Enquiry Question “How did secret codebreakers working in wooden huts at Bletchley Park help shorten the Second World War?”
Part 1 The "Unbreakable" German Enigma Machine

During World War II, German generals, airfields, and U-boat captains sent thousands of top-secret battle orders every day over invisible radio waves. Anyone with a radio aerial could hear the Morse code beeps — yet the letters were scrambled into gibberish by a portable typewriter-like device called the Enigma machine.

Inside every Enigma box sat spinning wired wheels (rotors) and an electrical plugboard at the front. Pressing the letter "A" might light up "Q" first, then "M" the very next time! Altogether there were over 158 million million million possible ways to set the machine, and German operators switched to a brand-new setting on their code sheets at midnight every night.

Part 2 Station X, Alan Turing & The Bombe Machine

Just before war broke out in 1939, brilliant Polish mathematicians secretly shared their early Enigma research with Britain. British intelligence gathered puzzle experts, chess champions, linguists, and mathematicians at a quiet country estate in Buckinghamshire called Bletchley Park, codenamed "Station X".

Testing millions of settings by hand before midnight was impossible. Mathematician Alan Turing and fellow codebreaker Gordon Welchman invented a wardrobe-sized electromechanical machine called the Bombe. Using clever guesses — such as predictable daily German weather forecasts — spinning Bombe drums ruled out wrong combinations in minutes! Later in 1944, Post Office engineer Tommy Flowers built Colossus at Bletchley: the world's first programmable electronic computer.

Part 3 7,500 Women & A Thirty-Year Secret

By 1945, around 10,000 staff worked day and night in wooden huts across Bletchley Park — and around 75% (three-quarters, or 7,500 workers) were women, including thousands of Wrens from the Women's Royal Naval Service. Working noisy eight-hour shifts in hot machine rooms, they wired heavy Bombe drums, intercepted radio signals, and translated decoded messages.

Every worker signed the Official Secrets Act, promising never to breathe a word — even telling their own parents they did dull typing jobs! Kept secret until the 1970s, Bletchley's decoded intelligence (codenamed Ultra) steered food convoys safely past U-boat traps, aided the D-Day landings, and historians estimate it shortened World War II by at least two years, saving millions of lives.

LESSON 10 • READING CHAPTER 1939–1945

VE Day, Rebuilding Britain & Remembrance (1945)

Enquiry Question “Why was VE Day on 8 May 1945 a day of both joyful street parties and quiet sadness for British families?”
Part 1 Victory in Europe (VE) Day (8 May 1945)

After nearly six years of conflict in Europe, Nazi Germany surrendered unconditionally. At 3:00pm on Tuesday 8 May 1945 — Victory in Europe (VE) Day — Prime Minister Winston Churchill announced over the radio that the war in Europe was over, before waving alongside King George VI and Queen Elizabeth to cheering crowds in Whitehall and Trafalgar Square.

Across towns and villages, long-silent church bells rang out and families tore down hated blackout curtains. Neighbours carried kitchen tables and chairs into the middle of roads, strung up red, white, and blue bunting, pooled rationed sugar and margarine to bake cakes for children's street parties, and danced round bonfires — while remembering soldiers still fighting Japan in Asia until VJ Day on 15 August 1945.

Part 2 Joy Mixed with Sorrow & Rebuilding Britain

Amidst the cheering, almost every street held quiet grief. Over 380,000 British service personnel and 67,000 British civilians had been killed, alongside millions across Europe and volunteers from across the British Commonwealth — including India, the Caribbean, Africa, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand — who fought beside Britain.

Peace did not make daily life easy overnight. Over 2 million British homes had been damaged or destroyed by bombs, Britain was heavily in debt, and food rationing continued until 1954. Determined to build a fairer future for every family after sharing wartime hardships together, Britain built hundreds of thousands of new council and prefab homes and in 1948 founded the free National Health Service (NHS).

Part 3 The Cenotaph & Why We Remember Today

Every autumn on 11 November (Armistice Day) and Remembrance Sunday, people across the United Kingdom gather at village and town war memorials and at The Cenotaph on Whitehall in London — the tall white stone monument whose name means "empty tomb".

At 11:00am, the nation pauses for a two-minute silence and wears the red poppy — first inspired by the wild poppies that grew across First World War battlefields and worn today to honour everyone who served and suffered in all conflicts. Remembering World War II reminds each new generation of the cost of war, the courage of ordinary people, and the duty to protect peace and human rights.

LESSON 11 • READING CHAPTER 1939–1945

After the War — The Windermere Children (1945)

Enquiry Question “How did 300 child survivors begin to rebuild their lives by Lake Windermere in August 1945?”
Part 1 The RAF Mercy Airlift from Prague (August 1945)

When war in Europe ended in May 1945, Allied soldiers liberated the Nazi concentration camps and found thousands of malnourished, orphaned Jewish teenagers and children whose families had been murdered in the Holocaust. With their old homes destroyed, they had nowhere safe to go.

Persuaded and funded by the Central British Fund for Jewish Relief, the British government agreed to offer sanctuary to child survivors. On 14 August 1945, 10 converted Royal Air Force Short Stirling bombers took off from Prague on a remarkable mercy flight — carrying 300 child survivors to RAF Crosby-on-Eden in Cumbria, where coaches drove them south into the peaceful green hills around Lake Windermere.

Part 2 Healing at Calgarth Estate: Bread Under Pillows & Red Bicycles

The children moved into quiet wooden chalets on the Calgarth Estate near Troutbeck Bridge, originally built for wartime flying-boat factory workers. Caring child psychologist Dr Oscar Friedman and art therapist Marie Paneth never forced the children to talk before they were ready. Having suffered years of starvation, many children secretly hid slices of bread under their pillows at bedtime because they could not believe food would still be there tomorrow.

Step by step, gentle kindness worked wonders. The children had hot baths, clean clothes, medical care, English lessons, painting studios, football matches, and swims in Lake Windermere. Best of all, local well-wishers donated shiny red bicycles, giving children who had been locked behind barbed wire the pure joy of cycling freely along country lanes.

Part 3 The '45 Aid Society & Keeping Real Voices Alive

Calling themselves "The Boys" (a nickname shared by both the boys and girls in the group), the Windermere children remained lifelong friends like one big family. Despite arriving in Britain with nothing and speaking little English, they grew up to become doctors, teachers, engineers, parents, grandparents, and even British Olympic weightlifter Sir Ben Helfgott.

In 1963, they founded The '45 Aid Society to raise money for charities and thank the country that sheltered them. In later years, survivors such as Arek Hersh MBE and Sam Laskier shared their oral history memories in schools and worked with children's author Tom Palmer on his novel After the War: From Auschwitz to Ambleside, ensuring future generations never forget how kindness can rebuild broken lives.
