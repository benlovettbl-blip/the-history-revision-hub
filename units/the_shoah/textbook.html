KEY ENQUIRY “Who were the Jews of Europe before the Second World War?”
STAGE 1 The European Mosaic: 9.5 Million Lives c. 1930s

In the early 1930s, approximately 9.5 million Jewish people lived across continental Europe, representing under two percent of the total population. European Jewry was immensely diverse, spanning distinct traditions, occupations, and vibrant civic cultures.

Over sixty percent lived in Central and Eastern Europe, predominantly across Poland, Hungary, Romania, and the Soviet Union, where millions spoke Yiddish as their mother tongue. Smaller communities flourished in Western Europe, including Germany, Britain, and France, contributing richly to science, arts, and civic life.

STAGE 2 Yiddishland: The Cultural Heartland of Eastern Europe c. 1930s

Across the countryside of Eastern Europe lay a distinct cultural world known as Yiddishland. Millions of Jews lived in rural shtetls (small market towns) and expanding cities like Warsaw and Kraków, creating a flourishing Yiddish culture with its own literature, theatre, and newspapers.

In cities like Vilna, celebrated as the "Jerusalem of the North", Jewish community life hummed with energy. World-renowned talmudic academies existed alongside modern socialist political parties, Zionist youth organisations, and avant-garde art collectives, creating a deeply rooted, vibrant civilization.

STAGE 3 Integration, Patriotism, and Civic Life in Central Europe 1871–1933

The experience of Jewish communities in Western and Central Europe was markedly different. Following emancipation in 1871, German & Western Jews were thoroughly assimilated: they spoke German, attended universities, and integrated deeply into professional, cultural, and political life.

During the First World War, over 100,000 German Jewish soldiers served loyally in the imperial armed forces, with 12,000 dying on European battlefields. German Jews viewed themselves not as outsiders, but as proud, patriotic citizens who contributed monumental achievements to science, industry, and the arts.

STAGE 4 The Sephardic World: Heritage of Southern Europe c. 1930s

Further south, across Greece, the Balkans, and Turkey, lived Europe’s Sephardic Jewish communities, whose ancestors had preserved their Spanish heritage after being expelled in 1492. For centuries, the port city of Thessaloniki (Salonica) in northern Greece was known as the "Mother of Israel", where over half the total population was Jewish. Sephardic Jews had their own Ladino dialect spoken at home and in markets, worked as dockers, merchants, customs officials, and academics, and observed the Sabbath so universally that the entire commercial Thessaloniki port closes on Saturdays. Their heritage enriched the Mediterranean with centuries of distinctive music, philosophy, and culinary traditions that lived peacefully alongside Christian and Ottoman neighbours.

STAGE 5 The Dark Shift: From Religious Anti-Judaism to Racial Antisemitism 19th–20th C.

For centuries, medieval Christian Europe harboured deep religious anti-Judaism, blaming Jewish communities for the death of Christ, restricting guild membership, and carrying out violent expulsions. However, in the late nineteenth century, a fatal ideological mutation occurred.

Theorists invented modern racial antisemitism, treating Jewish identity not as a religious faith that could be escaped through Christian baptism, but as pseudo-scientific biological racism carried in the blood. Adolf Hitler and the Nazi Party adopted this toxic racial theory as the foundation of their totalitarian worldview.

KEY ENQUIRY “How were German Jews progressively stripped of citizenship and rights (1933–1939)?”
STAGE 1 The Boycott and the Civil Service Purge 1933

When Adolf Hitler became Chancellor on 30 January 1933, the Nazi state wasted no time putting antisemitic ideology into practice. On 1 April 1933, the regime launched its first nationwide campaign: a boycott of Jewish-owned shops, department stores, doctors, and lawyers. Uniformed SA picket windows outside storefronts, holding placards warning Germans not to buy from Jews, while the word "Jude" was smudged in paint across display glass. A week later, on 7 April 1933, the regime enacted the Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service, ensuring Jewish judges, civil servants, and teachers were fired from their state positions.

STAGE 2 The 1935 Nuremberg Laws: Redefining Citizenship 1935–1936

In September 1935, during the annual Nazi Party rally, Hitler announced the Nuremberg Laws. These two decrees legally codified racial persecution into German law. The Reich Citizenship Law stripped German Jews of their citizenship, with citizenship revoked and Jews reduced to "subjects of the state". The Law for the Protection of German Blood and German Honour made intermarriage banned between Jews and German citizens, criminalising marriages and sexual relations. Officials used a formal grandparent blood chart to classify individuals based on ancestry: anyone with three or four Jewish grandparents was classified as a full Jew, regardless of whether they practised Judaism or had converted.

STAGE 3 Daily Exclusion and "Social Death" 1935–1938

Between 1933 and 1938, the Nazi regime issued over 400 separate anti-Jewish decrees to strip Jewish citizens of their livelihoods and rights. Systematic Aryanization of businesses forced Jewish owners to sell shops and factories to German buyers at rock-bottom prices.

These pervasive regulations enforced brutal 'social death' exclusion from public life. Jews were expelled from schools, barred from cinemas and parks, restricted to yellow benches, and forbidden from owning radios or pets, as former neighbours crossed the street to avoid greeting them.

STAGE 4 The November Pogrom "Kristallnacht", 9–10 November 1938

On the night of 9–10 November 1938, the Nazi regime orchestrated a nationwide pogrom known as Kristallnacht (the Night of Broken Glass). Propaganda Minister Joseph Goebbels unleashed the SA, SS, and Hitler Youth to launch violent coordinated assaults on Jewish communities.

Across forty-eight hours, over 1,400 synagogues were burned or destroyed, 7,500 businesses trashed, and ninety-one Jews murdered. The Gestapo arrested 30,000 Jewish men and sent them to concentration camps, while imposing a punitive one-billion-mark fine on the Jewish community to pay for the destruction.

STAGE 5 The Refugee Crisis: Closed Doors and the Kindertransport 1938–1939

Kristallnacht shattered any remaining illusion that Jews could survive under Nazi rule. Tens of thousands rushed to foreign consulates seeking exit visas, but the world closed its doors. In July 1938, delegates from 32 nations meet at the Évian Conference in France to address the refugee crisis, but country after country expressed verbal sympathy while maintaining closed global borders to Jewish immigration. One rare exception was the British Kindertransport programme, which allowed nearly 10,000 unaccompanied refugee children to be rescued and brought to safety in Britain between December 1938 and August 1939. While their lives were saved, most would never see their parents again.

KEY ENQUIRY “How did the outbreak of WWII transform Nazi anti-Jewish policy (1939–1941)?”
STAGE 1 The Conquest of Poland and Heydrich’s Plan 1939

On 1 September 1939, German Panzer divisions and Luftwaffe bombers invaded Poland, triggering the outbreak of the Second World War. Within weeks, the Nazi state found itself in control of over two million Polish Jews—a population ten times larger than Germany's remaining Jewish community. On 21 September 1939, Reinhard Heydrich, head of the SS Reich Security Main Office (RSHA), issued a secret directive known as the Schnellbrief (express letter). It ordered that all Jewish communities in rural areas and small towns be rapidly deported and subjected to rail hub concentration in major cities with direct railway junctions.

STAGE 2 Stigmatisation, Forced Labour, and the Yellow Star 1939–1940

Long before physical walls were constructed, the occupation authorities set about stripping Polish Jews of their livelihood and dignity. In November 1939, all Jewish people aged ten and older were ordered to wear a white armband bearing a blue Star of David, marked for humiliation and abuse. Tens of thousands of Jewish men were rounded up into brutal forced labour gangs to clear war rubble and dig ditches. Jewish bank accounts were frozen, businesses and factories were confiscated without compensation, and community leaders were arrested or shot.

STAGE 3 The Walls Go Up: The Warsaw and Łódź Ghettos 1940

In 1940, the Nazi regime began enclosing Jewish populations into sealed ghettos across occupied Poland, including Łódź, Kraków, and Lublin. In the capital, the Warsaw Ghetto became the largest in Europe. Enclosed behind a 3-metre brick wall topped with barbed wire, over 450,000 Jewish people were imprisoned inside just 1.3 square miles—an average density of seven to nine people per room (7–9 people per room). The ghetto was completely cut off from the outside world: phone lines were disconnected, postal deliveries stopped, and German police and armed guards patrolled every gate with shoot-to-kill orders.

STAGE 4 Starvation, Typhus, and the Child Smugglers 1940–1942

The Nazi administration weaponised disease and starvation to decimate the ghetto population. Official food rations allotted to Jews provided barely 180 to 220 calories a day—roughly eight percent of what an adult body needs to survive. Between 1940 and 1942, more than 80,000 people were dead of hunger and rampant disease in Warsaw alone, their bodies placed on pavements overnight covered with newspapers. The ghetto survived only through clandestine smuggling: brave child smugglers climbed through drainage grates or squeezed between wall bricks to bring flour, potatoes, and medicines into the ghetto, risking instant execution if caught by German police.

STAGE 5 Spiritual Resistance: The Secret Oneg Shabbat Archive 1940–1943

Imprisoned within the ghetto, Jewish people refused to surrender their humanity. They organized clandestine soup kitchens, secret schools for children, underground religious services, and cultural concerts. A remarkable group of underground historians and intellectuals led by Emanuel Ringelblum founded a secret archive codenamed Oyneg Shabes ('Joy of the Sabbath'). They methodically gathered diaries, official notices, and eyewitness reports to document the reality of persecution. Before the final destruction of the ghetto, the archive was buried in milk cans deep beneath Warsaw basements. Unearthed after the war, these buried milk cans ensured the truth outliving terror, preserving the voices of the victims forever.

KEY ENQUIRY “How and why did persecution turn into systematic mass murder (1941–1942)?”
STAGE 1 Operation Barbarossa: Ideological Annihilation June 1941

On 22 June 1941, Adolf Hitler launched Operation Barbarossa, sending over three million German and Axis troops in a surprise invasion of the Soviet Union. This invasion was conceived not merely as a conventional military campaign, but as a racial war of extermination (Vernichtungskrieg). Hitler and his generals had the notorious Commissar Order issued, instructing troops to execute captured Soviet political officers without trial, grounded in the virulent Judeo-Bolshevism myth—the absurd lie that Jews were the hidden puppet-masters behind Soviet communism.

STAGE 2 The Einsatzgruppen and Order Police Battalions Summer 1941

Following directly behind front-line Wehrmacht combat divisions in June 1941 were four mobile paramilitary death squads known as the SS Einsatzgruppen (A, B, C, and D). These execution units were reinforced by militarized Order Police battalions to round up and execute Soviet Jews.

By August 1941, German orders escalated to total annihilation, shooting entire communities of women and children beside pre-dug ravines. This horrific phase, known as the 'Holocaust by Bullets', murdered over 1.5 million Jewish civilians in face-to-face mass executions across Soviet territories.

STAGE 3 The Massacre at Babi Yar Kyiv, September 1941

The most notorious massacre of the Holocaust by Bullets occurred outside Kyiv in Ukraine. On 29 September 1941, Sonderkommando 4a & Order Police battalions marched tens of thousands of Jewish residents to a vast ravine, ordering them to surrender valuables and undress.

Victims were forced into the gorge and shot in layers atop one another. In this horrific Babyn Yar ravine massacre, exactly 33,771 shot in 36 hours were murdered, while German commanders sent meticulous administrative reports to Berlin documenting the mass execution.

STAGE 4 Massacres Across the Baltics and Local Collaboration Autumn 1941

Mass shootings swept across Lithuania, Latvia, Belarus, and Ukraine. Operating in the Baltic states, Einsatzgruppe A killing squads slaughtered nearly 250,000 Jewish victims within six months of the invasion, devastating centuries-old centers of Jewish learning.

In the Ponary Forest massacres outside Vilnius, over 70,000 Jewish victims were systematically executed in wooded pits. The German death squads relied heavily on local Baltic collaboration, using auxiliary police battalions from Lithuania and Latvia who volunteered to round up, guard, and shoot their Jewish neighbours.

STAGE 5 Perpetrator Strain and the Shift toward Industrial Murder Late 1941

By the winter of 1941, the Einsatzgruppen and police battalions had shot over 500,000 people. However, Nazi commanders recognized practical limits to mass shooting. When SS chief Heinrich Himmler became physically sick at Minsk witnessing a shooting in August 1941, he grew alarmed by shooter nervous breakdowns and heavy drinking among execution squads. Himmler ordered the development of a more industrialized method of killing—humane only from the perspective of the executioners. This led directly to the use of Chełmno exhaust vans in occupied Poland, where carbon monoxide exhaust was pumped into sealed truck compartments to asphyxiate victims, paving the way for the extermination camps.

KEY ENQUIRY “How did the "Final Solution" operate, and how did Jews resist?”
STAGE 1 The Wannsee Conference: The Bureaucracy of Murder 1942

On 20 January 1942, 15 senior civil servants, Nazi officials, SS generals, and government ministry directors gathered at a lakeside villa in Wannsee, a wealthy suburb of Berlin. Chaired by Reinhard Heydrich and recorded by Adolf Eichmann, the Wannsee Conference lasted barely ninety minutes. Its purpose was to coordinate logistics and secure full railway cooperation to execute the "Final Solution". Eichmann presented Eichmann’s 11 million list calculating that eleven million Jews across Europe were marked for eradication.

STAGE 2 Operation Reinhard: The Pure Death Camps 1942–1943

Following Wannsee, the SS launched Operation Reinhard—the top-secret code name for the extermination of all 2.2 million Jews in the General Government of occupied Poland. Three specialized extermination centres were constructed: Bełżec, Sobibór, and Treblinka. These sites had zero selection for labour; their sole purpose was immediate murder. Arriving trains were unloaded onto ramps disguised as transit stations, victims were told they were entering a shower, forced to undress, and directed into gas chambers. Dead within 2 hours of arrival, almost every man, woman, and child was murdered with carbon monoxide. Between them, these three camps murdered approximately 1.7 million Jewish people before being dismantled in 1943.

STAGE 3 Auschwitz-Birkenau: The Factory of Industrial Death 1942–1944

The largest and most lethal centre was Auschwitz-Birkenau in southern Poland. Unlike Operation Reinhard, Auschwitz functioned as both a massive slave-labour concentration camp and an extermination centre. Cattle trains from across Europe arrived at the railway ramp selection platform, where SS doctors conducted the selection. Those deemed fit for slave labour were tattooed and worked to exhaustion. The elderly, sick, and young children were sent directly to underground gas chambers, where pellets of Zyklon B were dropped through ceiling vents. At least 1.1 million murdered at Auschwitz, of whom approximately one million were Jews.

STAGE 4 The Warsaw Ghetto Uprising April–May 1943

Contrary to the offensive myth that victims went passively to their deaths, Jewish people mounted desperate armed resistance. When the SS entered the Warsaw Ghetto in April 1943 to deport the remaining 60,000 residents, they were ambushed. The Jewish Combat Organisation (ŻOB), led by 24-year-old Mordechai Anielewicz, and the Jewish Military Union (ŻZW) fought back. Using smuggled pistols vs German tanks, grenades, and petrol bombs, a few hundred young fighters held out for 27 days of defiance. The uprising was finally crushed only when General Jürgen Stroop burned the ghetto block by block.

STAGE 5 Revolts in the Death Camps and the Partisans 1943–1944

Resistance did not end in Warsaw. In the very heart of the death camps, prisoners mounted heroic revolts. At Treblinka in August 1943, prisoners conducted a daring Treblinka armoury raid, seizing rifles and grenades, setting the camp ablaze, and enabling 300 to escape. At Sobibór in October 1943, an underground committee led a Sobibór mass breakout of 300 inmates. In October 1944 at Auschwitz-Birkenau, members of the Jewish Sonderkommando staged an armed revolt using smuggled explosives, having Crematorium IV blown up before being killed.

KEY ENQUIRY “Who was responsible, who collaborated, and who tried to save?”
STAGE 1 The Bureaucracy of Destruction: Ordinary Professionals 1941–1945

In his monumental study The Destruction of the European Jews, historian Raul Hilberg demonstrated that the Holocaust relied on the entire modern state machinery. It depended upon desk murderers (Schreibtischtäter)—civil servants who drafted decrees, lawyers who stripped citizenship, and clerks who organized Reichsbahn train schedules. Crucially, the transport of millions of people was managed by the Deutsche Reichsbahn, with railway clerks calculating billing per passenger km tariffs to transport victims to death camps on time while German troops fought on the front lines.

STAGE 2 European Collaboration: An All-European Catastrophe 1940–1944

The Holocaust was an all-European crime that relied heavily on foreign collaborators. In France, the collaborationist Vichy regime enacted anti-Jewish laws and deployed French police to carry out the notorious Vichy Vel’ d’Hiv arrests in Paris in July 1942, arresting over 13,000 Jews. In Hungary in 1944, Hungarian authorities worked with Adolf Eichmann to ensure 437,000 Hungarians deported to Auschwitz in just 54 days. Without foreign police and railway staff, the SS could never have murdered across an entire continent.

STAGE 3 The Bystanders: Silence, Fear, and Complicity 1940–1945

The vast majority of people across Nazi-occupied Europe were neither active perpetrators nor heroic rescuers; they were bystanders. Millions stood watching neighbours taken away, publicly humiliated and marched to train stations. While many were intimidated by severe Nazi terror, others actively profited from persecution, attending municipal public auctions of stolen furniture and household goods, or moving into apartments stripped from deported families. As British historian Ian Kershaw famously observed, the "road to Auschwitz was built by hatred, but paved with indifference."

STAGE 4 Righteous Among the Nations: Courage Defying Death 1940–1945

In the face of terror, a courageous minority chose to act. In occupied Denmark in October 1943, when word leaked that the Gestapo planned to round up the country's 7,800 Jews, Danish citizens mobilized a spontaneous nationwide effort. Ordinary Danish fishermen and citizens hid their Jewish neighbours in hospitals and coastal villages, using small fishing boats to ferry over 7,200 Jews across the Øresund strait to neutral Sweden safe from deportation. Over 99% of the Danish community was saved. In southern France, the Protestant village of Le Chambon-sur-Lignon, led by pastor André Trocmé, sheltered an estimated 3,500 Jewish refugees.

STAGE 5 Allied Knowledge, Dilemmas, and the Question of Action 1942–1944

What did the Allied powers know, and when did they know it? In late 1942, Polish underground courier Jan Karski was smuggled into the Warsaw Ghetto and a transit camp, travelling secretly to London and Washington as a Jan Karski eyewitness to deliver testimony to Anthony Eden and President Roosevelt. In December 1942, the Allied governments issued a Dec 1942 Allied warning publicly condemning Germany's "bestial policy of cold-blooded extermination". However, Britain and the United States took virtually no military action to disrupt the death camps, prioritising total war victory over rescue missions and declining requests to bomb the railway lines to Auschwitz.

KEY ENQUIRY “What was lost, and what does survivor testimony teach us?”
STAGE 1 The Death Marches: The Final Spasm of Terror 1944–1945

In the autumn and winter of 1944–1945, as the Soviet Red Army closes in from the east and Western Allied armies crossed the Rhine, the Nazi leadership realized defeat was imminent. Rather than allowing camp inmates to be liberated, Heinrich Himmler ordered the desperate evacuation of camps. In freezing temperatures, the SS forced hundreds of thousands of starving, skeletal prisoners onto brutal death marches toward the interior of Germany. Wearing thin cotton prisoner uniforms and wooden clogs, inmates marched for days across sub-zero freezing roads without food or water. Over 70,000 shot or frozen in roadside ditches, with 200,000 to 250,000 dying overall in the final months of the war.

STAGE 2 Liberation: The World Confronts the Unimaginable Spring 1945

Allied soldiers were completely unprepared for what they discovered. Soviet troops liberated Majdanek in July 1944 and Auschwitz on 27 January 1945 (Holocaust Memorial Day). In the west, American forces entered Buchenwald and Dachau. On 15 April 1945, the British 11th Armoured enters Bergen-Belsen in northern Germany. Expecting a prisoner-of-war camp, British soldiers found 60,000 starving prisoners living alongside 13,000 unburied corpses decaying in the open air. The BBC correspondent Richard Dimbleby entered with the troops and as Dimbleby weeps on BBC radio, he broadcast: "I passed through the barrier and found myself in a world of a nightmare..." British army bulldozers had to be used to bury thousands of bodies in mass graves.

STAGE 3 The Painful Aftermath: Displaced Persons and Windermere 1945–1948

For the survivors, liberation did not bring sudden joy; it brought the agonizing reality of total loss. Many returned home to find their houses occupied by hostile neighbours. Over 250,000 in DP camps (Displaced Persons camps) in Germany and Austria waited for years. In 1947, the drama of the SS Exodus 1947 crisis—carrying Holocaust survivors turned away from Palestine by British warships—galvanized world opinion. This intensified the Zionist statehood demand for an independent homeland, leading directly to the founding of Israel in 1948. In August 1945, Britain also permitted 300 child survivors (the Windermere Children) to heal in the Lake District.

STAGE 4 Justice and International Law: Nuremberg and Lemkin 1945–1948

In November 1945, the Allied powers convened the International Military Tribunal (IMT) at Nuremberg to try senior Nazi leaders for war crimes. The court established that following superior orders was no legal defence, sentencing twelve defendants to death for crimes against humanity.

Simultaneously, Polish-Jewish legal scholar Raphael Lemkin, who lost forty-nine relatives in the Shoah, pioneered Raphael Lemkin's concept of genocide to define the deliberate destruction of a national group. Lemkin’s tireless campaign led directly to the 1948 United Nations Genocide Convention, establishing universal legal protections under international law.

STAGE 5 What Was Lost? The Imperative of Memory Post-1945

The Shoah extinguished six million human lives—two-thirds of European Jewry, including 1.5 million children. Beyond the staggering death toll, an entire millennium of Yiddish civilisation across Central and Eastern Europe was permanently destroyed.

As eyewitnesses pass away, preserving Shoah historical memory depends upon survivor oral testimony from voices like Elie Wiesel and Primo Levi. Upholding this imperative of remembrance requires every generation to confront antisemitism, defend human rights, and ensure the solemn pledge of "Never Again" endures across civilized society.

Smuggled gunpowder from munitions factories to Sonderkommando prisoners to blow up Crematorium IV in October 1944.

Lost 49 relatives in the Shoah; coined the legal term "genocide" and campaigned tirelessly for the 1948 UN Genocide Convention.

ESSENTIAL DISCIPLINARY CONCEPTS & TERMINOLOGY
Shoah Hebrew word meaning "catastrophe", used specifically for the Nazi genocide of six million European Jews.
Antisemitism Hostility, prejudice, or racial discrimination directed against Jewish people.
Nuremberg Laws (1935) Nazi laws stripping German Jews of citizenship and outlawing marriage with non-Jewish Germans.
Ghettoisation Forced confinement of Jewish populations into walled, overcrowded urban districts under starvation rations.
Einsatzgruppen Mobile SS killing squads that murdered over 1.5 million victims by mass shooting in Eastern Europe.
Operation Reinhard Code name for the 1942–43 extermination camps (Belzec, Sobibor, Treblinka) built solely for mass murder.
Sonderkommando Prisoners forced under threat of immediate death to work in camp crematoria; led the Oct 1944 Auschwitz revolt.
Bystanders & Collaborators Ordinary people, police, and local regimes who profited from, ignored, or actively assisted Nazi persecution.
