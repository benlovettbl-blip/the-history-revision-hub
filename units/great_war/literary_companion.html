Causes of the Great War (1871 – 1914) Enquiry 1 of 6 · True Historical Story
Nine European kings and emperors photographed together in ceremonial uniforms at Windsor Castle on 20 May 1910—four years before war tore their continent apart.
Enquiry 1 True Historical Story Historiographical Hook & Micro-History

The Sleepwalkers: From Versailles (1871) to Windsor Castle (1910)

After Sir Christopher Clark ("The Sleepwalkers", 2012)
Historical Context: In The Sleepwalkers (2012), historian Sir Christopher Clark argued that World War I was not plotted by one lone villain. Instead, fearful monarchs, generals, and diplomats across six capitals walked step by step into a catastrophe none of them truly foresaw.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Sleepwalkers[SLEEP-waw-kurz]
Christopher Clark’s metaphor for Europe’s 1914 leaders—watchful and calculating, yet blind to the industrial slaughter their choices would unleash.
Otto von Bismarck[OT-oh fon BIZ-mark]
Prussian Minister-President and first German Chancellor who unified Germany through "blood and iron" between 1864 and 1871.
Alsace-Lorraine[al-SAS loh-RAYN]
Iron-rich eastern French border provinces seized by Germany in 1871, fuelling forty-three years of French bitterness.
Revanche[ruh-VAHNSH]
French for "revenge"—the national campaign taught in French schools to recover Alsace-Lorraine from Germany.

"The protagonists of 1914 were sleepwalkers, watchful but unseeing, haunted by dreams, yet blind to the reality of the horror they were about to bring into the world."

Look closely at the nine royal cousins posing in gold braid at Windsor Castle in May 1910. Gathered for King Edward VII’s funeral, Britain’s new King George V sits just in front of his cousin, Kaiser Wilhelm II of Germany. Within four summers, their empires would be slaughtering one another across mud and barbed wire.

The fault line beneath their polished boots had cracked open forty years earlier. On 18 January 1871, after Prussian steel cannon crushed France at Sedan, Chancellor Otto von Bismarck staged a calculated humiliation. Inside the glittering Hall of Mirrors at Versailles—the proudest palace of French kings—German princes crowned Wilhelm I as Emperor of a mighty, unified Germany.

Worse still for peace, Berlin seized the iron-rich border provinces of Alsace and Lorraine. Across France, maps in every village classroom draped those lost lands in black mourning cloth under the cry of revanche. Bismarck spent nineteen years weaving careful treaties with Russia to keep angry France isolated. Once Kaiser Wilhelm II dismissed Bismarck in 1890 and dropped the Russian treaty, however, France allied with Russia—trapping Europe between two armed camps waiting for a spark.

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 Who were the "sleepwalkers" in the opening quote, and what makes the May 1910 Windsor Castle scene so haunting?
2 Why did crowning the German Emperor inside France’s Hall of Mirrors—and seizing Alsace and Lorraine—leave such lasting bitterness?
3 Once Kaiser Wilhelm II dismissed Bismarck in 1890 and dropped the Russian treaty, Europe was trapped between two armed camps. How can one leader’s decision to break an old agreement make war harder to avoid?

Enquiry 2 Classic Literature & Verse Classic Imperial Literature (1899)

Heart of Darkness: "Firing into a Continent" (1899)

Joseph Conrad (1899)
Historical Context: Polish-British sailor and novelist Joseph Conrad captained a river steamer in King Leopold II’s Congo in 1890. His 1899 novella Heart of Darkness stripped away polite European speeches to expose the greed, fever, and absurd violence of the Scramble for Africa.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Imperialism[im-PEER-ee-ul-iz-um]
Conquering and ruling overseas lands for raw materials, strategic ports, and national prestige.
Man-of-War[MAN-uv-WOR]
A heavily armed European warship used to bombard coastal targets and enforce colonial claims.
Lugubrious[loo-GOO-bree-us]
Dismal and mournful—used by Conrad to capture the grim absurdity of shelling empty jungle.
Weltpolitik[VELT-poh-lih-teek]
Kaiser Wilhelm II’s aggressive "World Policy" demanding overseas colonies and "a place in the sun" for Germany.

In 1899, former Congo steamboat captain Joseph Conrad exposed the greed and violence behind Europe’s race for African colonies.

"The conquest of the earth, which mostly means the taking it away from those who have a different complexion ... is not a pretty thing when you look into it too much. What redeems it is the idea only.

"I left in a French steamer, and she called in every blamed port they have out there, for, as far as I could see, the sole purpose of landing soldiers and custom-house officers. Once, I remember, we came upon a man-of-war anchored off the coast. There wasn’t even a shed there, and she was shelling the bush. It appears the French had one of their wars going on thereabouts.

"Her ensign dropped limp like a rag; the muzzles of the long six-inch guns stuck out all over the low hull. In the empty immensity of earth, sky, and water, there she was, incomprehensible, firing into a continent. Pop, would go one of the six-inch guns; a small flame would dart and vanish, a little white smoke would disappear, a tiny projectile would give a feeble screech—and nothing happened. Nothing could happen.

"There was a touch of insanity in the proceeding, a sense of lugubrious drollery in the sight. Somebody on board assured me earnestly there was a camp of natives—he called them enemies!—hidden out of sight somewhere. We gave her her letters (I heard the men in that lonely ship were dying of fever at the rate of three a day) and went on."

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 How does Joseph Conrad describe "the conquest of the earth" in his opening lines, and what were the only two groups the French steamer was landing at every port?
2 Why does the narrator see "a touch of insanity" in the French warship anchored off the African coast?
3 Notice how someone on board insists there is a camp of "enemies" hidden out of sight even though there is nothing on the shore. Why do you think people conquering distant lands often convince themselves that strangers are "enemies"?

Enquiry 3 Classic Literature & Verse Edwardian Spy & Invasion Novel (1903)

The Riddle of the Sands: In the Fog of the Frisian Channels (1903)

Erskine Childers (1903)
Historical Context: British yachtsman Erskine Childers sailed the muddy North Sea flats of northern Germany himself before publishing The Riddle of the Sands in 1903. His bestselling thriller captured British dread over Admiral Tirpitz’s expanding German Navy—even prompting the Admiralty to build new North Sea bases.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Frisian Islands[FREE-zhun EYE-lundz]
The chain of low sandbank islands and tidal channels along Germany’s North Sea coast.
Seagoing Lighters[SEE-goh-ing LYE-turz]
Huge flat-bottomed barges capable of carrying hundreds of troops across shallow coastal waters.
Binnacle Lamp[BIN-uh-kul lamp]
A hooded ship’s light illuminating the steering compass at night without giving away the yacht’s position.
HMS Dreadnought[aych-em-ess DRED-nawt]
Britain’s revolutionary 1906 all-big-gun turbine battleship that escalated the Anglo-German naval arms race.

In Erskine Childers’s bestselling 1903 thriller The Riddle of the Sands, young British yachtsmen Davies and Carruthers grope through thick North Sea fog behind the German island of Memmert to spy on secret coastal channels.

"’We must cross the See Gat and strike that boomed channel, the Memmert Balje. Strike it, freeze on to it—can’t cut off an inch—and pass that watershed before it’s too late. It’s an infernally bad one, I can see. Not even a dinghy will cross it for an hour each side of low water.’

"’What about getting back?’

"’We shall have a rising tide, anyway. If the fog lasts—can you manage in a fog and dark?’

"’The dark makes it no more difficult, if we’ve a light to see the compass and chart by. You trim the binnacle lamp—no, the riding-light. Now give me the scissors, and don’t speak a word for ten minutes. Meanwhile, think it out, and load the dinghy—by Jove! though, don’t make a sound.’"

Stowing away in the mist, Carruthers uncovers the Kaiser’s secret invasion rehearsal—explained in the novel’s closing memorandum.

"[The plan is] to despatch an army of infantry ... in big seagoing lighters, towed by powerful, shallow-draught tugs, under escort of a squadron of warships. Multitudes of seagoing lighters, carrying full loads of soldiers, not half loads of coals, should issue simultaneously from seven shallow outlets. Under escort of the Imperial Navy, they would traverse the North Sea and throw themselves bodily upon English shores."

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 Who are Davies and Carruthers in The Riddle of the Sands, and what secret plan hidden behind the German coast do they uncover?
2 How does Erskine Childers build suspense as Davies and Carruthers prepare their dinghy behind the German sandbanks?
3 The Riddle of the Sands was a bestselling 1903 spy thriller rather than a government report. Why would a dramatic story about German troop barges sneaking through the fog have made British readers so anxious?

Enquiry 4 True Historical Story 28 June 1914

Outside Schiller’s Delicatessen: Chauffeur Lojka’s Wrong Turn in Sarajevo (28 June 1914)

The History Revision Hub · Historical Story (28 June 1914)
Historical Context: By 1914, south-eastern Europe was nicknamed the "Balkan Powder Keg" as Slavic nationalism clashed with Austria-Hungary. On 28 June 1914 in Sarajevo, a failed bomb attack, a botched route change, and one wrong turn placed history’s deadliest spark at a teenager’s feet.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Sarajevo[sar-uh-YAY-voh]
Capital city of Bosnia-Herzegovina (annexed by Austria-Hungary in 1908), visited by Franz Ferdinand on 28 June 1914.
Leopold Lojka[LAY-oh-pold LOY-kuh]
The Czech chauffeur driving Archduke Franz Ferdinand’s car who was never clearly told that the return route had changed.
Gavrilo Princip[gav-REE-loh PRIN-tsip]
Nineteen-year-old Bosnian Serb nationalist armed by Belgrade’s Black Hand society who assassinated the Archduke.
Oskar Potiorek[OS-kar pot-ee-OR-ek]
Austro-Hungarian military governor of Bosnia who shouted to halt the car right beside Princip.

On the bright Sunday morning of 28 June 1914, six teenage Bosnian Serb conspirators armed by Belgrade’s secret "Black Hand" society waited along the riverfront Appel Quay in Sarajevo. At 10:10 a.m., Nedeljko Čabrinović hurled a hand bomb toward Archduke Franz Ferdinand’s open green-and-black touring car. The bomb bounced off the folded canvas hood and exploded beneath the car behind, injuring twenty people.

Shaken yet unhurt, the Austro-Hungarian heir insisted on driving to Sarajevo Hospital after a tense reception at Town Hall to visit his wounded officers. To keep the royal couple safe, officials hastily decided to speed straight along the wide riverside quay, avoiding the crowded city centre. Nobody, however, clearly explained that change of plan to Czech chauffeur Leopold Lojka.

At 10:45 a.m., reaching the Latin Bridge, Lojka dutifully followed the original morning schedule and swung right into narrow Franz Josef Street—just past Moritz Schiller’s corner delicatessen.

"What is this? This is the wrong way!" shouted Governor Oskar Potiorek from inside the car. "Drive straight down the Appel Quay!"

Lojka pressed the brake pedal hard to wrestle the heavy Gräf & Stift car into reverse gear. Five feet away on the pavement stood nineteen-year-old assassin Gavrilo Princip, bewildered to see his lost target roll to a dead stop right beside him. Princip stepped to the running board and fired two Browning pistol shots—killing Sophie and Franz Ferdinand, and snapping the alliance tripwires of Europe.

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 What chain of small human mistakes left Archduke Franz Ferdinand stopped just five feet in front of Gavrilo Princip?
2 Why did Archduke Franz Ferdinand get back into his open touring car after barely surviving the morning bomb attack?
3 Gavrilo Princip was bewildered to see his lost target roll to a halt right beside him. Looking at this story, how much do huge turning points in history depend on careful plans versus split-second accidents?

Enquiry 5 Classic Literature & Verse 1914 Patriotic Sonnet vs 1918 Trench Verse

1914 Illusion vs Trench Reality: "The Soldier" (1914) & "Suicide in the Trenches" (1918)

Rupert Brooke (1914) & Siegfried Sassoon (1918)
Historical Context: When war broke out in August 1914, young poet Rupert Brooke captured idealistic patriotic pride before dying of blood poisoning en route to Gallipoli in 1915. After enduring Somme and Arras mud as a decorated infantry officer, Siegfried Sassoon answered home-front cheering with blistering anger in 1918.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Rupert Brooke[ROO-purt BROOK]
Idealistic English poet whose late-1914 war sonnets celebrated noble sacrifice before he saw trench combat.
Siegfried Sassoon[SEEG-freed suh-SOON]
Decorated Royal Welch Fusiliers officer whose fierce 1917–1918 poems exposed trench horror and civilian complacency.
Crumps[KRUMPS]
Soldiers’ trench slang for heavy high-explosive German shells, named after the deep thudding sound of their blast.
Disillusionment[dis-ih-LOO-zhun-munt]
The bitter collapse of 1914 romantic ideals once soldiers faced industrial artillery, mud, lice, and trauma.

When Europe marched to war in late 1914, twenty-seven-year-old volunteer Rupert Brooke welcomed combat as a joyful moral cleansing and peaceful sacrifice. Three winters later, decorated frontline officer Siegfried Sassoon answered cheering crowds at home with the grim truth of the trenches.

Rupert Brooke, "The Soldier" (1914):
If I should die, think only this of me:
That there’s some corner of a foreign field
That is for ever England. There shall be
In that rich earth a richer dust concealed.
Her sights and sounds; dreams happy as her day;
And laughter, learnt of friends; and gentleness,
In hearts at peace, under an English heaven.
Siegfried Sassoon, "Suicide in the Trenches" (1918):
I knew a simple soldier boy
Who grinned at life in empty joy,
Slept soundly through the lonesome dark,
And whistled early with the lark.
In winter trenches, cowed and glum,
With crumps and lice and lack of rum,
He put a bullet through his brain.
No one spoke of him again.
You smug-faced crowds with kindling eye
Who cheer when soldier lads march by,
Sneak home and pray you’ll never know
The hell where youth and laughter go.

Read aloud side by side, these famous verses trace the arc of a whole European generation. Patriotic dreams of August 1914 dissolved in winter mud, lice, and industrial artillery.

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 How does Rupert Brooke’s 1914 sonnet picture a soldier’s death abroad, and what happens to the "simple soldier boy" in Siegfried Sassoon’s 1918 poem?
2 Compare the end of Brooke’s sonnet with the end of Sassoon’s poem: how do both poets use the word "laughter" in opposite ways?
3 In his closing verse, Sassoon points angrily at "You smug-faced crowds" cheering marching soldiers. Why do you think frontline soldiers sometimes felt so bitter toward people cheering the war at home?

Enquiry 6 Eyewitness Voice August 1914 Mobilisation Eyewitness Diary

An English Wife in Berlin: Watching the Mobilisation Trains Roll Out (August 1914)

Princess Evelyn Blücher & Sir Edward Grey (August 1914)
Historical Context: Englishwoman Evelyn Stapleton-Bretherton was married to German nobleman Count Gebhard Blücher. Forced to leave London as Britain and Germany went to war on 4 August 1914, she rode east across the frontier while non-stop troop trains carried singing recruits toward Belgium—capturing the exact moment railway timetables locked Europe into war.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Mobilisation[moh-bil-eye-ZAY-shun]
Calling up millions of reserve troops and seizing national railways on strict minute-by-minute war timetables.
Schlieffen Plan[SHLEE-fun plan]
Germany’s rigid war timetable ordering an immediate sweep through neutral Belgium to defeat France before Russia finished mobilising.
Evelyn Blücher[EV-uh-lin BLOO-kur]
English-born aristocrat married to a German prince whose wartime Berlin diary recorded August 1914 from both sides.
Fischer vs Clark[FISH-ur vur-sus KLARK]
The classic historical debate between Fritz Fischer (German aggression) and Christopher Clark (shared six-power miscalculation).

At dusk on 3 August 1914 in London, British Foreign Secretary Sir Edward Grey watched streetlamps flicker on outside Whitehall. "The lamps are going out all over Europe," he told a friend quietly. "We shall not see them lit again in our life-time."

Hours later, Englishwoman Evelyn Blücher boarded a special diplomatic train leaving Britain for Berlin with her German husband. Once Russia had mobilised on 30 July and Germany had launched the Schlieffen Plan into neutral Belgium, generals’ railway timetables seized total control from civilian diplomats. Crossing the frontier into Germany on 5–6 August, Evelyn recorded an unforgettable scene in her diary.

"Passing us in an almost continuous stream on the other track were the trains full of troops—shouting, laughing, singing, and waving their hands, with intense excitement on their faces. Every carriage was chalked with slogans and decked with oak leaves and summer flowers handed up by village girls along the line.

"At every station, brass bands thundered patriotic hymns. The young soldiers leaned from the windows crying that they would be home from Paris before the autumn leaves fell. Yet behind the cheering platforms, I saw older women and mothers standing pale as stone. Clutching wet handkerchiefs, they watched iron wheels roll their sons toward Belgium—each nation convinced that it alone fought in self-defence."

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 What did Sir Edward Grey warn as London’s streetlamps flickered on, and what two contrasting scenes did Evelyn Blücher spot on German railway platforms days later?
2 Why did generals’ railway timetables make it so hard for civilian diplomats to stop the crisis at the start of August 1914?
3 Evelyn ends her diary entry by noticing that each nation was "convinced that it alone fought in self-defence." How can rival countries march to war at the very same moment while every side believes it is only defending itself?

Parent, Carer & Tutor Conversation Guide

Model Talking Points
No Specialist History Knowledge Needed: Children never need word-for-word answers—praise curiosity and listen for these core ideas during your 3-minute chat!
Enquiry 1: Clark: Sleepwalkers p. 2
Q1: They were Europe’s 1914 leaders—watchful yet blind to the horror ahead—and the nine royal cousins posing together in gold braid (including Britain’s King George V and Germany’s Kaiser Wilhelm II) would be slaughtering one another across mud and barbed wire within four summers.
Q2: Staging the coronation inside the proudest palace of French kings humiliated France, and taking those iron-rich border provinces left French village classrooms draping maps in black mourning cloth under the cry of revanche.
Q3: Dropping the Russian treaty pushed Russia straight into an alliance with angry France, dividing Europe into two rival armed camps waiting for a spark.
Enquiry 2: Conrad: Heart of Darkness p. 3
Q1: He calls it taking land away from people of a different complexion—"not a pretty thing when you look into it too much"—and notes the steamer landed only soldiers and custom-house officers.
Q2: With not even a shed on the shore, the warship keeps firing six-inch shells uselessly into empty bush at unseen "enemies" while its own sailors are dying of fever at three a day.
Q3: Calling unseen local people "enemies" lets empires disguise greed and violence as a necessary fight, even when—as Conrad shows—the warship is simply "firing into a continent" for nothing.
Enquiry 3: Childers: Frisian Fog p. 4
Q1: They are two young British yachtsmen spying behind the German island of Memmert who uncover a secret German rehearsal to tow fleets of flat-bottomed barges packed with soldiers across the North Sea to invade England.
Q2: They have to slip across treacherous tidal channels before low water in thick North Sea fog and darkness, steering by compass light and keeping completely silent.
Q3: Picturing fleets of barges packed with soldiers issuing simultaneously from seven hidden German outlets and landing straight on English shores made a sudden sea invasion feel alarmingly real.
Enquiry 4: Wrong Turn in Sarajevo p. 5
Q1: After the 10:10 a.m. bomb attack, officials changed the return route without briefing chauffeur Leopold Lojka, who turned at the Latin Bridge and rolled to a dead stop trying to reverse outside Schiller’s delicatessen.
Q2: Shaken yet unhurt, he insisted on driving to Sarajevo Hospital after leaving Town Hall so he could visit his wounded officers.
Q3: The Black Hand plot had already failed once that morning, yet one forgotten message to a chauffeur gave Princip a sudden second chance whose two pistol shots snapped the alliance tripwires of Europe.
Enquiry 5: Brooke vs Sassoon p. 6
Q1: Brooke imagines death as peaceful, gentle sacrifice turning foreign soil "for ever England," whereas Sassoon shows a cheerful boy broken by winter trenches, shells ("crumps"), lice, and lack of rum until he ends his own life.
Q2: Brooke imagines peaceful "laughter, learnt of friends ... under an English heaven," whereas Sassoon calls the trenches "The hell where youth and laughter go."
Q3: People cheering from safety pictured noble glory without ever seeing the winter trenches, shellfire, and silent despair that broke young recruits—and left "no one" speaking of them again.
Enquiry 6: August 1914 Trains p. 7
Q1: Grey warned "The lamps are going out all over Europe"; days later, Evelyn saw young soldiers singing from carriages decked with oak leaves and flowers while pale mothers stood behind the platforms clutching wet handkerchiefs.
Q2: Once Russia mobilised on 30 July and Germany launched the Schlieffen Plan into neutral Belgium, strict minute-by-minute war timetables seized total control from diplomats and sent continuous streams of troop trains rolling west.
Q3: Fear of being caught off guard drove each side to put its mobilisation timetables into motion first, leaving ordinary soldiers and families at every station convinced their own country was simply fighting in self-defence.
