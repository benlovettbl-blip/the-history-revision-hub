The Deer-Mask Hunters of Star Carr and Sunken Doggerland

The History Revision Hub (from Star Carr & North Sea Archaeology)
Historical Context: Middle Stone Age families left no written books, yet wet peat in Yorkshire and fishing nets in the North Sea preserved astonishing clues about how hunters lived before Britain became an island.
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Mesolithic[mez-oh-LITH-ik]
The Middle Stone Age (c. 10,000–4000 BC), when warming forests spread across Britain after the Ice Age.
Doggerland[DOG-er-land]
The wide green land bridge that joined Britain to Europe before rising seas flooded it around 6500 BC.
Peat[PEET]
Dark, wet, airless marsh soil that stops ancient wood and bone from rotting away.
Flint[FLINT]
A hard grey stone that Stone Age makers chipped into razor-sharp blades and scrapers.

Around 9000 BC, mist curled across a reedy lake at Star Carr in North Yorkshire. Middle Stone Age families split heavy birch trunks and laid Britain's oldest wooden platform over squelching marshland.

Beside crackling birch-bark fires, skilled makers scraped twenty-one red-deer skulls smooth using sharp flint blades. Next, they bored two neat holes through the bone so leather straps could tie the antlers onto a person's head! Perhaps hunters wore those branching masks to creep close to wild deer, or danced in firelight during winter gatherings.

Back then, Britain was not an island at all. Families walked east from Yorkshire to mainland Europe across grassy plains called Doggerland. Hunters speared fat eels in winding rivers, netted wild ducks, and gathered sweet autumn hazelnuts.

Yet giant northern ice sheets kept melting faster every century. Around 6500 BC, rising waves and fierce storms drowned low Doggerland beneath the cold North Sea forever. Even today, fishing trawlers out at sea haul up mammoth teeth and flint tools from Britain's sunken past!

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. What clever changes did makers at Star Carr carve onto the twenty-one red-deer skulls?
2. Why might the people at Star Carr have tied heavy deer antlers onto their heads? Can you spot both ideas in the story?
3. Imagine living on grassy Doggerland as the sea crept closer and closer—what would your family miss most once Britain became an island?

Night of the Great Gale: Uncovering Skara Brae (1850)

The History Revision Hub (from Orkney Archaeological Records)
Historical Context: For thousands of years, grassy sand dunes hid Britain's best-preserved Neolithic village until one fierce Scottish winter storm blew the sand away overnight.
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Skara Brae[SKA-ruh BRAY]
A 5,000-year-old New Stone Age stone village on Orkney, uncovered by a fierce winter storm in 1850.
Neolithic[nee-oh-LITH-ik]
The New Stone Age (c. 4000–2500 BC), when Britain's first farmers settled in year-round villages.
Flagstones[FLAG-stohnz]
Flat coastal slabs of stone stacked neatly like bricks to build snug walls and furniture.
Hearth[HARTH]
The central stone fireplace inside a home where families cooked hot stews and kept warm.

One wild winter night in 1850, fierce gales lashed the Bay of Skaill on Orkney. Giant waves tore thick grass and sand dunes clean off the Scottish shore.

Next morning, local landowner William Watt walked along the battered bay and gasped in shock! Sticking out of the sand stood eight roofless stone homes older than Egypt's pyramids.

Around 3100 BC, New Stone Age farmers settled here at Skara Brae. Hardly any trees grew on windy Orkney. Instead, clever builders stacked flat coastal flagstones into snug houses linked by roofed passages. Mounds of earth around the walls blocked out icy sea winds.

Because every piece was stone rather than wood, nothing had rotted away! Inside each door stood a proud stone dresser to show off special bowls and beads. Beside a warm peat hearth sat stone box beds once stuffed with soft heather and sheepskins. Villagers even built indoor stone drains!

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. How was the hidden village of Skara Brae accidentally discovered in winter 1850?
2. Why did Skara Brae's furniture survive for five thousand years when most Stone Age furniture rotted away?
3. Would you have enjoyed staying the night in a Skara Brae stone house? What details in the story show these families cared about comfort and pride in their homes?

Enquiry 3 True Historical Story True Archaeological Story

Sledges, Sunbeams, and Midwinter Feasts at Stonehenge

The History Revision Hub (from Stonehenge & Durrington Walls Excavations)
Historical Context: Without wheels, horses, or metal tools, Neolithic communities hauled giant boulders across Britain and gathered near Stonehenge for midwinter feasts.
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Sarsen[SAR-sun]
Giant 25-tonne blocks of hard grey sandstone dragged twenty miles to build Stonehenge's outer circle.
Trilithon[TRY-lith-on]
A giant three-stone archway made from two tall upright boulders topped by one horizontal lintel stone.
Preseli Hills[preh-SEL-ee HILZ]
Mountains in west Wales from which builders hauled eighty spotted bluestones 140 miles to Stonehenge.
Solstice[SOL-stiss]
The longest summer day or shortest winter day of the year, aligned directly with Stonehenge's stones.

Picture dragging a grey sarsen stone heavier than four big elephants without one wheel or crane! Around 2500 BC on frosty Salisbury Plain, hundreds of Stone Age builders pulled thick plant ropes together.

Log rollers groaned under oak sledges hauling 25-tonne boulders twenty miles across green hills. Earlier teams had even moved eighty spotted bluestones 140 miles from the Preseli Hills in Wales! Patient masons pounded hard stone balls to shape neat pegs and sockets. Those joints locked giant three-stone trilithons high against the sky.

Where did all those hungry workers sleep at night? Two miles away at Durrington Walls, diggers found hundreds of warm chalk houses. Tests on animal teeth proved travellers drove fat pigs and cattle all the way from Scotland!

On the cold Midwinter solstice, crowds cheered as roast pork sizzled over bright fires. Right on cue, the setting winter sun glowed straight between Stonehenge's tallest stones!

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. How did Stone Age builders move 25-tonne sarsen boulders and lock the giant top stones safely in place without wheels or cranes?
2. What surprising clue did tests on animal teeth reveal at the nearby village of Durrington Walls?
3. Why do you think hundreds of people travelled all the way from Scotland and worked so hard together just to gather at Stonehenge on the shortest day of winter?

Stranger from the Snowy Alps: The Amesbury Archer (c. 2300 BC)

The History Revision Hub (from Wessex Archaeology Salisbury Museum Finds)
Historical Context: Digging foundations for a new Wiltshire primary school in 2002, archaeologists uncovered Britain's richest Bronze Age burial and unlocked his childhood secret through his teeth.
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Amesbury Archer[AYMZ-bree AR-cher]
A wealthy Bronze Age metalworker buried around 2300 BC near Stonehenge with over a hundred grave treasures.
Beaker People[BEE-ker PEE-pul]
Newcomers from mainland Europe around 2400 BC who brought bell-shaped clay pots and metalworking skills.
Smelting[SMEL-ting]
Heating crushed metal ore over a roaring charcoal fire until gleaming liquid metal runs free.
Enamel[ih-NAM-ul]
The rock-hard outer coating on human teeth that locks in chemical clues from childhood drinking water.

In spring 2002, builders started work on a new school at Amesbury near Stonehenge. Diggers stepped in and found the richest Bronze Age grave ever uncovered in Britain!

Curled inside an old timber tomb lay a strong man who died around 2300 BC. Years before, a bad fall had torn off his left kneecap. Every step he took must have hurt, yet friends honoured him like a king.

Around his bones lay over a hundred rich gifts! Five clay beakers stamped with neat comb lines sat beside sixteen sharp flint arrowheads and stone wristguards. Brighter still shone Britain's earliest gold hair rings, a smooth cushion stone, and three flat copper knives!

Where had this skilled stranger come from? Scientists tested childhood water clues locked inside his hard tooth enamel. Those tests proved the Amesbury Archer grew up near the snowy Alps in central Europe! He had crossed the sea bringing bright new metal skills to Britain.

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. What treasures in his grave explain why this man is called the 'Amesbury Archer', and what painful injury had he survived?
2. Which gleaming objects in his grave show he knew exciting new metal skills as well as shooting bows?
3. Even though every step hurt because of his missing kneecap, friends honoured this newcomer from the Alps like a king—why do you think he was so special to them?

Frozen in River Mud: The Day Must Farm Burned (c. 900 BC)

The History Revision Hub (from Cambridge Archaeological Unit Must Farm Dig)
Historical Context: Wood, cloth, and food normally rot away in British soil, yet a sudden Bronze Age house fire in Cambridgeshire dropped an entire village into preserving wet mud.
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Must Farm[MUST FARM]
A 3,000-year-old Bronze Age stilt village in Cambridgeshire nicknamed 'Britain's Pompeii' after sinking into wet mud.
Waterlogged[WAW-ter-logd]
Soaked completely in wet, airless mud so bacteria cannot rot ancient wood, cloth, or food.
Wicker[WIK-er]
Bendy willow twigs woven tightly together to make sturdy baskets, walls, and funnel-shaped eel traps.
Pompeii[pom-PAY]
A famous Roman city frozen in time by volcanic ash—giving Must Farm its nickname 'Britain's Pompeii'.

Around 900 BC in Cambridgeshire, Bronze Age families built five roundhouses on tall oak stilts above a slow river. Below their wooden floors, fishers dropped woven wicker traps to catch fat eels.

Less than a year later, sudden fire struck the village! Bright flames raced across dry straw roofs while thick smoke filled the rafters. Every family scrambled out safely into boats, leaving dinner pots right on the fire. Seconds later, burning timbers splashed straight into the river.

Soft, waterlogged river mud put out the flames at once and shut out all air. Nothing rotted away, freezing Must Farm in time like Britain's own Pompeii!

When diggers peeled back that dark mud, everyday life lay waiting. Shiny bronze axes still sat inside smooth ash handles beside woven cloth and glass beads. Nearby rested Britain's oldest complete wooden wheel. Best of all, one scorched clay bowl still held wheat porridge with its wooden spoon inside!

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. How did the families at Must Farm escape when fire suddenly swept across their stilt roundhouses?
2. Why did wooden handles, woven cloth, and even a bowl of porridge survive at Must Farm for three thousand years without rotting?
3. If you had been one of the diggers peeling back the dark river mud at Must Farm, which everyday find—the bronze axes, glass beads, wooden wheel, or porridge bowl with its spoon—would have amazed you most, and why?

Commentaries on the Gallic War (Books IV & V, 55–54 BC)

Julius Caesar (adapted for KS2 from W. A. McDevitte's 1869 translation)
Historical Context: Iron Age Druids memorised knowledge instead of writing books, so our earliest eyewitness description of Iron Age Britons comes from Roman general Julius Caesar during his landings in 55 and 54 BC.
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Julius Caesar[JOO-lee-us SEE-zer]
The famous Roman general who sailed across the Channel in 55 BC and 54 BC and wrote down what he saw.
Woad[WOHD]
A blue plant dye that Iron Age British warriors painted on their skin to look fierce in battle.
Chariot[CHA-ree-ut]
A fast two-wheeled wooden war cart pulled by two ponies, carrying a driver and a spear-throwing warrior.
Javelin[JAV-lin]
A light, sharp throwing spear hurled at enemies from a galloping chariot or on foot.

Countless people live across the island of Britain, and great herds of cattle graze their green fields. For money, the Britons use bronze or gold coins, or iron bars weighed to an exact measure. Tin comes from inland mines, while iron is dug near the coast.

All the Britons dye their skin blue with woad, which makes them look fierce in battle! Warriors wear their hair long and grow thick moustaches above their upper lip.

In chariot fighting, British warriors show amazing skill. First, they gallop fast across every part of the field while hurling sharp javelins! Even the thunder of hooves and the rattle of wheels throw our Roman ranks into panic.

Next, each warrior leaps down from his moving chariot to fight on foot. His driver waits close by, ready for a quick rescue if enemies press near. Daily practice makes them steady enough to halt horses on a steep slope. Warriors even run right out along the wooden chariot pole!

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. According to Julius Caesar's eyewitness journal, what metals and coins did Iron Age Britons already make and use?
2. Why did British war-chariots shock Caesar's disciplined Roman soldiers so badly at first?
3. Caesar was an enemy Roman general—why do you think he sounded so amazed by the Britons' coins, mines, and daring chariot tricks?

Parent, Carer & Tutor Conversation Guide & Answer Key Stone Age to Iron Age Britain
No Specialist History Knowledge Needed: Pupils do not need word-for-word answers—praise curiosity and listen for these core historical ideas during your 3-minute chat!
Enquiry 1 (p. 2): Antler Masks & Doggerland
Q1: They scraped the skull bone smooth with sharp flint blades and bored two neat holes so leather straps could tie the antlers onto a wearer's head.
Q2: Hunters might have worn them as disguises to creep close to wild deer, or people may have worn them while dancing in firelight at winter gatherings.
Q3: Families would miss walking across the grassy plains to mainland Europe, spearing fat eels in winding rivers, netting wild ducks, and gathering autumn hazelnuts before the North Sea covered their hunting grounds.
Enquiry 2 (p. 3): The Storm at Skara Brae
Q1: A fierce winter storm tore away grassy sand dunes at the Bay of Skaill on Orkney, revealing eight ancient stone houses to local landowner William Watt.
Q2: Because few trees grew on windy Orkney, villagers built their beds, dressers, and hearths out of flat coastal flagstones that could not rot.
Q3: They made their homes cosy with warm peat hearths, soft heather and sheepskin bedding, earth mounds to block icy winds, indoor stone drains, and a proud stone dresser inside each door for special bowls and beads.
Enquiry 3 (p. 4): Stonehenge Winter Feasts
Q1: Hundreds of people pulled oak sledges over log rollers with thick plant ropes, and patient masons pounded hard stone balls to shape pegs and sockets that locked the trilithons together.
Q2: The tests proved that travellers drove fat pigs and cattle all the way from Scotland to share midwinter feasts near Stonehenge.
Q3: Working side by side to haul huge stones, feast on roast pork around bright fires, and watch the Midwinter solstice sun glow straight between the stones brought distant communities together to celebrate the turning of winter.
Enquiry 4 (p. 5): The Amesbury Archer
Q1: He was buried at Amesbury with sixteen sharp flint arrowheads and stone wristguards, and years earlier a bad fall had torn off his left kneecap.
Q2: Alongside his arrowheads lay Britain's earliest gold hair rings, a smooth cushion stone, and three flat copper knives.
Q3: He had travelled all the way from central Europe across the sea bringing bright new metal skills, and his friends buried over a hundred treasures—including gold hair rings and copper knives—to show their respect.
Enquiry 5 (p. 6): Fire at Must Farm
Q1: Every family scrambled out safely into boats so quickly that they left their dinner pots right on the fire—and even a wooden spoon sitting inside a bowl of wheat porridge.
Q2: The burning roundhouses splashed straight into soft, waterlogged river mud, which put out the flames and shut out all air so bacteria could not rot them.
Q3: Seeing ordinary objects like a wooden spoon left inside wheat porridge, woven cloth, glass beads, and Britain's oldest complete wooden wheel makes the Bronze Age families feel real and surprisingly skilled.
Enquiry 6 (p. 7): Caesar Meets the Britons
Q1: Caesar noted that Britons used bronze and gold coins as well as weighed iron bars, and mined both tin and coastal iron.
Q2: Blue-painted warriors galloped wildly with thundering hooves and clattering wheels, hurled javelins, leaped off to fight on foot, and even ran along the chariot pole!
Q3: Even as an enemy fighting against them, Caesar saw with his own eyes how organised, fearless, and well-practised the British warriors and their drivers really were.
