WALK-IN LESSON PLANS • ENQUIRIES 1 & 2 NON-SPECIALIST PRIMARY TEACHER GUIDE
Enquiry 1: Secret Code in Place-Names and Old Ordnance Survey Maps TB p.4 • WB pp.3–4
Key Enquiry: How do place-names and old maps unlock secret history on our doorstep?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This opening Key Stage 2 fieldwork enquiry equips pupils anywhere in the United Kingdom to decode settlement place-names and read historical Ordnance Survey maps. Pupils investigate Celtic river names, Roman "-chester/-caster" forts, Anglo-Saxon "-ham/-ton/-ford/-ley" farms and clearings, Viking "-by/-thorpe" villages, and the 1086 Domesday Book, before comparing 1880s Six-Inch and Twenty-Five-Inch Ordnance Survey sheets with modern street maps and spotting stone benchmarks and hilltop trig pillars outdoors.
Learning Objectives:
  • Decode Celtic, Roman, Anglo-Saxon, Viking, and Norman clues hidden inside British town and village names.
  • Compare 1880s Victorian Ordnance Survey maps with modern streets to spot continuity and change.
  • Identify physical Ordnance Survey benchmarks carved in walls and concrete trig pillars on British hilltops.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Together, place-names, old Ordnance Survey maps, and street survey marks unlock local history on any UK doorstep because they connect ancient words to exact places. Place-names and the 1086 Domesday Book reveal Roman, Saxon, or Viking roots, 1880s Ordnance Survey maps expose vanished mills and fields, and carved stone benchmarks and hilltop trig pillars show how Britain was measured!”
Enquiry 2: Reading Buildings on the High Street — Timber, Brick and Royal Cyphers TB p.5 • WB pp.5–6
Key Enquiry: How can timber frames, brickwork, and postboxes date a British street?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This second fieldwork enquiry teaches pupils to "look above the modern plastic shopfront" along any British high street or village lane. Pupils compare Tudor oak timber-framed houses with overhanging upper jetties, symmetrical Georgian brick-and-stone terraces with sliding sash windows, and ornate Victorian machine-made red brick and terracotta buildings, before hunting street-level clues including Royal Cyphers on cast-iron pillar boxes (V.R., E.VIIR, G.R., E.IIR, C.IIIR), carved datestones, and faded painted brick "ghost signs".
Learning Objectives:
  • Distinguish Tudor timber-framed buildings, Georgian sash terraces, and Victorian red-brick civic architecture.
  • Date street postboxes and buildings using Royal Cyphers and carved stone datestones.
  • Use faded painted brick "ghost signs" to uncover vanished Victorian and Edwardian shops and products.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Even without digging underground, any pupil can date a British street by combining building styles and street clues because each era used distinct materials. Overhanging oak frames mark Tudor buildings, neat sliding sash windows mark Georgian terraces, and bright red brick with bay windows marks Victorian expansion—confirmed on the pavement by postbox Royal Cyphers, carved datestones, and brick ghost signs!”

Enquiry 3: Voices From Number 12 — Censuses, Parish Books and School Logbooks TB p.6 • WB pp.7–8
Key Enquiry: What can censuses, parish books, and school logbooks reveal about past families?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This third enquiry moves inside local homes, churches, and classrooms using Britain’s three richest social-history archives. Pupils explore the every-ten-year UK Census (started in 1801, naming every person from 1841 under statistician Dr William Farr and kept private for 100 years), decode vanished Victorian jobs ("ostler", "lamplighter", "charwoman", "ag. lab."), trace pre-1801 families through 1538 parish registers and churchyard gravestones, and read handwritten post-1862 Victorian School Logbooks.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain how the ten-year UK Census works and use an 1881 Census sheet to investigate Victorian households.
  • Combine parish church registers (from 1538) and churchyard headstones to trace families before 1801.
  • Use Victorian School Logbooks (from 1862) to uncover everyday childhood, weather, illness, and farm work.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Used together, censuses, parish registers, and school logbooks bring ordinary local families back to life because they record people who never became famous kings or generals! Census enumerators listed every household and vanished jobs like ostlers and lamplighters, church baptisms and headstones linked generations from 1538, and School Logbooks captured children’s daily lives!”
Enquiry 4: How Our Town Earned Its Living — Farms, Canals, Mills and Stations TB p.7 • WB pp.9–10
Key Enquiry: How do street names, canals, mills, and stations show past local work?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This fourth enquiry investigates how geography, trade, and transport shaped every British village, market town, port, and city. Pupils trace medieval and Stuart market crosses and turnpike milestones ("117 Miles to London"), discover how 18th-century canals, locks, and towpaths carried fragile pottery and heavy coal before 19th-century steam railways and stations transformed speed and fresh food supply, and decode occupational street names (Mill Lane, Foundry Street, Ropewalk, Station Road) even where tracks closed in the 1960s.
Learning Objectives:
  • Read market crosses, turnpike milestones, and street signs to trace early local trade and road travel.
  • Explain how canals, wooden locks, and waterwheels powered heavy industry before steam railways.
  • Spot surviving clues of Victorian steam railways and vanished local industries on modern streets.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “By exploring market squares, waterways, railway bridges, and street signs, detectives can reconstruct how a town earned its living because every trade shaped the landscape! Roofed market crosses and turnpike milestones served farming trade, riverside waterwheels and canals powered mills and coal barges, and Victorian steam railways—still echoed in Station Road and Mill Lane signs—linked local workers to the whole country.”

Enquiry 5: Names Carved in Stone — Local War Memorials and Commonwealth Records TB p.8 • WB pp.11–12
Key Enquiry: How can War Memorials and Commonwealth records trace one local soldier’s story?
Subject Knowledge Primer: Handled with solemn dignity, this fifth enquiry shows pupils how national and global events touched every single British village and street during the First and Second World Wars. Pupils investigate why communities raised over 100,000 local War Memorials after 1918 (because fallen service personnel were buried overseas rather than brought home), how Sir Fabian Ware founded the Commonwealth War Graves Commission in 1917 with equal white Portland stone headstones for rich and poor alike, and how local detectives combine CWGC records, bronze Memorial Plaques ("Dead Man’s Pennies"), medals, 1911 Census sheets, and local newspapers to tell one neighbour’s life story.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain why British towns, villages, churches, and workplaces built local War Memorials after 1918.
  • Explain Sir Fabian Ware’s 1917 Commonwealth War Graves rule of equal honour regardless of rank or wealth.
  • Trace one carved memorial name back to a real home street using CWGC registers, medals, plaques, and censuses.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Starting from a single carved name on a local War Memorial, detectives can piece together a real neighbour’s life story by linking four sources step by step! The Commonwealth War Graves register matches that surname to a white Portland stone headstone, regiment, age, and parents’ address—letting pupils check the 1911 Census and local newspapers to find their childhood home and school!”
Enquiry 6: Living Memory, Oral History and Saving Local Heritage TB p.9 • WB pp.13–14
Key Enquiry: How do oral history, Blue Plaques, and trails protect local heritage today?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This synoptic final enquiry empowers pupils as active community historians and heritage guardians. Pupils learn how to plan respectful oral history interviews exploring living memory since 1945 (post-war Co-op and corner shops, street games, bus tickets, and Decimal Day on 15 February 1971), why historians cross-check human memories against dated photographs and maps, how Octavia Hill’s 1895 National Trust, Blue Plaques (started 1866), and 1947 Listed Building laws protect historic places, and how to combine all six enquiries into a local Heritage Trail!
Learning Objectives:
  • Plan open-ended oral history questions and explain why memories should be cross-checked with photographs.
  • Explain how Octavia Hill’s 1895 National Trust, Blue Plaques, and 1947 Listed Buildings protect UK heritage.
  • Synthesize place-names, maps, buildings, censuses, memorials, and oral history into a local Heritage Trail.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “To uncover the full story of any British neighbourhood, a 6-stop Local History Detective Trail combines outdoor and archive clues by moving from ancient roots to living memory! First we decode place-names and 1880s Ordnance Survey maps, date buildings and postbox Royal Cyphers, and check Census households and School Logbooks. Then we trace canal, mill, or railway street names, research one local name on the War Memorial, and record oral history memories!”
