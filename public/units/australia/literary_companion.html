Enquiry 1 Eyewitness Voice Endeavour Expedition Journal (1770)

The Endeavour Journal of Sir Joseph Banks at Kamay / Botany Bay (1770)

Joseph Banks (April–August 1770 · State Library of NSW)
Historical Context: Wealthy twenty-seven-year-old botanist Joseph Banks sailed aboard HMS Endeavour alongside Lieutenant James Cook and Raiatean navigator Tupaia. At Kamay (Botany Bay) on 29 April 1770, Banks recorded brave Gweagal resistance—yet later told London MPs the continent was nearly empty.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Kamay[KAH-my]
The Indigenous Dharawal name for Botany Bay, cared for by the Gweagal clan for tens of thousands of years.
Gweagal[GWAY-uh-gul]
The Dharawal clan on the southern shore of Kamay whose warriors opposed Cook’s landing on 29 April 1770.
Woomera[WOO-muh-ruh]
A carved Aboriginal spear-thrower acting as a lever to launch spears faster and further.
Tupaia[too-PYE-uh]
Master Polynesian star-navigator and priest from Raiatea who guided HMS Endeavour across the Pacific.
Terra Nullius[TERR-uh NULL-ee-us]
Latin for "nobody’s land"—the false British legal claim used to seize Australia without treaties.

On 29 April 1770, HMS Endeavour anchored inside the sheltered waters of Kamay, which James Cook named Botany Bay. Aboard sailed Polynesian navigator Tupaia and young botanist Joseph Banks, who wrote in his journal:

"28–29 April 1770: Under the south head we saw four small bark canoes. In each stood one man striking fish with a long pole. As our boats rowed toward their huts on the beach, two Gweagal men stepped down onto the rocks. Each carried a ten-foot lance and a short stick [woomera] shaped like a lever to hurl the spear.

"They called to us loudly in a sharp language. Neither we nor Tupaia understood a single word. Shaking their lances, those two men stood resolved to block our landing, though they were only two and we numbered thirty or forty. A musket was fired between them as a warning. At the blast, one man dropped his spears, snatched them straight back up, and threw a stone at our boat. Small shot was then fired at his legs before both men withdrew into the woods.

"[August 1770:] Inland, the country looked like open woods and grassy parks clear of scrub. Seeing few people on the beaches, we guessed that the vast interior must be almost wholly uninhabited."

3 Compare Banks’s April journal with his August guess that the land was "almost wholly uninhabited"—how do his own earlier words prove that guess wrong?

Enquiry 2 True Historical Story 1783–1788

From Rotting Hulks to Sandstone Rock: The First Fleet Reaches Warrane (1787–1788)

The History Revision Hub · Historical Story (1783–1788)
Historical Context: When America won independence in 1783, Britain lost its old dumping ground for convicts. Overcrowded floating prisons in Portsmouth and the Thames pushed Westminster to gamble on a penal colony 15,000 miles away in New South Wales.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Bloody Code[BLUD-ee KOHD]
Britain’s harsh 18th-century criminal laws punishing over 200 offences with death or overseas transportation.
Prison Hulks[PRIZ-un HULKS]
Rotting, mastless wooden warships anchored in Portsmouth Harbour and the Thames as overcrowded floating jails.
Warrane[wuh-RAHN]
The Gadigal clan’s ancestral cove in Port Jackson, renamed Sydney Cove by Arthur Phillip in January 1788.
Gadigal[GAD-ih-gul]
The First Nations clan of the Eora Nation whose Country surrounds Warrane (Sydney Harbour).

Before dawn on 13 May 1787, heavy leg-irons clanked below the waterline in Portsmouth Harbour. Since losing its thirteen American colonies in 1783, Britain had nowhere to ship prisoners sentenced under its harsh "Bloody Code." Thousands of men, women, and children—some jailed for stealing bread or a handkerchief—shivered aboard damp, disease-ridden warships known as prison hulks.

That May morning, Captain Arthur Phillip led eleven small wooden vessels out into the Channel. Packed below decks sailed more than 730 convicts bound for the far side of the earth. For eight gruelling months, the First Fleet pitched across fifteen thousand miles of ocean.

When the ships reached Botany Bay in January 1788, Phillip saw straight away that Joseph Banks had been wrong. The bay was shallow, exposed to gales, and short of fresh water. Exploring just north into Port Jackson, Phillip found what he called "the finest harbour in the world"—a deep sheltered cove known to its Gadigal custodians as Warrane.

On 26 January 1788, marines raised the Union Jack at Sydney Cove. Survival, however, hung by a thread. Cheap axes from English contractors bent against giant red gum trees and hard sandstone, leaving jailers and convicts alike facing starvation on Gadigal Country.

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 How did losing the thirteen American colonies in 1783 lead to the First Fleet sailing from Portsmouth in May 1787?
2 Why did Captain Arthur Phillip abandon Botany Bay after arriving in January 1788?
3 Raising the Union Jack at Sydney Cove sounded like a triumph, yet survival "hung by a thread"—why did both convicts and their jailers face such a desperate struggle?

Enquiry 3 True Historical Story 1789–1802

The Warrior Who Defied Muskets: Pemulwuy’s Twelve-Year Eora War (1790–1802)

The History Revision Hub · Historical Story (1789–1802)
Historical Context: After devastating smallpox struck Sydney Harbour in April 1789, Eora leaders fought for survival in contrasting ways. While Wangal diplomat Bennelong sought negotiation, Bidjigal warrior Pemulwuy waged a twelve-year guerrilla campaign defending Eora Country.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Pemulwuy[PEM-ul-way]
Bidjigal resistance leader who led a twelve-year guerrilla war (1790–1802) defending Eora land around Sydney.
Bennelong[BEN-uh-long]
Wangal elder and diplomat who learned English and sailed to London (1792) hoping to broker respect between cultures.
Galgalla[gal-GAL-uh]
The Eora word for the catastrophic April 1789 smallpox outbreak that killed roughly half of Sydney’s Indigenous clans.
Bidjigal[BID-jih-gul]
Pemulwuy’s Eora/Dharug clan whose hunting grounds stretched from Botany Bay toward Parramatta.

Fifteen months after the First Fleet anchored at Sydney Cove, silent terror swept the harbour. In April 1789, a deadly wave of smallpox—called galgalla by the Eora—killed at least half the First Nations people around Port Jackson. Empty bark canoes drifted into quiet bays as elders and song-keepers died with no natural defence against the new sickness.

Amid that grief, two Eora men chose opposite paths to save their clans. Wangal elder Woollarawarre Bennelong learned English, dined with Governor Phillip, and sailed to London in 1792 hoping talks would win respect. Bidjigal warrior Pemulwuy saw British fences swallowing yam beds and kangaroo plains, so he chose war.

From 1790 onward, Pemulwuy led a bold twelve-year bush campaign along the Parramatta and Hawkesbury rivers. Knowing spears could not beat massed muskets in open lines, his men set fire to ripe cornfields and drove off settlers’ sheep.

In March 1797, during fierce fighting at Parramatta, seven buckshot pellets tore into Pemulwuy’s head and chest. Chained to a hospital bed under guard, the wounded leader waited for night. He slipped out into the bush wearing a heavy leg-iron on his ankle and fought on. Across Sydney, awed fighters believed no British bullet could kill Pemulwuy—until an ambush ended his life in 1802.

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 How did the April 1789 smallpox outbreak (galgalla) devastate Eora society right before frontier farms expanded?
2 Compare Woollarawarre Bennelong and Pemulwuy: how did these two Eora leaders choose opposite paths to defend their clans?
3 Why did Pemulwuy target ripe cornfields and settlers’ sheep instead of fighting British muskets in the open—and why did his 1797 escape make him a legend?

Enquiry 4 Classic Literature & Verse 19th-Century Australian Bush Balladry

"Clancy of the Overflow" (1889) & "Waltzing Matilda" (1895): Bush Myths vs Frontier Reality

A. B. "Banjo" Paterson (1889–1895)
Historical Context: Poet Andrew Barton "Banjo" Paterson wrote Australia’s best-known bush ballads celebrating outback drovers, swagmen, and sheep stations. Historians read his verse alongside frontier archives to see both settler bush pride and the silenced dispossession of First Nations Country.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Banjo Paterson[BAN-joh PAT-ur-sun]
Nineteenth-century Australian bush poet who wrote "Clancy of the Overflow" (1889) and "Waltzing Matilda" (1895).
Squatter[SKWOT-ur]
A wealthy colonial grazier who seized huge inland tracts of Aboriginal Country to run Merino sheep.
Billabong[BIL-uh-bong]
An Australian river waterhole—adopted from Wiradjuri language—vital to both Aboriginal clans and pastoral flocks.
Truganini[troo-guh-NEE-nee]
Nuennonne woman of Tasmania who endured the Black War (1824–1831) and forced exile at Wybalenna on Flinders Island.

Across the nineteenth century, wealthy colonial "squatters" drove millions of Merino sheep inland to supply British wool mills. Bush ballads by poet Banjo Paterson painted those vast sheep runs as a romantic world of sunlit plains and free-spirited drovers:

Banjo Paterson, "Clancy of the Overflow" (1889):
In my wild erratic fancy visions come to me of Clancy
Gone a-droving "down the Cooper" where the western drovers go;
As the stock are slowly stringing, Clancy rides behind them singing,
For the drover’s life has pleasures that the townsfolk never know.
And the bush hath friends to meet him, and their kindly voices greet him
In the murmur of the breezes and the river on its bars,
And he sees the vision splendid of the sunlit plains extended,
And at night the wondrous glory of the everlasting stars.
Banjo Paterson, "Waltzing Matilda" (1895):
Down came a jumbuck to drink at that billabong,
Up jumped the swagman and grabbed him with glee...
Up rode the squatter, mounted on his thoroughbred,
Down came the troopers, one, two, three.

Yet notice who vanishes from those famous bush verses. Every "sunlit plain" and billabong was stolen Aboriginal Country. Squatters’ sheep triggered Tasmania’s brutal Black War—survived by Nuennonne diplomat Truganini—and mainland massacres like Myall Creek in 1838.

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 How does Banjo Paterson make the drover’s life sound so free and joyful in "Clancy of the Overflow"?
2 Who holds power in the bush according to "Waltzing Matilda", and what drove squatters so deep inland?
3 Look at the contrast between Banjo Paterson’s sunlit verses and the final paragraph—who vanishes from these famous bush poems, and why does that matter?

Enquiry 5 Eyewitness Voice Eyewitness Goldfields Memoir (1855)

The Eureka Stockade: The Bakery Hill Oath & Fight for Democracy (1854–1855)

Raffaello Carboni (Melbourne, 1855 · State Library Victoria)
Historical Context: Gold discoveries in 1851 tripled Australia’s settler population within a decade. Outraged by expensive monthly Gold Licences enforced at sword-point while miners lacked the vote, Italian digger Raffaello Carboni stood beside Irishman Peter Lalor beneath the Southern Cross flag at Ballarat in 1854.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Raffaello Carboni[raf-eye-EL-oh kar-BOH-nee]
Italian-born Ballarat miner and translator whose 1855 eyewitness book preserved the Eureka Stockade oath.
Eureka Stockade[yoo-REE-kuh stok-AYD]
The rough timber barricade built by Ballarat gold miners in Victoria and stormed by British troops on 3 December 1854.
Gold Licence[GOHLD LYE-suns]
The hated thirty-shilling monthly tax every miner had to pay whether they found gold or dug empty clay.
Secret Ballot[SEE-krit BAL-ut]
Private voting on printed official papers—pioneered in Australia in 1856 right after Eureka and later copied worldwide.

Italian-born prospector Raffaello Carboni dug for gold at Ballarat in Victoria. In 1855, he published his eyewitness book about armed police "licence hunts" and the rebel oath sworn beneath the Southern Cross flag.

"Whether a digger struck rich gold in wet clay or worked an empty hole for weeks, the Commissioners showed no mercy. Twice a week, mounted troopers rode in with drawn swords shouting, ’Traps! Show your licences!’ Any miner caught without his thirty-shilling monthly paper was chained to a gum tree at the camp. It was taxation at the point of a bayonet upon men denied a single vote!

"On 29 November 1854, twelve thousand diggers of twenty nations met on Bakery Hill. As the blue-and-white Southern Cross flag unfurled overhead, we burned our hated licences on bonfires. Next afternoon, Peter Lalor knelt bareheaded beneath that flag, raised his right hand, and led five hundred diggers in a solemn oath.

"’We swear by the Southern Cross to stand truly by each other, and fight to defend our rights and liberties!’

"At dawn on Sunday 3 December, three hundred soldiers and police stormed our timber stockade, killing at least twenty-two diggers. Thirteen of us were dragged to Melbourne for High Treason trials. Yet citizen juries cheered and freed every single prisoner—winning white male votes and the secret ballot within eighteen months."

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 Why did Raffaello Carboni call the Victorian Gold Licence "taxation at the point of a bayonet"?
2 How did the Eureka diggers lose the dawn battle on 3 December 1854 yet still win a major political victory within eighteen months?
3 Carboni noted that twelve thousand diggers of "twenty nations" stood together on Bakery Hill, yet the reforms won "white male votes"—what does that show about both the strength and the limits of Eureka’s victory?
