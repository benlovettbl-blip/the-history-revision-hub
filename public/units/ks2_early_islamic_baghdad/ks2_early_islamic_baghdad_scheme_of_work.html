WALK-IN LESSON PLANS • ENQUIRIES 1 & 2 NON-SPECIALIST PRIMARY TEACHER GUIDE
Enquiry 1: Crossroads of the World — Why Did Caliph al-Mansur Build the Round City of Baghdad? (AD 750–800) TB p.4 • WB pp.3–4
Key Enquiry: Why did Caliph al-Mansur build the Round City of Baghdad, and how did it compare with Britain around AD 900?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This opening Key Stage 2 enquiry introduces the rapid spread of early Islam from Mecca and Medina across Damascus, Cairo, Persia, and Spain before focusing on Abbasid Caliph al-Mansur’s decision to found Baghdad in AD 762. Primary pupils investigate why the fertile bend between the Tigris and Euphrates was a Silk Road crossroads, how 100,000 bricklayers built the 2km circular Round City (*Madinat al-Salam*), and how Baghdad’s nearly one million citizens around AD 900 contrasted starkly with Anglo-Saxon Britain’s small timber towns of 5,000–10,000 people.
Learning Objectives:
  • Locate Baghdad on the River Tigris and explain why Caliph al-Mansur chose this Silk Road crossroads in AD 762.
  • Describe how 100,000 builders planned and constructed the two-kilometre circular Round City of Baghdad.
  • Contrast daily urban life in Baghdad around AD 900 with Anglo-Saxon towns and villages in Britain.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “By AD 900, Baghdad had grown into the bustling Crossroads of the World because Caliph al-Mansur placed his AD 762 capital where Tigris and Euphrates canals met overland Silk Road caravans and sea ships from Asia and Africa. Protected by the double brick walls and four gates of Madinat al-Salam, trade and gold dinars funded paved streets, bathhouses, and grand libraries for nearly one million people. In contrast, divided Anglo-Saxon Britain had far smaller local trade networks, with towns of just 5,000 to 10,000 people living in thatched timber huts.”
Enquiry 2: Inside the House of Wisdom — How Did Paper & Translators Rescue World Knowledge? (c. AD 800–1000) TB p.5 • WB pp.5–6
Key Enquiry: How did cheap paper and Baghdad’s House of Wisdom rescue and expand world knowledge?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This second Key Stage 2 enquiry explores how Baghdad’s House of Wisdom (*Bayt al-Hikma*) under Caliphs Harun al-Rashid and al-Ma’mun united technological innovation with cultural exchange. Primary pupils trace how rag paper-making arrived from China via Samarkand to create over 100 bookshops (while Europe still scraped expensive calfskin parchment), how Muslim, Christian, and Jewish translators like Hunayn ibn Ishaq rescued and tested Greek, Persian, and Sanskrit texts, and how women patrons Fatima al-Fihri (al-Qarawiyyin in Fez, AD 859) and Queen Zubayda (Hajj road wells) shaped the Golden Age.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain how learning rag paper-making via Samarkand transformed bookmaking in Baghdad compared with parchment in Europe.
  • Describe how Muslim, Christian, and Jewish scholars worked together during the Translation Movement in the House of Wisdom.
  • Evaluate the lasting impact of female patrons Fatima al-Fihri in Fez (AD 859) and Queen Zubayda on the Hajj road.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Rescuing and spreading Golden Age knowledge took an extraordinary team effort across the Islamic world. Caliphs Harun al-Rashid and al-Ma’mun provided royal funding to expand the House of Wisdom and send messengers for rare scrolls. Yet without skilled Muslim, Christian, and Jewish translators like Hunayn ibn Ishaq, those Greek, Persian, and Sanskrit books could never have been read, tested, and improved in Arabic on cheap rag paper. Equally important, visionary women patrons like Fatima al-Fihri — who founded al-Qarawiyyin in Fez in AD 859 — and Queen Zubayda kept students learning and travellers safe!”

Enquiry 3: Zero, Algebra, Astrolabes & Free Hospitals — What Did Golden Age Scientists Discover? (c. AD 820–1050) TB p.6 • WB pp.7–8
Key Enquiry: How did Golden Age mathematicians, astronomers, engineers, and doctors change world science forever?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This third Key Stage 2 enquiry celebrates scientific, mathematical, engineering, and medical breakthroughs across the Islamic Golden Age (c. AD 820–1050 and al-Jazari’s mechanical legacy). Primary pupils investigate Muhammad al-Khwarizmi (0–9 numerals, zero/*sifr*, algebra, algorithm), Mariam al-Asturlabi’s brass astrolabes, al-Jazari’s Elephant Water Clock, Ibn al-Haytham’s pinhole *camera obscura* experiments proving how vision works, free *bimaristan* hospitals, al-Razi’s clean-air meat test and measles vs smallpox research, and al-Zahrawi’s 200 surgical tools and dissolving catgut stitches.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain how al-Khwarizmi’s Hindu-Arabic numerals (0–9), algebra, and Mariam al-Asturlabi’s astrolabes still shape modern life.
  • Describe how Ibn al-Haytham used a dark pinhole room (camera obscura) to prove how human eyes see light.
  • Explain why medieval Islamic bimaristan hospitals, al-Razi, and al-Zahrawi were centuries ahead of healthcare in Europe.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Although maths, optics, and medicine all transformed human life, I think al-Khwarizmi’s mathematics changed our modern world the most because every other science depends on it! His Hindu-Arabic numerals (0–9 including zero, sifr), algebra, and step-by-step algorithms power modern computers, engineering, and hospital machines alike. Even so, Ibn al-Haytham’s pinhole camera obscura gave us eyeglasses and cameras, while free bimaristans, al-Razi’s clinical notes, and al-Zahrawi’s 200 surgical tools and dissolving catgut stitches created the blueprint for modern healthcare!”
Enquiry 4: Silk Road Caravans, Dhows & Viking Dirhams — How Far Did Baghdad's Traders Reach? (c. AD 850–1000) TB p.7 • WB pp.9–10
Key Enquiry: How did land caravans and sea dhows make Baghdad a trading superpower, and how did Islamic coins reach Britain?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This fourth Key Stage 2 enquiry explores Baghdad's global trade networks between c. AD 850 and 1000. Primary pupils trace overland Silk Road camel caravans stopping every 30 km at fortified caravanserais, maritime dhow voyages from Basra across the Indian Ocean using astrolabes, and early paper cheques (sakk). Crucially, pupils connect Baghdad directly to British history through Abbasid silver dirham hoards found around Viking Jorvik (York) and Anglo-Saxon King Offa of Mercia's gold imitation dinar.
Learning Objectives:
  • Describe what goods entered Baghdad's covered bazaars and how caravanserais protected Silk Road camel caravans.
  • Explain how wooden dhow ships, brass astrolabes, and paper sakk cheques transformed sea trade and banking.
  • Use coin evidence from Viking Jorvik (York) and King Offa of Mercia to prove Baghdad’s links with Britain.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Between AD 850 and 1000, Baghdad's traders linked three continents, and astonishing coin clues even connect them to Britain. Eastwards, camel caravans trekked the Silk Road to China while wooden dhow ships sailed from Basra across the Indian Ocean for pepper, cardamom, and pearls. Northwards, pure Abbasid silver dirhams flowed along Russian rivers to Viking traders, who buried thousands of them in hoards across Scandinavia and near Jorvik (York). Even Anglo-Saxon King Offa of Mercia minted a gold coin around AD 774 copying an Arabic dinar!”

Enquiry 5: Geometric Tilework, Calligraphy & Chess — What Was Everyday Life Like in Baghdad c. AD 900? TB p.8 • WB pp.11–12
Key Enquiry: How did Baghdad’s families keep cool, eat, worship, create dazzling art, and relax around AD 900?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This fifth Key Stage 2 enquiry steps inside everyday life and visual culture in Baghdad around AD 900. Primary pupils investigate passive air-cooling in shaded courtyard houses with windcatcher (badgir) towers, hammam bathhouses, soap, and rosewater, and new orchard crops (lemons, oranges, aubergines, spinach, pomegranates, almonds, cane sugar). Pupils also explore why mosque artists avoided human or animal statues in worship, mastering geometric star mosaics, arabesque vines, Arabic calligraphy, lustreware, tiraz silks, and shatranj (chess).
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain how courtyard houses, windcatcher towers, and hammams suited Iraq’s hot climate and Islamic hygiene.
  • Identify new foods grown and cooked in Golden Age Baghdad and explain how irrigation transformed diets.
  • Explain why mosque artists avoided human statues and instead excelled at geometry, arabesque, and calligraphy.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Exploring homes, kitchens, mosques, and workshops in AD 900 Baghdad proves early Islamic civilisation was inventive, clean, artistic, and globally connected. Builders engineered natural windcatcher cooling and piped water to tiled hammams where people washed with soap and rosewater. Canal orchards filled kitchens with lemons, oranges, aubergines, spinach, and cane sugar. Meanwhile, artists used geometry and calligraphy to create eight-pointed star mosaics, lustreware bowls, silk tiraz robes, and ceramic shatranj chess sets enjoyed by families at home!”
Enquiry 6: The 1258 Siege of Baghdad & Our World Today — Did the Golden Age Really Disappear? (AD 1258 & Today) TB p.9 • WB pp.13–14
Key Enquiry: Why did Baghdad fall in 1258, how did Golden Age ideas survive, and how do they shape our lives today?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This capstone Key Stage 2 enquiry investigates both the dramatic February 1258 Mongol Siege of Baghdad under Hulagu Khan and why the Islamic Golden Age was NOT lost. Primary pupils trace how rival breakaway states left Baghdad vulnerable to Mongol counterweight trebuchets, then follow surviving manuscript copies to Cairo, Damascus, Samarkand, and Al-Andalus (Córdoba and the Alhambra), where multi-faith translators in Toledo turned Arabic maths, medicine, and optics into Latin — leaving a living legacy in modern science and everyday English words.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain why the weakened Abbasid capital fell to Hulagu Khan’s Mongol army in January–February 1258.
  • Explain why Golden Age discoveries survived 1258 in Cairo, Damascus, Samarkand, Córdoba, and Toledo.
  • Evaluate the lasting legacy of early Islamic science, medicine, and Arabic words in our world today.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Although Hulagu Khan destroyed Baghdad’s House of Wisdom in February 1258, the Golden Age never truly disappeared because books and ideas proved tougher than brick walls! Copies already thrived in Cairo, Damascus, Samarkand, Córdoba, and the Alhambra, while Toledo’s translators shared them in Latin to spark Europe’s Renaissance. Today, every time we write 0–9, solve algebra, run a phone algorithm, focus a camera lens, or say zero, chemistry, sugar, lemon, orange, sofa, and cotton, Baghdad’s Golden Age lives on!”
