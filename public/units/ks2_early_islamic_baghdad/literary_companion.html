A Circle of Fire: Caliph al-Mansur Plans Baghdad

The History Revision Hub Narrative Archive
Historical Context: How do you preview a giant circular capital city two kilometres wide before laying a single mud brick? Read aloud to see the dazzling night-time trick Caliph al-Mansur used beside the River Tigris in AD 762!
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Caliph[KAY-lif]
The supreme religious and political ruler leading the vast Islamic Empire after the time of the Prophet Muhammad.
Al-Mansur[al-man-SOOR]
The visionary Abbasid Caliph who chose the River Tigris crossroads in AD 762 to build Baghdad's Round City.
Madinat al-Salam[mah-DEE-nat al-sah-LAHM]
Caliph al-Mansur's official Arabic name for Baghdad, meaning 'City of Peace'.
Quffa[KOOF-uh]
A traditional round Iraqi river boat woven from reeds and waterproofed with sticky tar to carry goods on the Tigris.

Warm breezes rippled across the River Tigris in summer AD 762 as Caliph al-Mansur stood on a green plain. Round woven quffa boats bobbed along deep canals linking the Tigris and Euphrates. "Here is the finest crossroads on Earth!" the Caliph smiled. Ships from China and India could meet Silk Road camel trains right at his gates.

Instead of tangled lanes, al-Mansur planned a giant round city two kilometres wide. Builders pegged cords into the soil and poured white ash lines for double brick walls and four gates. Still, how could the Caliph see the whole huge circle at once?

At dusk, workers laid trails of fluffy cottonseeds soaked in lamp oil along every ash line. Torches touched the trail—whoosh! Two bright rings of golden fire blazed out across the dark plain! Standing right in the middle, proud al-Mansur ordered 100,000 builders to raise Madinat al-Salam, his City of Peace.

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. Why did Caliph al-Mansur ask his workmen to lay oil-soaked cottonseeds along the ash lines at night?
2. What made that green bend on the River Tigris such a clever spot for a world capital?
3. Why do you think Caliph al-Mansur planned a giant round city with four gates instead of tangled lanes?

Kitab al-Buldan (The Book of Countries: Inside Baghdad)

Ahmad al-Ya'qubi (Abbasid Geographer, c. AD 891)
Historical Context: Born in Baghdad, 9th-century traveller Ahmad al-Ya'qubi walked through his home city's markets taking vivid notes. Listen to his eyewitness wonder at a city buzzing with cheap rag paper and over a hundred bookshops!
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Bayt al-Hikma[bayt al-HIK-mah]
Baghdad's famous 'House of Wisdom' library and research centre where scholars translated and tested world science.
Parchment[PARCH-munt]
Expensive writing sheets scraped from animal skins in medieval Europe before cheap Chinese-style rag paper spread.
Hunayn ibn Ishaq[hoo-NAYN ib-in is-HAHK]
Baghdad's brilliant Christian doctor and translator who turned Greek medical manuscripts into clear Arabic.
Patron[PAY-trun]
A generous ruler or wealthy citizen—like Caliph al-Ma'mun or Fatima al-Fihri—who funds libraries, colleges, or wells.

Around AD 891, Baghdad writer Ahmad al-Ya'qubi walked past splashing watermills and shaded arches to describe his home city.

"I start my book with Baghdad because it stands at the heart of the world! No other city from East to West can match it. Cool river breezes blow in summer, and green gardens ring every street. Wooden boats glide up the Tigris bringing silks and spices from India, China, and Africa."

"Walk down the Street of the Booksellers, and you will count over one hundred busy bookshops side by side! Watermills beat old linen and cotton rags into smooth white paper far cheaper than animal skin. Inside the shops, scribes copy poems and star maps. Nearby, Muslim, Christian, and Jewish thinkers meet to test books translated from Greek, Persian, and Sanskrit!"

While Europe chained rare calfskin books to desks, Baghdad's cheap paper put bright ideas into thousands of eager hands.

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. How many bookshops did eyewitness geographer al-Ya'qubi count along just one market street in 9th-century Baghdad?
2. Why did making paper from old cotton and linen rags spread learning so much faster than scraping calfskin parchment?
3. What does Muslim, Christian, and Jewish thinkers meeting to test books from Greek, Persian, and Sanskrit show about Baghdad?

Clever Zero, Clean Wards & The Mechanical Elephant Clock

The History Revision Hub Narrative Archive
Historical Context: Try multiplying Roman numerals like MMDCCXLVIII in your head—no wonder sums took hours! Discover how scholars in the Islamic world gave us 0–9 numbers, free hospitals, and a robot elephant clock!
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Al-Khwarizmi[al-kwah-RIZ-mee]
Baghdad's star mathematician whose books gave us 0–9 place-value numbers, 'algebra' (al-Jabr), and 'algorithm'.
Sifr[SIF-er]
The Arabic word for empty or 'zero'—the clever placeholder digit that makes large sums quick and accurate.
Bimaristan[bee-MAH-rih-stahn]
A clean, free Islamic teaching hospital with separate wards, fountains, pharmacies, and trained nurses.
Al-Jazari[al-jah-ZAH-ree]
The brilliant 12th–13th century Islamic engineer who designed automatic water clocks and mechanical machines.

In Baghdad's House of Wisdom around AD 820, scholar al-Khwarizmi wrote ten neat signs on white paper. He used digits `1` to `9` plus a small circle called sifr—our word zero! With zero holding empty places, traders solved big sums fast while Europe used slow Roman letters. Better still, his book al-Jabr gave us algebra, and his name gave us algorithm!

Across town, doctor al-Razi welcomed rich nobles and poor guests alike into Baghdad's free bimaristan hospital. Cool fountains splashed in clean wards while nurses served fresh food. Leaving patients even got clean clothes and coins so they could rest at home!

Later in AD 1206, builder al-Jazari unveiled his tall Elephant Water Clock. Inside the brass elephant floated a copper bowl with a tiny hole. Every half hour, the full bowl sank and pulled hidden cords! A bird whistled, a dragon dropped a ball, and cymbals clashed—blending ideas from India, China, Greece, and Islam.

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. Which three everyday maths words come from al-Khwarizmi's name, his book 'al-Jabr', and the Arabic word 'sifr'?
2. How did Baghdad's 'bimaristan' hospitals care for poor patients even on the day they went home?
3. Why did al-Jazari blend ideas from India, China, Greece, and Islam into his single Elephant Water Clock?

One Thousand and One Nights (Sinbad Sets Sail From Baghdad)

Traditional Abbasid Storytellers (Alf Layla wa-Layla, Public Domain)
Historical Context: Set during the reign of Caliph Harun al-Rashid, the tales of Sinbad the Sailor turned real Indian Ocean dhow voyages from Baghdad and Basra into unforgettable bedtime adventures!
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Dhow[DOW]
A sleek Arab ocean trading ship built from wooden planks stitched tightly together with strong coconut-fibre rope.
Basra[BAZ-ruh]
Iraq's bustling southern gateway port where river boats from Baghdad met tall dhow ships bound for the Indian Ocean.
Bazaar[buh-ZAR]
A shaded market street (or souk) fragrant with eastern spices, fine silks, carpets, and cut glass.
Dirham[DEER-ham]
A trusted pure silver Abbasid coin stamped with Arabic writing—traded as far away as Viking York!

During Caliph Harun al-Rashid's reign, a tired porter named Hindbad set his heavy load outside a cool Baghdad house. Smelling sweet rosewater inside, he sighed at his hard life. Kindly, rich Sinbad the Sailor called him indoors, piled his plate with spiced food, and told his sea tale.

"Friend, I never won these silks while resting on soft cushions! As a young man, I walked into Baghdad's shaded bazaars. There I spent my last silver dirhams on bales of cloth, cut glass, and sweet dates. With brave traders, I sailed down the fast River Tigris to the port of Basra."

"At Basra we climbed aboard a tall wooden dhow stitched tight with strong coconut rope! Raising our triangle sail to the wind, we steered at night with a brass star astrolabe. Across the wide Indian Ocean, we swapped Baghdad's goods at distant islands for cloves, cinnamon, and bright gems!"

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. Which real river and real harbour city did Sinbad travel through to reach the open ocean from Baghdad?
2. Why did rich Sinbad invite the tired porter Hindbad indoors and tell him about his sea voyages?
3. What does Sinbad swapping Baghdad cloth, cut glass, and dates for cloves, cinnamon, and gems tell us about Baghdad?

The Silent Courtyard Battle: An Abbasid Shatranj Poem

Abbasid Baghdad Court Poetry Tradition (c. AD 900)
Historical Context: When evening breezes blew down Baghdad's windcatcher towers into orange-scented courtyards, friends unrolled a cloth board for 'shatranj'—the game that gave us chess! Can you picture every piece in this medieval Arabic riddle-poem?
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Shatranj[shat-RANJ]
The strategic sixty-four-square board game brought from India and Persia to Baghdad that grew into modern chess.
Badgir[bad-GEER]
A tall rooftop windcatcher tower that scooped cool breezes down into Baghdad courtyard homes during hot summers.
Geometric[jee-uh-MET-rik]
Art built from repeating symmetrical stars, polygons, and lines drawn carefully with a ruler and compasses.
Shah Mat[SHAH MAHT]
The winning cry in Persian and Arabic shatranj meaning 'the King is helpless'—giving English our word 'checkmate'!

As hot Iraqi afternoons cooled beneath tall badgir windcatcher towers, Baghdad families sat beside courtyard fountains sipping sweet lemon and rosewater drinks. Between plates of spiced almonds, two players spread a sixty-four-square leather mat and set out smooth turquoise and brown shatranj pieces. Poets loved chanting riddles about this bloodless game of wits:

Two silent armies face each other on a square-cut plain,
Where neither drum beats loud nor drop of blood is ever spilled!
Swift horses leap across the lines while war-elephants advance,
And loyal viziers guard their Shah behind a footmen's wall.
No human faces mark their clay, just curves of turquoise glaze,
Yet sharper than a steel-tipped spear is every silent move!
One careless step—a trap springs shut around the cornered king,
And laughter rings across the tiles at "Shah mat!" in the shade!

Centuries later, travellers carried shatranj into Spain and Britain, turning Shah mat into checkmate!

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. What kind of battle does the poem describe on the sixty-four-square mat, and why is no blood ever spilled?
2. Why does the poet say 'sharper than a steel-tipped spear is every silent move'?
3. Which everyday details in this courtyard scene show how Baghdad families stayed cool and relaxed on hot afternoons?

Mules Loaded With Star Charts: How Golden Age Ideas Survived 1258

The History Revision Hub Narrative Archive
Historical Context: When Mongol boulders smashed Baghdad's brick walls in February 1258, flames ruined the House of Wisdom. Read aloud to discover how brave scholars and distant libraries saved Golden Age science for our world today!
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Hulagu Khan[hoo-LAH-goo KAHN]
The Mongol commander whose vast army surrounded and captured Abbasid Baghdad in January–February AD 1258.
Trebuchet[TREB-yoo-shet]
A giant wooden counterweight siege machine used to hurl heavy stones against fortress walls.
Al-Andalus[al-AN-duh-loos]
Islamic Spain, where cities like Córdoba and Granada preserved hundreds of thousands of Arabic books.
Toledo[tuh-LAY-doh]
The Spanish city where Muslim, Jewish, and Christian translators turned Arabic science and maths books into Latin.

In January 1258, the ground by the River Tigris shook under thousands of Mongol horses. Hulagu Khan's men built tall wooden trebuchets to fling big rocks at Baghdad's brick walls. When the city fell in February, dark smoke rose over broken palaces and the House of Wisdom.

Yet five hundred years of bright science did not burn away! Brave star-watcher al-Tusi begged to save boxes of rare scrolls. He packed 400,000 books onto strong mules bound for his new star tower in the north! Better still, cheap rag paper meant spare copies sat safe in Cairo, Damascus, and Al-Andalus in Spain.

Far west in Spanish Toledo, Muslim, Jewish, and Christian thinkers worked side by side. By warm candlelight, they turned al-Khwarizmi's 0–9 sums and Ibn al-Haytham's light tests into Latin! Every time you write zero or snap a photo today, Baghdad's bright torch shines on.

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. How did brave star-watcher al-Tusi save hundreds of thousands of books when Baghdad fell in 1258?
2. Why did Baghdad's cheap rag paper turn out to be a lifesaver when the House of Wisdom burned?
3. Why does the story say that Baghdad's bright torch still shines whenever you write zero or snap a photo today?

Parent, Carer & Tutor Conversation Guide & Answer Key Early Islamic Civilisation
No Specialist History Knowledge Needed: Pupils do not need word-for-word answers—praise curiosity and listen for these core historical ideas during your 3-minute chat!
Enquiry 1 (p. 2): Circle of Flaming Seeds
Q1: Setting the cottonseed trails alight let him stand in the middle and see the exact full-size glowing outline of his circular walls and gates!
Q2: Deep canals linked the Tigris and Euphrates for quffa river boats, right where ships from China and India could meet Silk Road camel trains at his gates.
Q3: A neat circle with double brick walls and four gates kept his new City of Peace well organised and let him stand right at the centre of his capital.
Enquiry 2 (p. 3): Street of 100 Bookshops
Q1: More than one hundred bookshops stood side by side along the Street of the Paper-Makers and Booksellers!
Q2: Rag paper was light, smooth, and cheap to make in watermills, so scribes could copy hundreds of affordable books quickly.
Q3: It shows that Baghdad welcomed clever thinkers of different faiths and languages so they could share, translate, and test the best ideas from around the world.
Enquiry 3 (p. 4): Zero & The Elephant Clock
Q1: Algorithm (from al-Khwarizmi), algebra (from al-Jabr), and zero (from sifr)!
Q2: Treatment was completely free, and recovering poor patients were given clean clothes and coins so they could rest safely at home.
Q3: It showed that great inventions grew strongest by welcoming and combining clever ideas from cultures across the world.
Enquiry 4 (p. 5): Sinbad Sails From Baghdad
Q1: He sailed down the River Tigris to the bustling port of Basra, where river boats met tall dhow ships bound for the Indian Ocean.
Q2: Sinbad wanted to show Hindbad that he did not win his silks resting on soft cushions—he spent his last silver dirhams and sailed across the Indian Ocean to trade.
Q3: It shows Baghdad was a busy trading hub linked by rivers and oceans to distant islands, with silver dirhams traded as far away as Viking York!
Enquiry 5 (p. 6): Baghdad Shatranj Riddle
Q1: It is a silent, friendly game of shatranj (early chess) where clay horses, war-elephants, viziers, and footmen battle with wits instead of real weapons!
Q2: Winning at shatranj depended on clever planning, patience, and foresight rather than physical strength.
Q3: Tall badgir windcatcher towers scooped cool breezes indoors while families sat by fountains sipping sweet lemon and rosewater drinks, snacking on spiced almonds, and playing shatranj in the shade.
Enquiry 6 (p. 7): Saving Books Before 1258
Q1: He begged to save boxes of rare scrolls and packed 400,000 books onto strong mules bound for his new star tower in the north.
Q2: Because cheap rag paper meant spare copies already sat safe in faraway cities like Cairo, Damascus, and Al-Andalus in Spain!
Q3: Translators in Spanish Toledo turned al-Khwarizmi's 0–9 maths and Ibn al-Haytham's light tests into Latin, so Baghdad's ideas helped shape numbers and cameras we still use!
