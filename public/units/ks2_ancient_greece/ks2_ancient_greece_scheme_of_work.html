WALK-IN LESSON PLANS • ENQUIRIES 1 & 2 NON-SPECIALIST PRIMARY TEACHER GUIDE
Enquiry 1: Mountains, Islands & Rival Poleis — Why Was Ancient Greece Divided Into City-States? (c. 800–500 BC) TB p.4 • WB pp.3–4
Key Enquiry: Why did geography divide Ancient Greece into rival city-states, and what shared culture still united all Greeks?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This opening Key Stage 2 enquiry introduces the physical geography and early chronology of Ancient Greece. Primary pupils trace Bronze Age roots on Minoan Crete and at Mycenae's Lion Gate before investigating how steep mountain ranges and over 1,000 Aegean islands fragmented Greece into independent city-states (poleis) like Athens, Sparta, Corinth, and Thebes. Pupils explore the Acropolis and Agora inside a typical polis and identify the cultural bonds uniting all Hellenes: shared language, the 24-letter Greek alphabet adapted from Phoenician traders, shared gods, and Mediterranean olive-oil and pottery sea trade.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain how Bronze Age Minoans and Mycenaeans preceded classical Greece and why mountains and islands divided Greek city-states.
  • Compare the defensive and religious role of the high Acropolis with everyday trade and debate in the low Agora.
  • Identify the shared language, 24-letter alphabet, gods, and sea trade that united all Greeks as Hellenes.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Although rugged geography divided Ancient Greece into rival city-states, all Greeks still shared a strong Hellene identity. High mountain ridges and over a thousand Aegean islands separated poleis like Athens, Sparta, Corinth, and Thebes, so each city fiercely kept its own laws, army, and silver coins. Yet almost every polis centred on a rocky Acropolis and a lively Agora marketplace. Even more importantly, proud Hellenes spoke the same Greek language, wrote with the same 24-letter Greek alphabet adapted from Phoenician traders, worshipped the same Olympian gods, and linked their harbours through olive-oil and pottery sea trade!”
Enquiry 2: Athens vs Sparta — Where Would You Rather Grow Up in Ancient Greece? (c. 508–430 BC) TB p.5 • WB pp.5–6
Key Enquiry: How did democratic Athens and warrior Sparta govern their cities differently, and whose lives were freer in each polis?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This second Key Stage 2 enquiry builds civic comparison skills by contrasting sea-trading democratic Athens with land-locked military Sparta (c. 508–430 BC). Primary pupils investigate how Athenian direct democracy worked on the Pnyx hill, through pottery ostrakon ballots and the marble kleroterion jury machine — while critically spotting who was excluded (women, enslaved people, and foreign-born metics). Pupils then contrast Sparta's two kings, elders, helot farmworkers, and age-7 agoge barracks, uncovering the striking historical twist that Spartan girls trained outdoors and Spartan women owned land and spoke far more freely than Athenian women!
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain how direct democracy worked in Athens from c. 508 BC — and identify which residents were excluded from voting.
  • Describe how land-locked Sparta was ruled by two kings and elders and how boys were trained in the agoge from age seven.
  • Compare growing up as a boy or girl in Athens versus Sparta to reach an evidenced historical judgement.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Choosing whether to grow up in Ancient Athens or Ancient Sparta depends hugely on whether you were a boy or a girl! A citizen boy would probably pick Athens, where he studied reading, music, and poetry instead of leaving home at seven for Sparta's freezing barefoot agoge barracks, and grew up to vote freely on the Pnyx hill. However, a girl might strongly prefer Sparta! While Athenian girls stayed mostly indoors weaving and could never vote or own property, Spartan girls trained outdoors in athletics and grew into confident Spartan women who ran estates, owned land, and spoke freely in public.”

Enquiry 3: Mount Olympus, Myths & The Ancient Olympics — How Did Religion Shape Greek Life? (776–400 BC) TB p.6 • WB pp.7–8
Key Enquiry: How did belief in the Olympian gods inspire Greek temples, hero myths, and the Ancient Olympic Games?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This third Key Stage 2 enquiry explores how shared religion, mythology, and pan-Hellenic festivals shaped everyday Greek culture (776–400 BC). Primary pupils meet the Twelve Olympians on Mount Olympus (Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Athena, Apollo, Artemis, Hermes), discover why marble temples like the Parthenon housed sacred statues while sacrifices happened outdoors, and visit the Oracle at Delphi. Pupils read Greek myths directly off black-figure and red-figure clay vases (Heracles and the Hydra, Odysseus, Medusa) before investigating the Ancient Olympic Games held every four years at Olympia from 776 BC under the Sacred Truce (Ekecheiria).
Learning Objectives:
  • Describe the key Olympian gods on Mount Olympus and explain how Greeks worshipped at marble temples like the Parthenon.
  • Explain why Greeks visited the Oracle at Delphi and how black-figure and red-figure vases preserve famous myths.
  • Explain why the Ancient Olympic Games began at Olympia in 776 BC and how the Sacred Truce united warring poleis.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Religion was never separate from everyday life in Ancient Greece — instead, honouring the gods shaped almost everything Greeks did! Belief in the twelve Olympians on snowy Mount Olympus inspired cities to crown their hills with marble temples like Athena's Parthenon and gather around outdoor altars. Before wars or voyages, leaders climbed mountain Delphi to ask Apollo's Oracle for guidance, while potters painted myths of Heracles and the Hydra onto everyday clay vases. Most remarkably, honouring Zeus at Olympia from 776 BC paused wars under the Sacred Truce for the Ancient Olympic Games!”
Enquiry 4: Marathon, Thermopylae & Salamis — How Did Tiny City-States Defeat the Persian Empire? (490–479 BC) TB p.7 • WB pp.9–10
Key Enquiry: How did tiny, rival Greek city-states unite to defeat the mighty Persian Empire between 490 and 479 BC?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This fourth Key Stage 2 enquiry explores one of history’s most dramatic turning points: the Greco-Persian Wars (490–479 BC). Primary pupils investigate why Darius I and Xerxes of the Achaemenid Persian Empire invaded mainland Greece, and analyse how outnumbered Greek poleis triumphed through citizen hoplite phalanx teamwork at Marathon (490 BC), King Leonidas’s delaying stand at the mountain pass of Thermopylae (480 BC), and Admiral Themistocles’s naval trap using 170-oar wooden triremes inside the narrow Strait of Salamis (480 BC).
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain why the Persian Empire invaded Greece in 490 BC and how the Athenian phalanx won at Marathon.
  • Analyse how Spartan King Leonidas and Greek allies used mountain geography at Thermopylae in 480 BC.
  • Explain how Themistocles and 170-oar wooden triremes defeated Persia’s fleet in the Strait of Salamis.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Tiny Greek city-states defeated the mighty Persian Empire through three vital strengths: teamwork, weaponry, and clever tactics. First, at Marathon in 490 BC, heavily armoured citizen hoplites locked bronze shields into a charging phalanx to beat Darius I’s archers. Second, when Xerxes returned in 480 BC, rival poleis like Sparta and Athens united, as King Leonidas’s brave stand at narrow Thermopylae bought precious time. Most decisive of all, Admiral Themistocles’s 170-oar wooden triremes trapped and sank Xerxes’s fleet inside the narrow Strait of Salamis!”

Enquiry 5: Theatre, Philosophy, Maths & Medicine — How Did Greek Thinkers Change Human Knowledge? (c. 460–322 BC) TB p.8 • WB pp.11–12
Key Enquiry: How did Ancient Greek playwrights, philosophers, doctors, and mathematicians transform human knowledge?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This fifth Key Stage 2 enquiry explores the intellectual flowering of Classical and Hellenistic Greece. Primary pupils examine open-air hillside amphitheatres such as Epidaurus (tragedies, comedies, and expressive masks), meet Athens’s great philosophers ("lovers of wisdom": Socrates, Plato’s Academy, and Aristotle’s natural classification), contrast Hippocrates’s clinical observation on Kos with earlier supernatural explanations of illness, and trace breakthroughs in geometry and physics by Pythagoras, Euclid, and Archimedes.
Learning Objectives:
  • Describe how open-air Greek amphitheatres like Epidaurus staged tragedies and comedies using masks and acoustics.
  • Explain what "philosopher" means and how Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle shaped careful thinking and science.
  • Assess the significance of Hippocrates in medicine and Pythagoras, Euclid, and Archimedes in maths and levers.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Ancient Greek thinkers transformed human knowledge across philosophy, medicine, and mathematics. In Athens, philosophers ("lovers of wisdom") led by Socrates, Plato at the Academy, and Aristotle taught people to ask sharp questions and classify plants and animals scientifically. On Kos, Hippocrates saved lives by observing natural symptoms, while Pythagoras, Euclid, and Archimedes unlocked geometry and levers. Overall, philosophy’s habit of testing ideas with reason and evidence mattered most because it powered both Greek medicine and Greek mathematics!”
Enquiry 6: Alexander the Great & Our World Today — What Is Ancient Greece’s Lasting Legacy? (336–146 BC & Today) TB p.9 • WB pp.13–14
Key Enquiry: How did Alexander the Great spread Greek culture, and what is Ancient Greece’s lasting legacy in our world today?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This capstone Key Stage 2 enquiry traces how Greek ideas spread across the ancient world and live on today. Primary pupils follow Alexander the Great of Macedon (taught by Aristotle, riding Bucephalus) from 336 to 323 BC across Egypt, Persia, and north-west India, explore Hellenistic Alexandria’s Great Library and Pharos Lighthouse, note Rome’s conquest of Greece in 146 BC alongside Roman admiration for Greek teachers and art, and evaluate Ancient Greece’s everyday legacy in Britain and the modern world: democracy and juries, Greek-rooted English words, Doric/Ionic/Corinthian columns, geometry, and the 1896 Athens Modern Olympic Games.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain how Alexander the Great created a vast empire by age 32 and spread Hellenistic Greek culture.
  • Describe Alexandria’s Great Library and Pharos Lighthouse, and explain why conquering Romans admired Greece after 146 BC.
  • Identify and evaluate Ancient Greece’s lasting legacy in Britain and the world today.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “More than two thousand years on, Ancient Greece leaves an extraordinary legacy across our daily lives. Every school day we speak Greek-rooted words like school, dinosaur, planet, music, theatre, and history, while studying Euclid’s geometry! Our grand buildings display Doric, Ionic, and Corinthian columns, doctors honour Hippocrates, and athletes compete in the Modern Olympic Games revived at Athens in 1896. Above all, Greece’s greatest legacy is democracy, juries, and free questioning — giving ordinary citizens a voice in shaping fair laws!”
