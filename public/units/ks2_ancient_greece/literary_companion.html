Enquiry 1 Classic Literature, Myth & Verse Ancient Greek Epic Verse

The Odyssey (Sailing the Wine-Dark Aegean)

Homer (Ancient Greek Epic Tradition, c. 750 BC)
Historical Context: Even when steep mountains split Ancient Greece into rival city-states, every Hellene loved chanting blind Homer's sea adventures! Read these rhythmic lines aloud to feel the splash of pine oars across the island-dotted Aegean Sea.
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Aegean Sea[ee-JEE-un SEE]
The island-filled sea between mainland Greece and modern Turkey that served as Greece's busy water highway.
Polis[POH-liss]
An independent Greek city-state—such as Athens, Sparta, or Corinth—that guarded its own laws and coins.
Hellenes[HEL-eenz]
The shared family name proud Greeks used for themselves across every mainland valley and Aegean island.
Bard[BARD]
A skilled travelling poet-singer who chanted long epic tales like Homer's Odyssey from memory to lyre music.

Nearly three thousand years ago, travelling poets called bards stood in torchlit Greek halls and chanted adventure tales from memory. Across mountain valleys and scattered islands, every Hellene family knew Homer's Odyssey—the ten-year voyage of clever Odysseus sailing home to rocky Ithaca.

As soon as early, rosy-fingered Dawn lit up the sky,
Odysseus called his eager shipmates down along the shore.
They climbed aboard the hollow ship and sat upon the benches,
Then struck the grey salt water all together with their oars!
Athena sent a singing breeze that raced behind the stern,
Rippling across the wine-dark sea and foaming round the prow.
They raised the tall pine mast on high and spread the shining sail,
As rocky islands slipped behind and dolphins leaped ahead!

Whenever Greek merchants loaded golden olive oil and painted clay cups onto wooden ships, Homer's sea-songs echoed across the waves. Even rival city-states felt like one Hellene family whenever a bard began to sing!

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. Which two famous picture-phrases (epithets) does Homer use to describe sunrise and the deep Aegean water?
2. How did Odysseus's crew move their wooden ship forward first, and what changed once goddess Athena sent a breeze?
3. Why did sea voyages and shared poems like Homer's 'Odyssey' matter so much in a land divided by steep mountains?

History of the Peloponnesian War (Pericles's Speech to Athens)

Pericles, recorded by Eyewitness Thucydides (431 BC)
Historical Context: In 431 BC, Athenian general Pericles stood on a high platform before thousands of families while historian Thucydides listened in the crowd. Hear how proudly Pericles compared open, voting Athens with tough warrior Sparta!
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Demokratia[dem-oh-KRAH-tee-uh]
Athens's invention of 'people-power', where adult male citizens debated openly and raised hands to vote on every law.
Agoge[uh-GOH-gay]
Sparta's tough army barracks training system that Spartan boys entered barefoot at just seven years old.
Pnyx[puh-NIKS]
The rocky hillside in Athens where thousands of male citizens met outdoors every few days to debate and vote.
Thucydides[thoo-SID-ih-deez]
The sharp-eyed Athenian general and historian who listened to speeches in person and wrote careful eyewitness history.

In the winter of 431 BC, thousands of families gathered outside the walls of Athens. Eyewitness writer Thucydides stood in the crowd and took careful notes. Climbing onto a high wooden stage, general Pericles explained why Athens looked so different from rival Sparta.

"Our city is called a democracy because power rests in the hands of the many, not a tiny few! Every citizen stands equal before our laws. Even a poor man can serve Athens if he brings wise ideas. We never copy harsh rules or chase curious guests out of our busy market."

"Look at how differently we raise our boys! In Sparta, boys march into tough barracks life from early childhood just to make them brave. Here in Athens, boys enjoy books, music, and open talk. Yet when danger strikes, free men stand just as bravely beside their friends!"

Across Greece, listeners still argued fiercely. Did open Athens or tough Sparta raise stronger citizens?

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. Why did Pericles say Athens deserved the name 'democracy' (demokratia)?
2. How did Pericles contrast childhood for boys in Sparta with childhood for boys in Athens?
3. What might a proud Spartan girl or woman have answered back if she had heard Pericles boasting about Athenian freedom?

The Contest for Athens: Athena and Poseidon

Traditional Ancient Greek Myth (Acropolis & Parthenon Pediment Tradition)
Historical Context: Sculptors carved this famous contest high onto the marble Parthenon temple in Athens! Read aloud to discover how wise Athena beat sea-god Poseidon—and why Olympic champions prized humble olive leaves above gold.
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Mount Olympus[MOWNT oh-LIM-pus]
Greece's tallest snow-capped mountain, believed to be the cloud-ringed palace of the twelve Olympian gods.
Parthenon[PAR-thuh-non]
The gleaming marble Doric temple built atop Athens's Acropolis to honour patron goddess Athena.
Trident[TRY-dunt]
Sea-god Poseidon's mighty three-pronged bronze spear used to stir storms and crack solid rock.
Kotinos[KOT-ih-nos]
The sacred crown woven from wild olive branches placed on victorious athletes' heads at the Ancient Olympics.

High on the windy limestone rock of the Acropolis, Greek storytellers pointed to a gnarled olive tree and told how Athens won its name. Two mighty Olympians both wanted to guard the young city: sea-god Poseidon and grey-eyed wisdom goddess Athena. Zeus sat in judgement and promised the city to whichever god offered mortals the wiser gift.

Poseidon strode across the rock and swung his bronze-tipped trident,
Splitting the stone until a spring leaped high into the sun!
The crowd cheered loud, then spat in shock to taste the salty water,
For bitter sea-spray could not feed a farmer's dry hillside.
Then wise Athena knelt in peace and touched the dusty summit,
And up there sprang a silver-leafed and fruit-filled olive tree!
Its wood gave warmth, its fruit gave food, its golden oil lit lamps,
So joyful citizens named Athens after their clever guide!

Even at Zeus's Olympic Games from 776 BC, champions wore simple kotinos crowns woven from sacred olive leaves.

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. Why were the citizens disappointed right after Poseidon struck the Acropolis rock with his bronze trident?
2. Name three practical everyday uses listed in the second stanza that made Athena's olive tree such a clever gift.
3. What does crowning Olympic winners with wild olive leaves instead of heavy gold tell us about Ancient Greek beliefs?

The Histories (The Persian Scout at Thermopylae)

Herodotus of Halicarnassus (c. 440 BC)
Historical Context: Called the 'Father of History', Greek traveller Herodotus interviewed veterans across Greece and Persia so brave deeds would never fade. Listen to the astonishing sight King Xerxes's scout saw at the narrow pass of Thermopylae in 480 BC!
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Thermopylae[ther-MOP-ih-lee]
A narrow Greek coastal pass between steep cliffs and the sea where King Leonidas blocked Persia's army in 480 BC.
Hoplites[HOP-lyts]
Heavily armoured Greek foot soldiers who locked round bronze shields and long spears together in a tight phalanx wall.
Herodotus[huh-ROD-uh-tus]
The 5th-century BC Greek writer remembered as the 'Father of History' for investigating the Persian Wars.
Leonidas[lee-ON-ih-dus]
The brave Spartan king who held the pass of Thermopylae for three days with 300 Spartans and Greek allies.

In summer 480 BC, Persian King Xerxes marched a huge army south towards the narrow pass of Thermopylae. Greek writer Herodotus spoke to war veterans and told what happened next. Xerxes sent a lone horseman ahead to spy on King Leonidas's small camp.

"Riding near the stone wall across the pass, the Persian scout stared in shock. Only three hundred Spartans stood on guard in front of the wall that morning. Instead of shaking at Persia's giant army, some Spartans were exercising in the sun. Others sat calmly combing and braiding their long hair!"

"When the scout rode back, King Xerxes laughed aloud and called those Greeks fools. Yet an exiled Spartan in his camp spoke quietly. 'O King, Spartans always comb their hair neatly before risking their lives in fight. Beat these brave men, and no army left in Greece will dare lift a spear against you!'"

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. What two unexpected things were the three hundred Spartans doing outside the wall when the Persian scout rode up?
2. Why did King Xerxes laugh at first, and how did the exiled Spartan explain the real meaning of the combed hair?
3. Why was the narrow mountain-and-sea geography of Thermopylae (and later the Strait of Salamis) so helpful to outnumbered Greeks?

The Apology of Socrates (The Buzzing Horsefly of Athens)

Plato (Eyewitness at the Trial of Socrates, 399 BC)
Historical Context: Athenians loved debating fairness in stone theatres like Epidaurus and down in the sunny Agora. In 399 BC, young student Plato sat in court and wrote down how barefoot philosopher Socrates bravely defended asking awkward questions!
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Philosopher[fih-LOS-uh-fer]
From Greek words meaning 'lover of wisdom'—a thinker who asks deep questions about truth, fairness, and nature.
Socrates[SOK-ruh-teez]
The barefoot Athenian philosopher who questioned powerful citizens in the Agora instead of writing books.
Plato[PLAY-toh]
Socrates's devoted student who recorded his teacher's conversations and opened the Academy school in Athens.
Jury[JOOR-ee]
Five hundred ordinary Athenian citizens chosen by the marble kleroterion machine to hear speeches and vote in court.

Whether watching masked actors on stone theatre steps or chatting in the sunny Agora, Athenians loved bold debates. Nobody asked sharper questions than seventy-year-old Socrates. He walked barefoot in an old wool cloak and never charged a coin. In 399 BC, young Plato sat in court and wrote down Socrates's calm words before five hundred jurors.

"Men of Athens, suppose you offer to set me free today on one strict rule. You ask me to stop asking questions and hunting for truth. Friends, I cannot stay quiet! While I still breathe, I will nudge everyone in the market to care less about gold and more about goodness."

"Great Athens is like a proud warhorse grown sleepy because of its size. I am simply the stubborn little horsefly sent to buzz round your ears and wake you up! An unexamined life is barely worth living." Brave to the end, Socrates kept questioning right until sunset.

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. What funny, vivid creature did seventy-year-old Socrates compare himself to in front of the five hundred jurors?
2. Why did Socrates's constant questions in the Agora annoy some rich and powerful leaders in Athens?
3. How did Socrates's habit of testing beliefs inspire later Greek thinkers like Plato, Aristotle, and doctor Hippocrates?

Turn Him Into the Sun: Young Alexander and Bucephalus

The History Revision Hub Narrative Archive
Historical Context: Before young Prince Alexander of Macedon studied with Aristotle or marched as far as Egypt and India, everyone at his father's court laughed at a wager he made over an 'untameable' black horse!
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Macedon[MASS-ih-don]
The rugged northern Greek kingdom ruled by King Philip II and his famous son Alexander the Great.
Bucephalus[byoo-SEF-uh-lus]
Alexander's beloved black warhorse—his Greek name meant 'Ox-Head' after a broad white mark on his brow.
Hellenistic[hel-uh-NIS-tik]
The lively shared culture created when Alexander's conquests blended Greek language and learning with Egyptian and Persian ideas.
Legacy[LEG-uh-see]
Lasting ideas, words, buildings, and traditions handed down from an ancient civilisation to our world today.

Dust swirled across a royal field in Macedon as a tall black stallion reared and snorted wildly. Named Bucephalus—meaning "Ox-Head" for a white star on his brow—the horse cost a small fortune! Yet whenever King Philip's strongest grooms stepped close, Bucephalus kicked out with flashing hooves.

Annoyed, King Philip waved the horse away, until young Prince Alexander spoke up boldly. "What a fine horse they are losing, just because nobody has the skill or heart to handle him!" Laughing nobles dared the boy to try. While grown men stared at the hooves, sharp-eyed Alexander spotted one quiet clue on the grass.

Running forward, Alexander took the bridle and turned Bucephalus straight into the bright sun. At once, the shaking horse grew calm! His own dark shadow darting ahead on the grass had scared him. Alexander leaped onto his back and rode across the plain on the loyal steed who would carry him to India.

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. What tiny detail on the sunny grass did young Alexander notice that all of King Philip's experienced grooms had missed?
2. How did turning Bucephalus straight towards the sun solve the problem without using a whip or force?
3. Why does this boyhood story match the way Aristotle trained Alexander to observe the world?

Parent, Carer & Tutor Conversation Guide & Answer Key Ancient Greece
No Specialist History Knowledge Needed: Pupils do not need word-for-word answers—praise curiosity and listen for these core historical ideas during your 3-minute chat!
Enquiry 1 (p. 2): Homer's Wine-Dark Sea
Q1: He calls sunrise 'rosy-fingered Dawn' and describes the deep rolling water at dusk or dawn as 'the wine-dark sea'.
Q2: First they pulled pine oars together from wooden benches; once the wind blew, they raised the pine mast and spread a white sail.
Q3: Overland mountain tracks were slow, so sailing linked distant harbours while Homer's Greek poems united rival poleis as one Hellene culture.
Enquiry 2 (p. 3): Athens vs Warrior Sparta
Q1: Because voting power belonged to the many adult male citizens rather than to a king or a tiny group of rich nobles.
Q2: Spartan boys endured harsh, painful barracks training from age seven, whereas Athenian boys enjoyed books, music, poetry, and open debate.
Q3: She could point out that Athenian women were shut out of voting and school, whereas Spartan girls trained outdoors and women owned land!
Enquiry 3 (p. 4): Athena's Sacred Olive Tree
Q1: Although the leaping fountain looked dramatic at first, the spring gushed salty seawater that nobody could drink or use on crops.
Q2: Olive wood warmed homes, ripe olives fed families, and pressed golden olive oil lit clay lamps (and paid for imported grain!).
Q3: Greeks prized honour, glory for their polis, and sacred gifts from the gods far higher than cash prizes.
Enquiry 4 (p. 5): Spartans at Thermopylae
Q1: Some were calmly exercising in the sun, while others sat combing and braiding their long hair.
Q2: Xerxes thought they were vain or foolish, but combing their hair meant the Spartans had calmly prepared to fight to the death.
Q3: Steep cliffs and narrow channels stopped Persia's huge numbers from surrounding the tight Greek shield-wall or agile triremes.
Enquiry 5 (p. 6): Barefoot Socrates Speaks
Q1: He compared Athens to a proud, sleepy warhorse and himself to a stubborn buzzing horsefly sent to wake citizens up!
Q2: His calm questions exposed when boastful leaders did not really understand justice or truth as well as they claimed.
Q3: Instead of accepting old myths blindly, Greek scholars and doctors began testing ideas, observing nature, and checking real symptoms.
Enquiry 6 (p. 7): Taming Wild Bucephalus
Q1: He noticed Bucephalus was shying and rearing whenever he saw his own dark shadow moving on the ground in front of him.
Q2: Facing the bright sun threw the horse's shadow safely behind him where it could no longer startle him.
Q3: Aristotle taught his pupil to look closely at real evidence and think calmly instead of relying on brute strength alone.
