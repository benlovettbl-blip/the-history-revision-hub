🎯 High Overdue: Key Topic 1.1 (Tehran Conference 1943 — Last appeared: 2019)
Explain one consequence of the Tehran Conference (1943). [4 Marks]
Target: AO1 (demonstrate knowledge and understanding) & AO2 (explain cause and consequence).
Level 2 (3–4 Marks): Specific factual explanation demonstrating a direct, supported consequence using precise causation.
Level 1 (1–2 Marks): Simple or general statement of a consequence with limited contextual development.
Exemplar Model Answer (Level 2 — 4 Marks):
One major consequence of the Tehran Conference was that the Western Allies formally committed to opening a Second Front in Western Europe to relieve pressure on the Soviet Red Army. Meeting for the first time in November 1943, Churchill, Roosevelt, and Stalin agreed that the USA and Britain would launch Operation Overlord—the amphibious invasion of Nazi-occupied France—in May 1944. In return, Stalin agreed to launch a simultaneous major offensive on the Eastern Front (Operation Bagration) and promised to join the war against Japan once Germany was defeated. Consequently, this established the military strategy that liberated Europe, but also guaranteed that the Red Army would advance deep into Central and Eastern Europe, laying the territorial foundation for Stalin's post-war satellite sphere.
🎯 Specification Gap: Key Topic 3.3 (Dissolution of Warsaw Pact 1991 — Exam count: 0)
Explain one consequence of the dissolution of the Warsaw Pact (1991). [4 Marks]
Target: AO1 & AO2. Mark out of 4 using standard Edexcel 2-level tariff.
Level 2 (3–4 Marks): Specific factual explanation demonstrating a direct, supported consequence.
Level 1 (1–2 Marks): Simple or general statement of a consequence.
Exemplar Model Answer (Level 2 — 4 Marks):
One decisive consequence of the dissolution of the Warsaw Pact in July 1991 was the total collapse of Soviet military hegemony in Eastern Europe and the dramatic shift of former communist states towards Western integration. Formed in 1955 as Moscow's mutual defense alliance, the pact had been used repeatedly to enforce Soviet control through invasions of Hungary (1956) and Czechoslovakia (1968). When newly democratic governments in Poland, Hungary, and Czechoslovakia demanded the withdrawal of all Soviet troops, the alliance formally disbanded. Consequently, this left the Soviet Union militarily isolated in Europe, eliminated the Eastern European buffer zone that Russian leaders had relied upon since 1945, and cleared the path for former satellite states to eventually join NATO and the European Union.

Exemplar Model Answer (Level 3 — 8 Marks):
The era of the 'Second Cold War' was triggered in December 1979 when the Soviet Union invaded Afghanistan to prop up a failing communist regime, prompting US President Jimmy Carter to declare that détente was dead, withdraw the SALT 2 treaty from Senate ratification, and organize a 65-nation boycott of the 1980 Moscow Olympic Games.

Tensions escalated sharply with the election of President Ronald Reagan in November 1980. Rejecting containment and détente, Reagan championed a policy of confrontation, branding the Soviet Union an 'evil empire' in 1983 and dramatically boosting the US defense budget by over 40%. In March 1983, Reagan announced the Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI or 'Star Wars')—a satellite-based anti-ballistic missile defense system—which threatened to nullify the USSR's nuclear deterrent. Relations plummeted further in September 1983 when a Soviet Su-15 interceptor shot down Korean Air Lines Flight 007 after it strayed into Soviet airspace, killing 269 civilians. In November 1983, NATO's realistic military exercise 'Able Archer 83' led paranoid Soviet leaders under Yuri Andropov to believe a Western nuclear strike was imminent, placing Soviet nuclear bombers on emergency alert.

In retaliation for the 1980 US action, the Soviet bloc boycotted the 1984 Los Angeles Olympic Games. However, by 1985, the acute confrontation and the massive US arms buildup had pushed the stagnating Soviet economy to the brink of collapse. The succession of Mikhail Gorbachev as General Secretary in March 1985 marked the turning point: recognizing that the USSR could not win the arms race, Gorbachev abandoned confrontation, opening the way for the summit diplomacy that ended the Cold War.
🎯 Explain Two of Three Options (Each marked out of 8, Total = 16 Marks)
Question 3: Explain the Importance of Two of the Following: [16 Marks Total]
Target: AO1 & AO2 (16 marks total • 8 marks per chosen option). Candidates must answer exactly TWO options.

Option A: The importance of the creation of NATO (1949) for superpower tensions. [8 Marks]

Exemplar Model Answer (Level 3 — 8 Marks):
The creation of the North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) in April 1949 was critically important for superpower tensions because it formalized the permanent military division of Europe and committed American nuclear power to Western defense.

Firstly, shocked by the Berlin Blockade and the Soviet-backed communist coup in Czechoslovakia, twelve Western nations (including the USA, Britain, Canada, and France) signed the North Atlantic Treaty. Under Article 5, all member states agreed that an armed attack against one would be considered an attack against all. This marked a historic transformation in American foreign policy: for the first time in peacetime, the USA entered into an entangling military alliance outside the Western Hemisphere, committing its troops and nuclear arsenal to defend Western Europe from Soviet invasion.

Secondly, NATO significantly intensified Soviet fears of capitalist encirclement. Stalin interpreted the alliance not as a defensive pact, but as an aggressive, imperialist coalition designed to threaten the USSR. In response, when West Germany was admitted to NATO in May 1955, the Soviet Union formed its own rival military alliance—the Warsaw Pact—integrating the armed forces of seven Eastern European satellite states. The creation of NATO thus established the permanent balance of terror and mutual deterrence that characterized the entire Cold War.

Option B: The importance of the Camp David and Vienna summit meetings (1959–61) for the status of Berlin. [8 Marks]

Exemplar Model Answer (Level 3 — 8 Marks):
The summit meetings between Eisenhower, Kennedy, and Khrushchev between 1959 and 1961 were critically important for the status of Berlin because they highlighted the irresolvable crisis over the divided city that culminated in the Berlin Wall.

Firstly, at the Camp David Summit (September 1959), Nikita Khrushchev visited President Eisenhower in the United States. Following Khrushchev's aggressive November 1958 Berlin Ultimatum (demanding Western troops leave Berlin within six months), the summit provided a temporary thaw: Khrushchev withdrew his ultimatum and agreed to further negotiations, establishing the 'spirit of Camp David'. However, this fragile progress was shattered in May 1960 when Soviet air defenses shot down an American U-2 spy plane piloted by Gary Powers over Sverdlovsk, causing Khrushchev to storm out of the Paris Summit.

Secondly, at the Vienna Summit (June 1961), Khrushchev met the newly inaugurated, inexperienced President John F. Kennedy. Viewing Kennedy as weak following the Bay of Pigs disaster, Khrushchev aggressively reissued his Berlin Ultimatum, threatening war if Western forces did not vacate West Berlin. Kennedy refused to yield, declaring that the USA would defend West Berlin and requesting an additional $3.25 billion in defense spending. Faced with Kennedy's resolute stance and the continuing escape of 40,000 East Germans per month, Khrushchev concluded that military confrontation was too dangerous, ordering instead the construction of the Berlin Wall in August 1961 to physically seal the border.

Option C: The importance of Mikhail Gorbachev's policies of Glasnost and Perestroika for the Soviet Union. [8 Marks]

Exemplar Model Answer (Level 3 — 8 Marks):
Mikhail Gorbachev's dual policies of Glasnost ('openness') and Perestroika ('restructuring'), introduced after 1985, were of historic importance because they inadvertently caused the political disintegration of the Soviet Union.

Firstly, Perestroika sought to revitalize the stagnant Soviet command economy by introducing limited private enterprise, allowing cooperatives, and decentralizing economic decision-making. However, the reforms failed disastrously: they dismantled state price controls and planning without establishing a functioning free market, leading to severe food shortages, hyperinflation, and widespread worker strikes. This destroyed public faith in the economic capability of the communist system.

Secondly, Glasnost permitted freedom of speech, historical investigation, and press transparency to build support for reform against conservative communist bureaucrats. Instead of revitalizing socialism, Glasnost unleashed devastating revelations: Soviet citizens learned the truth about Stalinist purges, the Katyn massacre, the Chernobyl nuclear disaster cover-up, and the lavish corruption of Party elites. Furthermore, it empowered long-suppressed nationalist movements in the non-Russian republics (such as the Baltic states, Ukraine, and Georgia) to demand complete independence. When Gorbachev refused to use military violence to suppress these movements, Glasnost directly catalyzed the collapse of the Soviet Union in December 1991.
