Paper 2: Superpower relations and the Cold War, 1941–91 • 32 Marks Total • Option P4 (1HI0/P4) • Official Specification Mock
Key Topic 1: Early Cold War (Eastern Europe Takeover 1945–48)
Explain one consequence of the Soviet takeover of Eastern Europe (1945–48). [4 Marks]
Target: AO1 (demonstrate knowledge and understanding) & AO2 (explain cause and consequence).
Level 2 (3–4 Marks): Specific factual explanation demonstrating a direct, supported consequence using precise causation.
Level 1 (1–2 Marks): Simple or general statement of a consequence with limited contextual development.
Exemplar Model Answer (Level 2 — 4 Marks):
One major consequence of the Soviet takeover of Eastern Europe was the permanent division of Europe into two hostile ideological and military blocs, triggering intense Western alarm. Through 'salami tactics'—gradually eliminating political opposition, manipulating elections, and establishing coalition governments that were later purged—Stalin imposed communist puppet regimes across Poland, Romania, Bulgaria, Hungary, and Czechoslovakia by 1948. Consequently, this convinced Western leaders like Truman and Churchill that Stalin had broken his Yalta promise of free democratic elections, leading directly to Churchill's March 1946 'Iron Curtain' speech and the launch of the American policy of containment.
Key Topic 2: Cold War Crises (Prague Spring 1968)
Explain one consequence of the Prague Spring (1968). [4 Marks]
Target: AO1 & AO2. Mark out of 4 using standard Edexcel 2-level tariff.
Level 2 (3–4 Marks): Specific factual explanation demonstrating a direct, supported consequence.
Level 1 (1–2 Marks): Simple or general statement of a consequence.
Exemplar Model Answer (Level 2 — 4 Marks):
One major consequence of the Prague Spring was the formulation and enforcement of the Brezhnev Doctrine across the Soviet bloc. When Alexander Dubček introduced 'socialism with a human face'—granting press freedom, legalizing rival political groups, and relaxing state censorship—Soviet leader Leonid Brezhnev feared this democratic contagion would unravel the Warsaw Pact. On 20 August 1968, 500,000 Warsaw Pact troops invaded Czechoslovakia to crush the reforms and arrest Dubček. Brezhnev subsequently declared that the USSR had the right to intervene militarily in any socialist country where communist rule was threatened, terrifying satellite states into obedience and demonstrating that Moscow would never tolerate political liberalization within its empire.

Target: AO1 (4 marks) & AO2 (4 marks).

Exemplar Model Answer (Level 3 — 8 Marks):
Following the failed CIA-sponsored Bay of Pigs invasion in April 1961, Fidel Castro turned to the Soviet Union for military protection, declaring Cuba a socialist republic. In the summer of 1962, Soviet Premier Nikita Khrushchev initiated Operation Anadyr, secretly transporting medium- and intermediate-range nuclear ballistic missiles to Cuba to redress the superpower missile balance and deter another US invasion.

The crisis erupted into acute peril on 14 October 1962 when an American U-2 spy plane piloted by Major Richard Heyser took photographic proof of Soviet missile launch sites being constructed near San Cristóbal, capable of striking major US cities within minutes. President John F. Kennedy convened EXCOMM (the Executive Committee of the National Security Council), which debated between an immediate airstrike or an invasion. On 22 October, Kennedy addressed the American public, announcing a naval 'quarantine' (blockade) around Cuba to stop Soviet ships carrying military equipment, and placing US forces on DEFCON 2—the highest alert level short of war.

Tensions peaked on 27 October ('Black Saturday') when a Soviet SAM battery shot down an American U-2 plane over Cuba, killing the pilot. However, secret diplomacy between Robert Kennedy and Soviet Ambassador Anatoly Dobrynin resolved the standoff: Khrushchev agreed to dismantle and withdraw all missiles from Cuba under UN inspection, while Kennedy publicly pledged that the USA would never invade Cuba, and secretly agreed to withdraw US Jupiter nuclear missiles from Turkey. The resolution avoided nuclear armageddon and prompted both superpowers to implement new crisis communication measures, including the Moscow-Washington Hotline.
🎯 Explain Two of Three Options (Each marked out of 8, Total = 16 Marks)
Question 3: Explain the Importance of Two of the Following: [16 Marks Total]
Target: AO1 & AO2 (16 marks total • 8 marks per chosen option). Candidates must answer exactly TWO options.

Option A: The importance of the Potsdam Conference (1945) for relations between the USA and the Soviet Union. [8 Marks]

Exemplar Model Answer (Level 3 — 8 Marks):
The Potsdam Conference in July–August 1945 was critically important for superpower relations because it revealed the irreconcilable differences between the wartime allies and hardened ideological suspicions.

Firstly, personal dynamics had shifted drastically: President Roosevelt had died and was replaced by Harry Truman, who was far more suspicious of Soviet intentions and determined to adopt a 'get tough' stance against Stalin. During the conference, Truman was informed of the successful Trinity atomic bomb test in New Mexico and hinted at this weapon to Stalin. Instead of intimidating the Soviet dictator, this atomic diplomacy deepened Stalin's paranoia, convincing him that the USA intended to use its nuclear monopoly to dictate post-war terms, leading him to accelerate the Soviet crash atomic bomb program.

Secondly, the Big Three clashed bitterly over reparations and Eastern Europe. While they agreed to divide Germany and Berlin into four occupation zones and to demilitarize Germany, Truman rejected Stalin's demand for $20 billion in German reparations, agreeing only that each power could extract reparations from its own zone. This disagreement guaranteed that Germany's economic recovery would proceed along divergent capitalist and communist lines, laying the structural groundwork for the permanent partition of Germany.

Option B: The importance of the Brezhnev Doctrine (1968) for Soviet control of Eastern Europe. [8 Marks]

Exemplar Model Answer (Level 3 — 8 Marks):
The Brezhnev Doctrine was of immense importance for Soviet control of Eastern Europe because it established an ironclad ideological justification for military repression to preserve the Warsaw Pact.

Firstly, the doctrine established that the sovereignty of individual communist states was subordinate to the collective security of the socialist commonwealth; any internal threat to one-party rule was deemed a threat to all. By explicitly declaring that the USSR would invade any satellite state attempting to introduce democratic reforms or leave the Warsaw Pact, Brezhnev extinguished all hopes for evolutionary reform in Eastern Europe for two decades, instilling deep psychological fear and forcing leaders like Gustav Husák in Czechoslovakia to enforce rigid orthodox 'normalization'.

Secondly, while the doctrine successfully preserved Soviet hegemony in the short term, it caused lasting reputational damage to international communism. Western communist parties (Eurocommunism) in Italy and France condemned the Soviet invasion, while Nicolae Ceaușescu of Romania and Mao Zedong of China openly denounced Soviet imperialism. When Mikhail Gorbachev finally abandoned the doctrine in 1989 (the 'Sinatra Doctrine'), the entire satellite empire collapsed within months, proving that Soviet control had rested solely on the threat of military violence.

Option C: The importance of Ronald Reagan's Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI, 1983) for the arms race. [8 Marks]

Exemplar Model Answer (Level 3 — 8 Marks):
Ronald Reagan's Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI, nicknamed 'Star Wars') in March 1983 was exceptionally important for the arms race because it fundamentally disrupted the doctrine of Mutually Assured Destruction (MAD) and forced the Soviet Union into economic exhaustion.

Firstly, SDI proposed creating a space-based anti-ballistic missile defense shield using lasers and particle beams to intercept incoming Soviet ICBMs in flight. If functional, this would eliminate the USSR's second-strike capability and leave the Soviet Union vulnerable to a US nuclear strike without fear of retaliation. This caused profound strategic panic in Moscow, where Soviet leaders believed that SDI was not purely defensive, but designed to give the USA first-strike impunity.

Secondly, SDI exposed the insurmountable economic and technological disparity between the superpowers. With the Soviet economy already stagnating under Brezhnev and devoting over 20% of its GDP to defense, Moscow simply could not afford the computer computing power and billions of dollars required to build an equivalent space shield or expand its missile arsenal. This technological crisis forced Mikhail Gorbachev to conclude that the USSR could no longer sustain the arms race, driving him to the summit negotiating table at Geneva (1985) and Reykjavik (1986) to secure radical nuclear disarmament treaties like the 1987 INF Treaty.
