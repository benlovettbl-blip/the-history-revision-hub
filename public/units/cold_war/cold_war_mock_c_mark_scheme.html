Key Topic 1: Early Cold War (Hungarian Uprising 1956)
Explain one consequence of the Hungarian Uprising (1956). [4 Marks]
Target: AO1 (demonstrate knowledge and understanding) & AO2 (explain cause and consequence).
Level 2 (3–4 Marks): Specific factual explanation demonstrating a direct, supported consequence using precise causation.
Level 1 (1–2 Marks): Simple or general statement of a consequence with limited contextual development.
Exemplar Model Answer (Level 2 — 4 Marks):
One major consequence of the Hungarian Uprising was that it proved to the world that the Western powers would not intervene militarily behind the Iron Curtain to liberate Soviet satellite states. When reformist Prime Minister Imre Nagy announced Hungary's withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact and declared national neutrality, Soviet tanks invaded Budapest in November 1956, killing over 20,000 Hungarians and executing Nagy. Despite Radio Free Europe broadcasts encouraging Hungarian resistance, neither the USA nor NATO provided military support due to fear of triggering a direct nuclear war with the USSR and preoccupation with the simultaneous Suez Crisis. Consequently, this demonstrated that American rhetoric of 'rolling back' communism was empty, confirming Eastern Europe as an undisputed Soviet sphere of influence.
Key Topic 3: End of the Cold War (INF Treaty 1987)
Explain one consequence of the INF Treaty (1987). [4 Marks]
Target: AO1 & AO2. Mark out of 4 using standard Edexcel 2-level tariff.
Level 2 (3–4 Marks): Specific factual explanation demonstrating a direct, supported consequence.
Level 1 (1–2 Marks): Simple or general statement of a consequence.
Exemplar Model Answer (Level 2 — 4 Marks):
One major consequence of the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty was the complete physical destruction of an entire category of nuclear weapons for the first time in history. Signed by Ronald Reagan and Mikhail Gorbachev in Washington in December 1987, the treaty eliminated all ground-launched ballistic and cruise missiles with ranges between 500 and 5,500 kilometers, leading to the destruction of 2,692 missiles by 1991 (including US Pershing II and Soviet SS-20s). Consequently, this eliminated the immediate threat of a tactical nuclear exchange in Central Europe, established unprecedented on-site verification inspections that built genuine trust between the superpowers, and marked the decisive beginning of the end of the Cold War arms race.
Key Topic 2: Détente (Superpower Rapprochement 1969–79)
Write a narrative account analysing the development of détente between 1969 and 1979. [8 Marks]
Target: AO1 (4 marks) & AO2 (4 marks).
Level 3 (6–8 Marks): A sophisticated narrative account that shows clear chronological organisation and explains the causal links between beginning, middle developments, and outcome. Integrates own knowledge beyond the stimulus points.
LevelMarksDescriptor
Level 36–8Accurate, detailed chronological account analysing the process of change. Causal links between stages are clearly explained. Comprehensive own knowledge included.
Level 23–5Accurate narrative with some chronological sequence and causal explanation. Relies primarily on stimulus with some supporting detail.
Level 11–2Simple descriptive statements without clear narrative flow or causal connections.
Exemplar Model Answer (Level 3 — 8 Marks):
In the late 1960s, both superpowers had compelling economic and strategic motives to de-escalate Cold War tensions: the USA was bogged down in the costly Vietnam War and faced economic inflation, while the Soviet Union faced a sluggish agricultural economy and acute ideological friction with Communist China following border clashes along the Ussuri River in 1969.

The breakthrough in détente began with President Richard Nixon's visit to China in February 1972, using 'triangular diplomacy' to pressure Moscow into diplomatic concessions. In May 1972, Nixon traveled to Moscow to sign the landmark SALT 1 (Strategic Arms Limitation Treaty) with Leonid Brezhnev. SALT 1 capped the number of ICBMs and SLBMs each side could deploy and restricted Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) systems to just two sites each, enshrining the principle of nuclear parity and demonstrating that both superpowers could collaborate on arms control.

This constructive spirit reached its diplomatic zenith in August 1975 with the signing of the Helsinki Accords by 35 nations. The Accords established three 'baskets': recognizing post-war European borders (Basket 1), expanding economic and scientific trade (Basket 2), and committing all signatories to respect human rights and fundamental freedoms (Basket 3). Although superpower competition persisted in the Third World, détente stabilized Europe for a decade, until the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979 brought the era of cooperation to an abrupt and acrimonious end.

Question 3: Explain the Importance of Two of the Following: [16 Marks Total]
Target: AO1 & AO2 (16 marks total • 8 marks per chosen option). Candidates must answer exactly TWO options.

Option A: The importance of the Marshall Plan (1947) for the economic recovery of Western Europe. [8 Marks]

Exemplar Model Answer (Level 3 — 8 Marks):
The Marshall Plan (officially the European Recovery Program) was exceptionally important for Western European economic recovery and the containment of communism.

Firstly, Europe in 1947 faced economic ruin: devastated transport infrastructure, severe coal and food shortages, and bankrupt governments. US Secretary of State George Marshall recognized that economic misery provided fertile ground for communist electoral victories, particularly in France and Italy. Between 1948 and 1952, the Marshall Plan pumped over $13 billion in grants, machinery, and raw materials into sixteen Western European nations. This massive injection of capital revived industrial and agricultural production by over 35%, eliminated rationing, and laid the foundations for Western Europe's post-war economic miracle.

Secondly, the Marshall Plan had decisive geopolitical consequences: Stalin condemned it as 'dollar imperialism' designed to build an anti-Soviet capitalist empire. He strictly forbade Soviet satellite nations (such as Czechoslovakia and Poland) from accepting Marshall aid, creating Cominform (1947) to coordinate communist policy and Comecon (1949) to economically integrate Eastern Europe. This deepened the economic and physical partition of Europe, cementing two separate economic spheres.

Option B: The importance of summit meetings between Reagan and Gorbachev (1985–88) for the end of the Cold War. [8 Marks]

Exemplar Model Answer (Level 3 — 8 Marks):
The series of four superpower summit meetings between Ronald Reagan and Mikhail Gorbachev between 1985 and 1988 were critically important because they transformed superpower relations from hostile confrontation into genuine diplomatic partnership.

Firstly, at the Geneva Summit (November 1985), the two leaders established an immediate personal chemistry and issued a joint declaration stating that 'a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought.' This fundamentally reversed Reagan's earlier 'Evil Empire' rhetoric and broke years of diplomatic deadlock. Although the subsequent Reykjavik Summit (October 1986) collapsed over Reagan's refusal to abandon the Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI), the summit proved that both leaders were genuinely willing to contemplate the total abolition of nuclear weapons.

Secondly, this trust culminated in concrete disarmament at the Washington Summit (December 1987), where they signed the historic INF Treaty, eliminating an entire category of intermediate-range nuclear missiles with mutual on-site verification. When Reagan visited Moscow in May 1988 and declared outside the Kremlin that he no longer considered the Soviet Union an 'evil empire', it was clear that ideological enmity had dissolved, enabling Gorbachev to reform Soviet society and withdraw from Eastern Europe without fear of American military attack.

Option C: The importance of the fall of the Berlin Wall (1989) for the collapse of Soviet control in Eastern Europe. [8 Marks]

Exemplar Model Answer (Level 3 — 8 Marks):
The fall of the Berlin Wall on 9 November 1989 was of immense historic importance because it destroyed the defining physical symbol of the Cold War and irreversibly shattered Soviet hegemony in Eastern Europe.

Firstly, the wall had stood since 1961 as the physical embodiment of the Iron Curtain, proving that communist totalitarianism could only survive by forcibly walling in its citizens. When East German official Günter Schabowski mistakenly announced on television that travel restrictions were lifted 'immediately, without delay', tens of thousands of East Berliners converged on border checkpoints like Bornholmer Straße. Border guards, receiving no orders to fire, opened the gates, and jubilant crowds began dismantling the wall with sledgehammers. This peaceful popular uprising demonstrated that the communist regime had completely lost authority.

Secondly, because Mikhail Gorbachev adhered strictly to his pledge not to intervene with Soviet tanks (the 'Sinatra Doctrine'), the opening of the wall triggered an unstoppable domino effect across the satellite empire. Within weeks, the Velvet Revolution peacefully toppled communist rule in Czechoslovakia, Bulgaria's regime fell, and Romania's dictator Nicolae Ceaușescu was overthrown and executed. Crucially, the wall's destruction led directly to the reunification of Germany in October 1990 within NATO, sealing the total victory of the West in Central Europe.
