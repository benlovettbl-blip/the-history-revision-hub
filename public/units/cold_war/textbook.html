HISTORICAL OVERVIEW · 1945–1991 100% Verbatim Curriculum Narrative

Emerging from the ruins of the Second World War, two nuclear-armed superpowers—the capitalist United States and communist Soviet Union—locked the globe in a forty-six-year confrontation. Across spacious two-page enquiry spreads, trace how wartime alliance fractured at Yalta and Potsdam, why Berlin was blockaded and walled, how thirteen days over Cuba brought humanity to the brink of nuclear annihilation, why proxy wars raged in Korea and Vietnam, and how 1989 shattered the Iron Curtain.

1945–1991
KEY HISTORICAL INDIVIDUALS & ACTORS
Harry S. Truman (US President, 1945–53) Architect of Containment

Authorised Hiroshima/Nagasaki, launched the 1947 Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan, and ordered the 1948–49 Berlin Airlift.

Joseph Stalin (Soviet Leader, 1924–53) Creator of the Eastern Bloc

Installed communist satellite states across Eastern Europe, formed Cominform/Comecon, and imposed the Berlin Blockade.

John F. Kennedy (US President, 1961–63) Berlin & Cuban Crisis Leader

Authorised the failed Bay of Pigs invasion, stood firm on West Berlin, and navigated the October 1962 Cuban naval quarantine.

Nikita Khrushchev (Soviet Leader, 1953–64) Peaceful Coexistence & Brinkmanship

Crushed the 1956 Hungarian Uprising, approved the 1961 Berlin Wall, and deployed nuclear missiles to Castro’s Cuba in 1962.

Ronald Reagan (US President, 1981–89) Second Cold War & Summitry

Escalated US defence spending and SDI ("Star Wars") before signing the historic 1987 INF nuclear treaty with Gorbachev.

Mikhail Gorbachev (Soviet Leader, 1985–91) Reformist Final USSR Leader

Launched Glasnost and Perestroika, ended the Brezhnev Doctrine, and allowed peaceful 1989 revolutions across Eastern Europe.

ESSENTIAL DISCIPLINARY CONCEPTS & TERMINOLOGY
Containment US Cold War strategy aimed at preventing communism from spreading beyond countries where it already existed.
Domino Theory US belief that if one Asian nation fell to communism, neighbouring states would topple one after another.
Mutually Assured Destruction (MAD) Nuclear deterrence balance in which a first strike by either superpower guaranteed total annihilation of both.
Proxy War Indirect superpower conflict fought by supplying weapons, money, or troops to rival sides in third countries (Korea, Vietnam, Afghanistan).
NATO & Warsaw Pact Rival military alliances formed in 1949 (US-led Western bloc) and 1955 (Soviet-led Eastern bloc).
Brezhnev Doctrine (1968) Soviet policy declaring Warsaw Pact armies would intervene forcibly if any satellite state tried to abandon communism.
Glasnost & Perestroika Gorbachev’s 1985 reforms introducing political "openness" and economic "restructuring" inside the USSR.
Hotline & Limited Test Ban (1963) Direct Washington–Moscow communication link and ban on atmospheric nuclear tests signed after the Cuban Missile Crisis.

KEY ENQUIRY “Why did rival ideas turn the USA and USSR into enemies after 1945?”
STAGE 1 Incompatible Worldviews: Free-Market Capitalism vs Command Socialism 1917–1924
“Why were US capitalism and Soviet command socialism incompatible?”

On 25 April 1945, American and Soviet soldiers grinned for cameras and shook hands across broken timbers at Torgau while Berlin still burned. Yet as the United States of America and the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR) emerged supreme from the Second World War after defeating Adolf Hitler, wartime unity swiftly fractured.

Washington championed free-market capitalism, an economy built on private business, open trade, and personal wealth. By contrast, Moscow enforced Marxist-Leninist communism and command socialism, abolishing private property so central state planners in Moscow ran all factories, banks, and farms—making lasting peaceful coexistence unsustainable.

STAGE 2 Political Systems: Multi-Party Democracy vs Bolshevik Dictatorship 1920s–1930s
“How did Soviet one-party rule clash with Western liberal democracy?”

Inside Soviet polling stations, citizens received a single pre-printed ballot paper while secret police watched the booths. By contrast, the United States operated as a constitutional liberal democracy founded on universal suffrage, rival multi-party elections, civil liberties, an independent judiciary, and an uncensored press that condemned permanent one-party rule as tyranny.

Governed exclusively by the Communist Party of the Soviet Union (CPSU) and its Politburo, the USSR was a totalitarian one-party dictatorship. Guided by Vladimir Lenin’s Marxist ideology, Moscow dismissed Western parliamentary democracy as a bourgeois sham designed to exploit workers, insisting an iron vanguard party enforce the "dictatorship of the proletariat" and crush dissent.

STAGE 3 Historical Grievances: 1917 Intervention, Appeasement, and the Nazi-Soviet Pact 1918–1939
“Why did 1917 intervention and the 1939 Nazi-Soviet Pact breed mistrust?”

Long before 1945, British, French, and American troops had landed at frozen Russian ports in 1918 to aid anti-Bolshevik White armies during the Russian Civil War. Following the Bolshevik Revolution of 1917, Joseph Stalin never forgot that Western intervention, while 1930s Western appeasement of Hitler convinced Moscow the West wanted Soviet Russia destroyed.

Western democracies felt equally betrayed in August 1939 when Stalin signed the cynical Nazi-Soviet Pact with Hitler in Moscow. Secretly dividing Poland and shipping Soviet oil and grain to Nazi Germany while London endured the Blitz left bitter wounds that reopened immediately once Hitler fell in 1945.

STAGE 4 The Nuclear Monopoly and the Emerging Balance of Terror July–Aug 1945
“How did America’s 1945 nuclear monopoly trigger a superpower arms race?”

Before dawn on 16 July 1945 at Alamogordo in the New Mexico desert, Manhattan Project physicists triggered the Trinity atomic test, fusing desert sand into green glass. When President Harry S. Truman dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945, the United States held a temporary nuclear monopoly that reshaped post-war containment.

Inside the Kremlin, Joseph Stalin viewed that monopoly not merely as a way to end the Pacific war, but as an explicit instrument of atomic blackmail designed to intimidate Moscow in Europe. Refusing to yield, Stalin ordered Soviet physicists to build a Soviet atomic bomb at top speed, sparking a superpower nuclear arms race.

STAGE 5 The Historical Verdict: Geopolitical Vacuum in a Ruined Europe 1945–1946
“Why did Europe’s 1945 power vacuum turn wartime allies into rivals?”

Across shattered Central Europe in late 1945, bombed-out cities and bankrupt European empires left a vast political power vacuum. Standing face to face along the River Elbe in Germany, the Soviet Red Army and Western Allied armies eyed one another across the ruins, each misreading the other’s defensive security steps as plans for aggression.

Older multi-power diplomacy gave way to a tense bipolar superpower rivalry dividing Europe into competing spheres of influence. Because total nuclear destruction threatened both sides, French philosopher Raymond Aron captured the strange new post-war deadlock in six words: "Peace is impossible; war is improbable."

KEY ENQUIRY “Why did wartime allies turn into bitter rivals between Yalta and Potsdam?”
STAGE 1 The Yalta Summit (February 1945): The Big Three’s Fragile Consensus February 1945
“What key compromises did the Big Three agree at Yalta in February 1945?”

Inside the white marble ballroom of Tsar Nicholas II’s former Livadia Palace at the Crimean resort of Yalta in February 1945, an ailing Franklin D. Roosevelt sat between Winston Churchill and Joseph Stalin. Because Adolf Hitler remained undefeated, the Big Three preserved polite wartime unity while planning post-war Europe.

Behind warm banquet toasts, they agreed vital compromises: defeated Germany would disarm and split, alongside Berlin, into four occupation zones run by Britain, the US, the USSR, and France. Stalin also pledged to join the new United Nations to safeguard world peace and to enter the war against Japan.

STAGE 2 The Polish Question and the Declaration on Liberated Europe Feb–Mar 1945
“Why did rival Polish governments fracture Allied trust after Yalta?”

Beneath Yalta’s smiles, Poland exposed a deep gulf over democratic self-determination. Britain had declared war in September 1939 to protect Polish sovereignty and backed the democratic exiled London Poles, whereas Stalin—whose country had suffered twenty-seven million wartime casualties—demanded a loyal Polish buffer zone blocking future invasions from the west.

Although Stalin signed the Declaration on Liberated Europe promising free and unfettered elections, Soviet secret police arrested sixteen non-communist Polish leaders invited to talks that March. By shifting borders westward and installing the pro-Moscow Lublin Polish Communist Government against the London Poles, Stalin shattered Western trust.

From Yalta to Potsdam: End of the Grand Alliance — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 Changing of the Guard: Truman, Attlee, and Stalin at Potsdam July 1945
“How did Truman and Attlee alter diplomacy with Stalin at Potsdam?”

Days after staunchly anti-communist Harry S. Truman replaced Franklin D. Roosevelt in April 1945, the blunt new President scolded Soviet Foreign Minister Vyacheslav Molotov so sharply over Poland that Molotov protested he had never been talked to like that in his life. At Potsdam outside ruined Berlin in July 1945, Truman confronted Moscow head-on.

Midway through the summit, Clement Attlee replaced Winston Churchill after Labour won the British general election, leaving experienced Joseph Stalin facing two new Western leaders. With Nazi Germany’s surrender removing their common Nazi threat, the wartime Grand Alliance turned openly bitter over Eastern Europe.

STAGE 4 The Trinity Test and Truman’s Atomic Diplomatic Leverage July 1945
“Why did Truman’s atomic warning at Potsdam deepen Stalin’s suspicion?”

On 16 July 1945, on the eve of Potsdam, a coded message notified Harry S. Truman that the Trinity nuclear test had succeeded in New Mexico. Emboldened by America’s new atomic monopoly, Truman stiffened his negotiating stance, hoping nuclear leverage would extract Soviet concessions across Eastern Europe.

Strolling over to Joseph Stalin after talks on 24 July 1945, Truman casually remarked that the United States possessed "a new weapon of unusual destructive force." Stalin showed little surprise because Soviet spies had already briefed him, yet Washington’s atomic secrecy convinced him America meant to bully the USSR.

STAGE 5 Reparations and the Division of Germany: The Grand Alliance Dies Aug 1945
“How did disputes over German reparations fracture the Grand Alliance?”

Across eastern Germany in summer 1945, Red Army work crews unbolted steel mills and railway tracks for shipment east. Because Nazi armies had devastated western Russia, Stalin demanded $20 billion in German reparations—with $10 billion directly for the USSR—across the four occupation zones, whereas Truman and Attlee refused because crushing reparations had wrecked the Weimar Republic after 1919.

Under a compromise supervised by the four-power Allied Control Council, each occupier took reparations mainly from its own zone while Moscow traded eastern grain for limited western machinery. Dividing German industry by zone killed the Grand Alliance and hardened Europe into rival spheres of influence.

KEY ENQUIRY “How did containment and the Berlin Blockade divide Europe into rival blocs?”
STAGE 1 Churchill’s Fulton Speech: The Descent of the Iron Curtain 1946
“Why did Soviet satellite states prompt Churchill’s 1946 Fulton speech?”

Between 1945 and 1947, Hungarian communist leader Mátyás Rákosi boasted of slicing away rival democratic parties piece by piece using "salami tactics." Through rigged elections and secret police arrests, Stalin installed obedient satellite regimes across Poland, Romania, Bulgaria, Hungary, and Albania, violating his Yalta pledges.

Speaking beside President Truman at Westminster College in Fulton, Missouri, in March 1946, Winston Churchill warned the Western public that "an iron curtain has descended across the Continent." Furious, Stalin denounced Churchill in Pravda, branding him a warmonger comparable to Adolf Hitler.

STAGE 2 The Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan: The Strategy of Containment 1947
“How did the Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan enact containment in 1947?”

During the freezing rationed winter of early 1947, bankrupt Britain informed Washington it could no longer fund anti-communist forces in Greece and Turkey. On 12 March 1947, President Truman announced the Truman Doctrine before Congress, ending peacetime American isolationism by pledging money and weapons to enact containment for threatened free nations.

Believing poverty and social desperation were the breeding grounds of communism, Secretary of State George Marshall unveiled the Marshall Plan in June 1947. Washington shipped over $13 billion in economic aid—from coal and grain to factory steel—rebuilding Western European farms and industry so voters rejected communism.

The Iron Curtain & The Berlin Blockade — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 Soviet Salami Tactics and Cominform: Consolidating Eastern Europe 1947–1948
“How did Cominform and the 1948 Prague Coup cement Stalin’s eastern bloc?”

When Polish and Czech ministers prepared to join George Marshall’s European Recovery Program talks in June 1947, Stalin summoned them to Moscow and forbade Eastern Europe from accepting a single dollar. Condemning Marshall Aid as an act of American economic imperialism—"dollar imperialism"—Stalin and Andrei Zhdanov founded Cominform (the Communist Information Bureau) in September 1947 to enforce Kremlin discipline.

Eastern Europe’s last democratic coalition fell in February 1948 during the Prague Coup, when armed communist militias seized power in Czechoslovakia and Foreign Minister Jan Masaryk plunged dead from a palace window. Outraged by Prague’s fall, the US Congress swiftly approved Marshall Aid.

STAGE 4 The Berlin Blockade and Operation Vittles: The 11-Month Airlift 1948–1949
“How did the 324-day Berlin Airlift defeat Stalin’s 1948–49 blockade?”

Alarmed when Western Allies merged their German zones into Trizonia and launched the stable Deutsche Mark in June 1948, Stalin moved to starve Western forces out of isolated West Berlin. On 24 June 1948, his Berlin Blockade severed all road, rail, and canal routes connecting West Germany to West Berlin.

Rather than retreating or firing first, Allied crews sustained West Berlin for 324 days via the Berlin Airlift, codenamed Operation Vittles. Cargo planes landed every ninety seconds delivering 2.3 million tons of coal and food—while US pilot Gail Halvorsen dropped chocolate bars on handkerchief parachutes—forcing Stalin to lift the blockade in May 1949.

STAGE 5 The Permanent Division: FRG, GDR, NATO (1949), and the Warsaw Pact 1955
“Why did the Berlin crisis lead to FRG, GDR, NATO, and the Warsaw Pact?”

Stalin’s failed Berlin Blockade sealed Germany’s partition into two separate republics in 1949. That May, the western zones united as the democratic Federal Republic of Germany (FRG, or West Germany) with its capital in Bonn, while Moscow turned its eastern zone in October into the communist German Democratic Republic (GDR).

Determined to deter Soviet aggression, twelve Western nations founded NATO in April 1949 on collective defence. After West Germany joined NATO in 1955, Moscow formed the rival Warsaw Pact—and when Budapest citizens cut the Soviet hammer-and-sickle out of Hungarian flags in 1956, Red Army tanks crushed Hungary’s bid to leave.

KEY ENQUIRY “How close did Cuba bring nuclear war in 1962—and how was it avoided?”
STAGE 1 The Cuban Revolution: Castro, Nationalisation, and the Soviet Alliance 1959–1961
“How did US embargoes and the Bay of Pigs push Castro toward Khrushchev?”

Riding into Havana in January 1959, revolutionary guerrilla leader Fidel Castro overthrew US-backed dictator Fulgencio Batista in Cuba just ninety miles off Florida. When Castro nationalised American-owned sugar plantations, oil refineries, and telephone monopolies, Washington imposed crippling trade embargoes, pushing Havana to sell sugar to Nikita Khrushchev in return for Soviet crude oil and arms.

Hoping to topple Castro covertly, newly inaugurated US President John F. Kennedy authorised the CIA-backed Bay of Pigs invasion by 1,400 Cuban exiles in April 1961. Castro’s troops crushed the landing in three days, humiliating Kennedy and driving Cuba to seek Soviet military protection against a full American invasion.

STAGE 2 The Discovery: U-2 Spy Planes and Operation Anadyr October 1962
“Why did U-2 photos of Soviet missiles in Cuba spark a US crisis in 1962?”

Disguised in checked shirts as farm specialists, 40,000 Soviet troops sailed secretly to Cuba in summer 1962 under Nikita Khrushchev’s Operation Anadyr. Gambling on nuclear brinkmanship, Khrushchev deployed ballistic missiles to shield Cuba, counter US Jupiter missiles in Turkey, and narrow America’s massive 17-to-1 superiority in long-range ICBM warheads.

At dawn on 14 October 1962, a high-altitude U-2 reconnaissance plane piloted by US Air Force Major Richard Heyser photographed Soviet nuclear missile launch pads under construction across palm groves at San Cristóbal. Fired from ninety miles offshore, those warheads could strike Washington and major US cities within three minutes.

Nuclear Brinkmanship: The Cuban Missile Crisis — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 Thirteen Days on the Brink: ExComm, Quarantine, and DEFCON 2 22–24 Oct 1962
“Why did Kennedy choose a naval quarantine over air strikes on Cuba?”

Inside the White House from 16 October, Kennedy’s emergency twelve-member council ExComm (Executive Committee of the National Security Council) debated secretly while hidden tape recorders spun beneath the table. Pentagon generals urged surgical airstrikes and an invasion of Cuba, yet Kennedy feared bombing Soviet troops would trigger immediate Soviet retaliation against West Berlin.

Addressing stunned television viewers on 22 October 1962, Kennedy ordered a US Navy naval quarantine around Cuba—choosing that legal word over a blockade to avoid killing Soviet personnel, avert a counter-attack on West Berlin, and avoid declaring war—while Strategic Air Command moved to DEFCON 2.

STAGE 4 Secret Diplomacy: Submarine B-59, Turkey, and Khrushchev’s Deal 26–27 Oct 1962
“How did secret talks over missiles in Turkey resolve the October crisis?”

Danger peaked on 27 October—"Black Saturday"—when a U-2 reconnaissance plane was shot down over Cuba and US warships dropped signalling depth charges above submerged Soviet submarine B-59. Inside B-59’s stifling hull, Soviet naval officer Vasili Arkhipov refused unanimous consent to launch a nuclear torpedo, averting naval war.

That night in Washington, Attorney General Robert F. Kennedy and Soviet Ambassador Anatoly Dobrynin hammered out a quiet back-channel compromise. Khrushchev dismantled Cuban missile sites under UN inspection in return for America’s public non-invasion pledge and a secret promise to remove US Jupiter missiles in Turkey.

STAGE 5 De-escalation and Deterrence: The Hotline and Partial Test Ban Treaty 1963
“What 1963 arms-control steps followed the Cuban Missile Crisis?”

During the October 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis at DEFCON 2, Khrushchev’s urgent cables had taken hours to decode and pedal across Washington by bicycle courier. Shaken by how close slow messages had come to catastrophe, the White House and the Kremlin installed a direct "hotline" teletype link in June 1963 for rapid crisis talks.

Two months later, in August 1963, the United States, Britain, and the Soviet Union signed the Partial Nuclear Test Ban Treaty, banning nuclear weapons tests in the atmosphere, underwater, and in outer space. Under nuclear deterrence, Mutually Assured Destruction forced both capitals toward calmer restraint.

KEY ENQUIRY “Why did the Cold War turn into deadly wars in Korea and Vietnam?”
STAGE 1 Cold War Globalisation: The Asian Domino Theory and NSC-68 1949–1950
“Why did Mao’s 1949 victory inspire NSC-68 and the US Domino Theory?”

Standing atop Beijing’s Tiananmen Gate in October 1949, communist leader Mao Zedong crowned the Chinese Revolution by proclaiming the People’s Republic of China after defeating Chiang Kai-shek. Coming weeks after the first Soviet atomic bomb test (RDS-1), those twin late-1949 shocks ended Western strategic confidence and globalised the Cold War across Asia.

Policy blueprint NSC-68 in early 1950 urged President Truman to quadruple defence spending—a fourfold surge—to globalise American military containment. Washington’s Domino Theory warned that if one Asian nation fell to communist victory, neighbouring states would topple sequentially like a row of falling dominoes.

STAGE 2 The Korean War (1950–1953): 38th Parallel, Inchon, and Chinese Intervention 1950–1953
“How did the 1950–53 Korean War end in stalemate at the 38th Parallel?”

At dawn on 25 June 1950, Soviet-supplied T-34 tanks commanded by North Korean leader Kim Il-sung rolled south across the 38th Parallel—Korea’s 1945 partition line—turning containment into a bloody proxy war. Communist forces swiftly captured Seoul and pinned South Korean and American defenders inside a narrow coastal perimeter around Pusan.

Commanding United Nations forces, General Douglas MacArthur reversed the tide with a daring amphibious landing at Inchon, but advancing near China’s border provoked a massive Chinese counter-offensive. Fighting ground to an armistice stalemate in July 1953, leaving over three million dead along the fortified 38th Parallel Demilitarized Zone.

Cold War Proxy Wars in Asia: Korea & Vietnam — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 The Indochina Crucible: From Dien Bien Phu to the Gulf of Tonkin 1954–1964
“Why did the 1964 Gulf of Tonkin incident escalate US war in Vietnam?”

Hauling heavy artillery up steep jungle ridges on reinforced bicycles, communist nationalist Ho Chi Minh’s Viet Minh fighters decisively defeated French colonial forces at the battle of Dien Bien Phu in May 1954. That summer, the 1954 Geneva Accords temporarily partitioned Vietnam along the 17th parallel pending 1956 unity elections.

Guided by NSC-68 and fearing Ho would win the 1956 vote, Washington propped up South Vietnam’s anti-communist Saigon regime against southern Vietcong insurgents. After naval clashes during the August 1964 Gulf of Tonkin incident, Congress granted President Lyndon B. Johnson broad war powers, deploying over 500,000 US soldiers.

STAGE 4 Asymmetric Warfare: Cu Chi Tunnels vs Air Mobility and Chemical Weapons 1965–1968
“How did Vietcong tunnels and guerrilla tactics blunt US firepower?”

Beneath ironwood forests outside Saigon, southern Vietcong guerrillas vanished through tiny camouflaged trapdoors into a 250km complex of hand-dug tunnels at Cu Chi housing underground hospitals and kitchens. Supplied from the north by Ho Chi Minh down the jungle Ho Chi Minh Trail, fighters waged asymmetric guerrilla warfare using bamboo booby traps while avoiding open battle.

US commanders answered with a grinding war of attrition, launching helicopter search-and-destroy raids, incendiary napalm, and toxic Agent Orange defoliant to strip jungle cover. Destroying rural villages alienated Vietnamese peasants without breaking guerrilla supplies, while nightly televised carnage fuelled fierce anti-war protests across America.

STAGE 5 The Lessons of Asia: The Limits of Superpower Hegemony 1973–1975
“Why did the Tet Offensive and Saigon’s 1975 fall expose US limits?”

Under cover of Lunar New Year fireworks in January 1968, Vietcong commandos launched the surprise Tet Offensive across over a hundred towns, even blasting into the US Embassy courtyard in Saigon. Although US troops retook every town, televised street combat shattered domestic political will and pushed President Richard Nixon toward Vietnamization, handing combat roles to South Vietnamese troops.

Talks led by Richard Nixon and Henry Kissinger produced the 1973 Paris Peace Accords and American military withdrawal. In April 1975, as evacuees scrambled aboard rooftop helicopters, North Vietnamese tanks captured Saigon, reunifying Vietnam and proving superpower firepower could not defeat determined local nationalism.

KEY ENQUIRY “Why did Soviet rule in Eastern Europe—and the USSR itself—collapse by 1991?”
STAGE 1 The Construction of the Berlin Wall (August 1961) and Checkpoint Charlie August 1961
“Why did East Germany build the Berlin Wall in August 1961?”

Two months before sealing Berlin, East German leader Walter Ulbricht assured reporters, "Nobody has any intention of building a wall." Yet between 1949 and 1961, over 2.7 million East Germans fled west through Berlin—a devastating brain drain of skilled doctors, teachers, and engineers escaping harsh communist rule for Western prosperity.

With Nikita Khrushchev’s approval, Ulbricht’s guards unrolled barbed wire overnight on 13 August 1961 and raised the concrete Berlin Wall around West Berlin. Paired after 1968 with the Brezhnev Doctrine mandating Soviet intervention across every satellite state, the Wall admitted communism had to lock citizens inside.

STAGE 2 Soviet Stagnation, the Afghan Quagmire, and the Cost of the Arms Race 1979–1985
“How did stagnation, Afghanistan, and "Star Wars" exhaust Soviet power?”

Across Moscow by 1982, weary families queued in snow outside state shops for shoes and meat after eighteen years of economic stagnation and corruption under Leonid Brezhnev. Collective farms missed grain targets and Soviet factories lagged behind Western computers while military budgets consumed a quarter of national wealth.

Worse followed after Soviet troops invaded Afghanistan in December 1979, bleeding morale and money in a ten-year war against US-funded mujahideen guerrillas. When US President Ronald Reagan hiked defence spending and proposed his Strategic Defense Initiative ("Star Wars") space missile shield, Moscow faced a high-tech race its stalled economy could not afford.

STAGE 3 Gorbachev’s Reforms: Glasnost, Perestroika, and Ending the Brezhnev Doctrine 1985–1988
“How did Glasnost, Perestroika, and ending force weaken Soviet control?”

Becoming Soviet General Secretary in March 1985 at age 54, Mikhail Gorbachev sought to modernise communism rather than destroy it. He launched Glasnost ("openness"), relaxing censorship to permit press freedom and public debate, alongside Perestroika ("restructuring"), which eased central planning for small businesses yet disrupted state supplies and eroded Party authority.

Crucially, Gorbachev abandoned the Brezhnev Doctrine in favour of the "Sinatra Doctrine," letting Eastern European states choose their own path without Red Army tanks invading. Once citizens realised Moscow would not intervene by force, unstoppable demands for free elections swept the eastern bloc.

STAGE 4 9 November 1989: The Breaching of the Berlin Wall and Velvet Revolutions 9 Nov 1989
“How did Solidarity and Schabowski’s blunder bring down the Wall in 1989?”

Peaceful upheaval gathered pace in June 1989 when Lech Wałęsa’s independent Polish trade union Solidarity swept free elections, and in September Hungary dismantled its barbed-wire border with Austria so thousands of East Germans could escape west. By 9 November 1989, vast crowds were demanding change inside East Berlin.

Shuffling unread notes on live television that evening, East German spokesman Günter Schabowski mistakenly announced new travel regulations took effect "immediately, without delay." Tens of thousands surged to Bornholmer Strasse, overwhelmed guards lifted the barriers, and joyful Berliners climbed the Wall with hammers, bringing peaceful German reunification in 1990.

STAGE 5 The August Coup and the Dissolution of the Soviet Union 25 December 1991
“How did the failed August 1991 coup hasten the USSR’s collapse?”

As nationalist independence movements spread across the USSR’s fifteen constituent republics, hardline communist plotters staged a coup d’état in Moscow in August 1991 and placed Mikhail Gorbachev under house arrest in Crimea. Outside the Russian parliament, Russian President Boris Yeltsin climbed atop a tank to rally Muscovites and defeat the coup.

Rather than saving the USSR, the failed coup shattered Communist Party authority overnight as republic after republic voted for independence. On 25 December 1991, Gorbachev resigned on television, the red Soviet flag was lowered over the Kremlin for the final time, and the USSR dissolved.
