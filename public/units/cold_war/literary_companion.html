Snow in Moscow, Thunder in Missouri: George Kennan & Winston Churchill (1946)

George F. Kennan (Moscow, 22 Feb 1946) & Winston Churchill (Fulton, 5 March 1946)
Historical Context: Once Nazi Germany surrendered in May 1945, the wartime alliance between capitalist, democratic America and communist, one-party Soviet Russia swiftly froze. Inside the snowbound US Embassy in Moscow in February 1946, sick in bed with fever and sinus pain, diplomat George Kennan dictated an urgent 8,000-word warning—just eleven days before Winston Churchill stood in Fulton, Missouri, and gave the dividing line its immortal name.

On 22 February 1946, snow piled high against the windows of the American Embassy in Moscow. Propped on pillows with a fever, diplomat George Kennan dictated an urgent eight-thousand-word cable to Washington. Why was Joseph Stalin refusing to cooperate now that Hitler was dead? Kennan warned that deep Russian fear of invasion had fused with communist ideology. "At the bottom of the Kremlin’s neurotic view of world affairs is a traditional Russian sense of insecurity. Soviet leaders believe there can be no lasting peace with the United States."

Yet Kennan added one crucial insight that shaped forty years of history. Stalin was not reckless like Hitler. Soviet power was deaf to polite words, Kennan wrote, yet "highly sensitive to the logic of force." Whenever Moscow met firm, calm resistance, it backed down. America did not need to fight a third world war. It simply had to contain Soviet expansion.

Eleven days later in Fulton, Missouri, former British Prime Minister Winston Churchill gave that divide its famous name. "From Stettin in the Baltic to Trieste in the Adriatic, an iron curtain has descended across the Continent. Behind that line lie all the capitals of Central and Eastern Europe—Warsaw, Berlin, Prague, Vienna, Budapest, Bucharest, and Sofia." That autumn in Washington, Soviet Ambassador Nikolai Novikov cabled back Moscow's mirror-image fear. Untouched by invasion and holding the atomic bomb, he warned, America was ringing Russia with airbases.

President Harry S. Truman’s Private Potsdam Diary: The Atomic Secret & Stalin’s Nod (July 1945)

Harry S. Truman (Potsdam Diary, 17–25 July 1945 • Harry S. Truman Library)
Historical Context: Between the Yalta summit (February 1945) and the Potsdam summit outside ruined Berlin (July–August 1945), everything changed. Nazi Germany lay defeated, Red Army troops occupied Poland, Franklin Roosevelt died, and blunt Vice President Harry S. Truman took office. Then, on 16 July 1945—the day before Potsdam opened—American scientists secretly detonated the world’s first atomic bomb in the New Mexico desert.

Inside Cecilienhof Palace at Potsdam in July 1945, new American President Harry S. Truman scribbled candid notes in his private diary.

"17 July 1945. Just spent two hours with Stalin. He walked in at noon, looked me straight in the eye, and spoke directly. Most of our sharp clashes already lie over Poland and German reparations. At Yalta, Stalin promised free elections across liberated Europe, yet in Warsaw his secret police have already set up a pro-Moscow regime.

18 July 1945. Secretary of War Stimson handed me the coded cable from New Mexico. 'Operated on this morning. Results seem satisfactory and already exceed expectations.' Thirteen pounds of explosive turned a steel tower into gas and threw a fireball brighter than the midday sun forty thousand feet up. Believe Japs will fold up before Russia comes in!

25 July 1945. We have discovered the most terrible bomb in the history of the world. Yesterday evening after our session I walked casually over to Stalin and mentioned that we had a new weapon of unusual destructive force. The Russian Premier showed no special interest. All he said was that he was glad to hear it and hoped we would make 'good use of it against the Japanese.'"

Truman thought Stalin had not grasped the hint. In fact, Soviet spy Klaus Fuchs inside Los Alamos had already briefed Moscow. Leaving the palace with stone-faced calm, Stalin ordered secret-police chief Lavrentiy Beria to build a Soviet atomic bomb at top speed.

"Uncle Wiggly Wings": Gail Halvorsen, Chocolate Parachutes & Blockaded Berlin (1948)

The History Revision Hub (True Historical Story • Grounded in Gail Halvorsen & Ernst Reuter Records)
Historical Context: On 24 June 1948, after the Western Allies introduced the new Deutschmark currency, Joseph Stalin cut every road, rail line, canal, and power cable into West Berlin to starve 2.2 million citizens into surrender. Refusing either to abandon Berlin or start World War III with tanks, British RAF and American aircrews launched an unprecedented round-the-clock Airlift—where one pilot’s two sticks of chewing gum changed how the world saw Berlin.

In July 1948, twenty-seven-year-old American pilot Lieutenant Gail Halvorsen walked across the tarmac of Tempelhof Airport in blockaded West Berlin. Just three years earlier, Allied bombers had pounded these streets to rubble. Now Joseph Stalin had locked every road, railway, and canal around the city, gambling that hunger and winter darkness would force two million West Berliners to kneel.

Thirty thin children stood gripping the barbed-wire airport fence, watching British RAF Dakotas and American C-54 Skymasters thunder down every ninety seconds with coal and flour. Not one child begged. Reaching into his flight jacket, Halvorsen found just two sticks of Wrigley’s gum. He snapped them into four tiny strips and passed them through the wire. Children who missed out simply sniffed the silver wrappers in silence.

Moved to the core, the young pilot made a bold promise. Tomorrow, as his four-engined plane roared low over the rooftops to land, he would rock his wings left and right so they knew it was him. Then he would drop chocolate bars tied to pocket handkerchiefs! Back in barracks, his crew knotted string to cloth squares. Soon sacks of letters addressed to 'Onkel Wackelflügel'—Uncle Wiggly Wings—flooded the base, and aircrews floated twenty-three tons of sweets over Berlin.

Down below the flight path on 9 September 1948, Mayor Ernst Reuter stood before 300,000 freezing Berliners beside the bombed Reichstag. His voice echoed across the ruins. "Peoples of the world, look upon this city, and recognise that you must not and cannot abandon this city and this people!" In May 1949, Stalin admitted defeat and reopened the roads without a single shot being fired.

"One Man Said No": Vasili Arkhipov Inside Soviet Submarine B-59 (27 October 1962)

The History Revision Hub (True Historical Story • Declassified B-59 Logs & ExComm Records)
Historical Context: In October 1962, after American U-2 spy planes photographed Soviet nuclear missile sites in Fidel Castro’s Cuba just ninety miles off Florida, President John F. Kennedy threw a naval 'quarantine' around the island. For decades, even Kennedy’s advisers did not know how close humanity actually came to nuclear war beneath the waves on 'Black Saturday,' 27 October 1962.

Vasili Arkhipov[vah-SEE-lee ar-KHEE-pof]

Nikita Khrushchev[nih-KEE-tuh khrush-CHOV]

Brinkmanship & Quarantine[BRINK-mun-ship]
Pushing a crisis to the edge of nuclear war while using a careful naval 'quarantine' (rather than an act-of-war 'blockade') to leave room for diplomacy.

The terrifying nuclear balance in which a nuclear strike by either superpower guaranteed the complete destruction of both nations.

Deep beneath the Atlantic waves near Cuba on Saturday 27 October 1962, the air inside Soviet submarine B-59 was turning foul. Up in Washington and Moscow, Kennedy and Khrushchev were trading urgent letters over Soviet missile sites ninety miles off Florida. Down inside the dark steel boat, however, radio contact with Moscow had been dead for days.

Overhead, eleven American warships hunted the hidden submarine. To signal the Russian crew to come up, US ships dropped small practice depth charges. Inside B-59, each blast sounded like a giant hammer smashing the hull. The cooling fans had failed, cabin heat climbed past fifty degrees, batteries were dying, and sailors fainted on the deck plates.

Unknown to the Americans above, B-59 carried a ten-kiloton nuclear torpedo—nearly as strong as the Hiroshima bomb. Exhausted and sure that World War III had started overhead, Captain Valentin Savitsky lost his temper. "We are going to blast them now! We will die, but we will sink them all—we will not shame our Navy!" The political officer agreed.

On almost any other boat, the Captain’s order was enough. Yet thirty-six-year-old Second Captain Vasili Arkhipov happened to be aboard, so all three senior officers had to agree together. Staying calm in the stifling heat, Arkhipov gripped Savitsky’s arm. Those blasts were warning signals, he argued, not direct hits—and he firmly refused permission to fire.

B-59 rose safely into bright sunlight. That night, Kennedy promised never to invade Cuba while secretly agreeing to pull US missiles out of Turkey. Soon after, both shaken rivals wired a direct Moscow–Washington Hotline.

President Dwight D. Eisenhower (7 April 1954) & Dr Đặng Thùy Trâm (Quảng Ngãi Diary, 1969)

In a calm White House press room on 7 April 1954, President Dwight D. Eisenhower used a game-table image to explain why Washington feared losing Indochina. "You have a row of dominoes set up. You knock over the first one, and what will happen to the last one is the certainty that it will go over very quickly." If Vietnam turned communist, Eisenhower warned, Burma, Thailand, Malaya, and Indonesia would topple next. The West would lose tin, tungsten, rubber, and millions of people.

That tidy metaphor pulled half a million American troops, B-52 bombers, and napalm into South Vietnam, while Moscow and Beijing shipped rifles south. Yet on the ground, Vietnamese villagers were not wooden dominoes. Most fought for independence after generations of French colonial rule.

Inside a bamboo clinic in Quảng Ngãi province in June 1969, twenty-seven-year-old Vietnamese surgeon Dr Đặng Thùy Trâm wrote by oil lamp as bombs shook the earth. "Outside, jet planes scream overhead and napalm turns the green hillside into black ash. Today I held the hand of a twenty-year-old boy dying on our wooden table while his mother wept. Why must a wealthy nation cross an ocean to burn our small villages? We want neither foreign masters nor endless war—only peace under one sky."

Despite immense firepower, Saigon fell in April 1975. Four years later, Moscow marched into its own ten-year proxy quagmire in Afghanistan.

At 6:53 p.m. on Thursday 9 November 1989, East German spokesman Günter Schabowski sat sweating under hot television lights in East Berlin. For nearly an hour he had droned through dull party announcements. He had missed an earlier meeting and carried a folded draft note about travel visas that he had barely skimmed.

Near the end, Italian reporter Riccardo Ehrmann asked about new travel rules. Reading aloud from the unfamiliar sheet, Schabowski announced that East German citizens could apply to cross every border into the West. Journalists leaned forward in disbelief. "When does that take effect?" shouted a reporter. Schabowski pushed up his glasses, shuffled his papers in confusion, missed the next morning’s embargo date, and stammered live into millions of living rooms. "According to my information... immediately, without delay!"

Within minutes, East Berliners pulled coats over their pyjamas and walked through freezing mist toward the concrete Wall. At the Bornholmer Straße bridge, twenty thousand citizens pressed against the iron gate chanting, "Open the gate!" Inside the guard post, Lieutenant-Colonel Harald Jäger frantically telephoned his Stasi bosses for orders. Terrified of taking blame—and knowing Mikhail Gorbachev in Moscow had ordered 380,000 Soviet troops to stay locked in their barracks—his superiors barked insults and hung up.

Left completely alone with armed guards facing twenty thousand peaceful neighbours, Jäger refused to shed blood. At 11:30 p.m. he drew a breath and ordered, "Open the barrier!" Strangers wept, hugged, and chipped concrete with hammers. Two years later, on Christmas Night 1991, Gorbachev resigned peacefully and the Soviet flag came down over the Kremlin.

Enquiry 7 Historical Perspective Competing Historical Interpretations

Was the Cold War Ever Truly 'Cold'? Gaddis’s "Long Peace" vs Westad’s "Global Cold War"

John Lewis Gaddis ("The Long Peace", 1986) vs Odd Arne Westad ("The Global Cold War", 2005)
Historical Context: In your final Year 9 synoptic enquiry—'Was the Cold War ever truly cold?'—leading historians reach strikingly different verdicts depending on where on the world map they stand. Reading American historian John Lewis Gaddis alongside Norwegian global historian Odd Arne Westad shows how two scholars can use accurate evidence to build rival interpretations.

The 'Long Peace' (John Lewis Gaddis)[GAD-iss]
Gaddis’s argument that nuclear weapons and rival alliances prevented a third direct great-power war in Europe between 1945 and 1991.
Global Cold War (Odd Arne Westad)[ODD AR-neh VES-tahd]
Westad’s counter-argument that superpower meddling turned Asia, Africa, and Latin America into scorching battlefields killing up to twenty million people.
Eurocentrism[yoor-oh-SEN-triz-um]
Judging world history mainly through European or North American eyes while overlooking lived reality in Asia, Africa, and Latin America.
Sputnik & Space Race (1957–1969)[SPOOT-nik]
Superpower contest from the USSR’s Sputnik 1 satellite (1957) to America’s Apollo 11 Moon landing (1969), driven by the same rocket technology as nuclear missiles.

The Traditional View is called 'The Long Peace,' argued by historian John Lewis Gaddis. Look first at Europe in the early twentieth century. Twice inside thirty-one years, great powers tore the continent apart. Twenty million people died in the First World War, and over fifty million died worldwide in the Second. Yet between 1945 and 1991, something remarkable happened. Washington and Moscow built thousands of nuclear warheads, raced rockets from Sputnik to the Moon, and glared across Berlin and Cuba. Even so, American and Soviet armies never fought a single direct battle against each other. Not one nuclear bomb was dropped in anger after 1945. For Gaddis, fear of mutual destruction kept the superpower struggle 'cold' and gave Europe forty-six years of great-power peace.

The Modern View is 'The Global Cold War,' argued by historian Odd Arne Westad. Now step away from Berlin and stand in a Korean village, a Vietnamese rice field, or a valley near Kabul. Westad argues that calling 1945 to 1991 a 'Long Peace' is a narrow European blind spot. Direct war in Europe meant nuclear suicide. Instead, Washington and Moscow pushed their rivalry into newly independent nations across Asia, Africa, and Latin America. Across Korea, Vietnam, Cambodia, Angola, Chile, and Afghanistan, between fourteen and twenty million people died. Outside Europe, the Cold War was never cold for a single day.

Parent, Carer & Tutor Conversation Guide · Part 1 (Enquiries 1–2 Talking Points)
Enquiry 1: Kennan & Fulton (1946) p. 3
Q1: Kennan warned that a deep traditional Russian sense of insecurity had fused with communist ideology, yet Stalin was not reckless like Hitler: whenever Moscow met firm, calm resistance, it backed down.
Q2: Churchill warned that an 'iron curtain' had descended across Europe from Stettin to Trieste covering capitals like Warsaw, Berlin, and Prague; Novikov replied that untouched America, holding the atomic bomb, was ringing Russia with airbases.
Q3: Each side views its own steps as purely defensive while seeing the rival’s actions—from Soviet control over Eastern Europe to American airbases and the atomic bomb—as proof of hostile danger.
Enquiry 2: Truman’s Potsdam Diary p. 4
Q1: They were clashing over German reparations and Poland—where Stalin’s secret police had already set up a pro-Moscow regime despite promising free elections at Yalta—and on 18 July a coded cable announced that a thirteen-pound explosive had turned a steel tower into gas in a 40,000-foot fireball.
Q2: Soviet spy Klaus Fuchs inside Los Alamos had already briefed Moscow; hiding his alarm behind stone-faced calm, Stalin left the palace and ordered secret-police chief Lavrentiy Beria to build a Soviet atomic bomb at top speed.
Q3: Secrecy and spying replaced wartime cooperation with deep mistrust, turning a shared victory into a dangerous race to build ever more terrible weapons.

Pages 10–11 Key
Enquiry 3: Uncle Wiggly Wings p. 5
Q1: Joseph Stalin had locked every road, railway, and canal around West Berlin to force citizens to kneel, so British RAF Dakotas and American C-54 Skymasters landed at Tempelhof Airport every ninety seconds with coal and flour.
Q2: Not one child begged, and those who missed out on four tiny strips of gum simply sniffed the silver wrappers in silence; Halvorsen promised to rock his plane’s wings as he flew low and drop chocolate bars tied to pocket handkerchiefs.
Q3: Alongside keeping two million people fed and warm, Halvorsen’s candy parachutes and Mayor Ernst Reuter’s speech showed West Berliners they would not be abandoned—winning hearts and defeating the blockade peacefully.
Enquiry 4: Sub B-59 & Arkhipov p. 6
Q1: Radio contact with Moscow had been dead for days, eleven US warships were dropping practice depth charges that hammered the hull, cooling fans had failed in 50-degree heat, batteries were dying, and sailors were fainting on the deck plates.
Q2: Because Arkhipov happened to be aboard as Second Captain, all three senior officers had to agree together; staying calm in the stifling heat, he gripped Savitsky’s arm, argued the blasts were warning signals rather than direct hits, and firmly refused permission.
Q3: It shows how easily exhaustion and cut-off communication can push leaders toward catastrophe—and how one calm individual willing to pause and question panic can save millions of lives.
Enquiry 5: Dominoes & Proxy Voices p. 7
Q1: Eisenhower warned that knocking over the first domino would quickly topple the rest—fearing Burma, Thailand, Malaya, and Indonesia would fall next, costing the West tin, tungsten, rubber, and millions of people.
Q2: Writing by oil lamp as napalm turned green hillsides into black ash and a twenty-year-old boy died on her table, she shows villagers were not wooden dominoes—they wanted independence after generations of French colonial rule, 'neither foreign masters nor endless war—only peace under one sky.'
Q3: Distant leaders treat countries like pieces on a game table, whereas local people are fighting for their own villages and independence—something immense firepower alone cannot easily break.
Enquiry 6: Night the Wall Opened p. 8
Q1: Having missed an earlier meeting and barely skimmed a folded visa note, Schabowski overlooked the next morning’s embargo date—so his live-TV broadcast sent East Berliners pulling coats over their pyjamas and walking straight to the crossings.
Q2: Twenty thousand peaceful citizens were chanting at the iron gate, his panicked Stasi bosses barked insults and hung up, and he knew Gorbachev had ordered 380,000 Soviet troops to stay locked in their barracks; left alone, Jäger refused to shed blood and opened the barrier.
Q3: Every piece mattered—ordinary Berliners showed up peacefully in huge numbers, while Gorbachev’s refusal to send Soviet troops and Jäger’s refusal to shed blood ensured the gate opened with tears, hugs, and hammers instead of violence.
Enquiry 7: Gaddis vs Westad Debate p. 9
Q1: Gaddis notes that after two world wars killed over 70 million people, US and Soviet armies never fought directly and no nuclear bomb was dropped in anger after 1945; Westad counters that superpower rivalry killed between 14 and 20 million people across Asia, Africa, and Latin America.
Q2: It depends on where on the world map you stand: in Europe, fear of nuclear suicide kept American and Soviet armies from fighting directly, whereas in Korea, Vietnam, Cambodia, Angola, Chile, and Afghanistan, the struggle was deadly and hot.
Q3: Calling it 'cold' fits the direct nuclear standoff between Washington, Moscow, and Europe, yet hides the 14 to 20 million lives lost when both superpowers pushed their rivalry into newly independent nations across Asia, Africa, and Latin America.
