Edexcel GCSE: Superpower Relations and the Cold War, 1941–91

Paper 2: Superpower relations and the Cold War, 1941–91 • 32 Marks Total • Option P4 (1HI0/P4) • Official Specification Mock
Key Topic 1: Early Cold War (Truman Doctrine 1947)
Explain one consequence of the Truman Doctrine (1947). [4 Marks]
Target: AO1 (demonstrate knowledge and understanding) & AO2 (explain cause and consequence).
Level 2 (3–4 Marks): Specific factual explanation demonstrating a direct, supported consequence using precise causation.
Level 1 (1–2 Marks): Simple or general statement of a consequence with limited contextual development.
Exemplar Model Answer (Level 2 — 4 Marks):
One major consequence of the Truman Doctrine was that the United States formally abandoned its traditional peacetime isolationism and committed itself to the active global policy of containment. In his speech to Congress in March 1947, President Harry Truman declared that the world was divided between freedom and communist totalitarianism, pledging $400 million in economic and military aid to Greece and Turkey to prevent communist uprisings. Consequently, this established containment as the foundation of American foreign policy for the next four decades, directly paving the way for the massive Marshall Plan in 1948 and NATO in 1949, and convincing Stalin that the USA was determined to encircle and economically undermine the Soviet sphere.
Key Topic 2: Cold War Crises (Berlin Wall 1961)
Explain one consequence of the construction of the Berlin Wall (1961). [4 Marks]
Target: AO1 & AO2. Mark out of 4 using standard Edexcel 2-level tariff.
Level 2 (3–4 Marks): Specific factual explanation demonstrating a direct, supported consequence.
Level 1 (1–2 Marks): Simple or general statement of a consequence.
Exemplar Model Answer (Level 2 — 4 Marks):
One significant consequence of the construction of the Berlin Wall in August 1961 was that it permanently stopped the 'brain drain' of skilled East German workers escaping to the West, stabilising the East German economy at the cost of deep humanitarian division. Between 1949 and 1961, over 2.7 million highly educated East Germans had fled through the open border of West Berlin, creating a crippling crisis for Walter Ulbricht's communist regime. By sealing the border with concrete barriers, barbed wire, and armed guard towers, Khrushchev stabilized the East German state and ended Soviet threats to sign a separate peace treaty, but the Wall became a potent Western propaganda symbol exposing communism as a tyrannical system that had to imprison its own citizens to survive.
Key Topic 1: Early Cold War (Berlin Crisis & Airlift 1948–49)
Write a narrative account analysing the key events of the Berlin Crisis, 1948–49. [8 Marks]
Target: AO1 (4 marks) & AO2 (4 marks).
Level 3 (6–8 Marks): A sophisticated narrative account that shows clear chronological organisation and explains the causal links between beginning, middle developments, and outcome. Integrates own knowledge beyond the stimulus points.
LevelMarksDescriptor
Level 36–8Accurate, detailed chronological account analysing the process of change. Causal links between stages are clearly explained. Comprehensive own knowledge included.
Level 23–5Accurate narrative with some chronological sequence and causal explanation. Relies primarily on stimulus with some supporting detail.
Level 11–2Simple descriptive statements without clear narrative flow or causal connections.
Exemplar Model Answer (Level 3 — 8 Marks):
In early 1948, the Western Allies (the USA, Britain, and France) decided to unify their occupation zones into 'Trizonia' and introduced a new, stable currency—the Deutschmark—to stimulate German economic recovery. This infuriated Joseph Stalin, who feared that a prosperous, united West Germany would threaten Soviet security, and viewed Western economic measures in Berlin as an illegal imperialist enclave 100 miles deep inside the Soviet occupation zone.

On 24 June 1948, Stalin retaliated by imposing the Berlin Blockade, severing all road, rail, and canal routes linking West Berlin to the Western zones in an effort to starve the 2.5 million inhabitants of West Berlin into submission. Instead of using military force to open the roads—which risked starting the Third World War—US President Truman and British Foreign Secretary Ernest Bevin launched Operation Vittles (the Berlin Airlift). Over the next eleven months, Allied pilots flew over 277,000 flights through narrow air corridors, delivering more than 2.3 million tons of food, medical supplies, and coal, keeping the city alive under the leadership of Mayor Ernst Reuter.

Recognizing that the Western powers would not abandon West Berlin and that the airlift had become an extraordinary moral and logistical triumph for the West, Stalin was forced to call off the blockade on 12 May 1949. This crisis had decisive long-term consequences: within days, the Western Allies officially established the Federal Republic of Germany (West Germany), Stalin retaliated by creating the German Democratic Republic (East Germany), and the Western nations formed NATO in April 1949, permanently dividing Europe into two armed, hostile blocs.

Question 3: Explain the Importance of Two of the Following: [16 Marks Total]
Target: AO1 & AO2 (16 marks total • 8 marks per chosen option). Candidates must answer exactly TWO options.

Option A: The importance of the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962) for relations between the superpowers. [8 Marks]

Exemplar Model Answer (Level 3 — 8 Marks):
The Cuban Missile Crisis was critically important for superpower relations because it brought the USA and USSR to the terrifying threshold of nuclear war, prompting both Kennedy and Khrushchev to institute institutional mechanisms to reduce future brinkmanship.

Firstly, the acute risk of miscalculation during the thirteen-day crisis—such as the shooting down of a US U-2 spy plane—convinced both leaders that direct communication was essential. In June 1963, they installed the Moscow-Washington 'Hotline' (a direct teletype communication link between the Kremlin and the White House) to ensure immediate diplomatic contact during future crises, preventing delays in telegram transmission from causing accidental nuclear conflict.

Secondly, the crisis fostered the beginning of formal arms limitation agreements. Recognizing the catastrophic danger of unrestricted atmospheric nuclear tests, the superpowers signed the Partial Test Ban Treaty in August 1963, banning nuclear tests in the atmosphere, underwater, and in outer space. Although the arms race continued, the crisis initiated the first thaw in the Cold War and demonstrated that both sides prioritized mutual survival over total victory.

Option B: The importance of the Helsinki Accords (1975) for international security. [8 Marks]

Exemplar Model Answer (Level 3 — 8 Marks):
The Helsinki Accords of August 1975 were of paramount importance for international security because they represented the high-water mark of détente and established an international diplomatic framework governing borders and human rights.

Firstly, under Basket 1 (Security in Europe), the 35 signatory nations formally accepted the post-1945 frontiers of Europe as inviolable, officially recognizing the division of Germany and Soviet hegemony in Eastern Europe. This achieved a core Soviet foreign policy goal since 1945—gaining Western recognition of its satellite empire—while reducing the likelihood of armed conflict over territorial disputes in Central Europe.

Secondly, under Basket 3 (Human Rights), the signatories pledged to respect freedom of thought, conscience, religion, and human rights. While the Kremlin viewed this as mere diplomatic rhetoric, Western governments and dissidents inside the Soviet bloc (such as Charter 77 in Czechoslovakia and the Moscow Helsinki Watch Group) seized upon Basket 3 to hold communist regimes accountable to international standards. This legitimized domestic resistance and ultimately eroded the ideological legitimacy of Soviet totalitarianism from within.

Option C: The importance of the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (1979) for détente. [8 Marks]

Exemplar Model Answer (Level 3 — 8 Marks):
The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979 was decisively important for détente because it completely destroyed the decade-long process of superpower rapprochement and initiated the aggressive 'Second Cold War'.

Firstly, President Jimmy Carter viewed the invasion of a non-aligned state near the Persian Gulf as the greatest threat to world peace since 1945. In response, Carter issued the Carter Doctrine (January 1980), declaring that any attempt by outside forces to gain control of the Persian Gulf would be repelled by US military force. He also suspended the ratification of the SALT 2 treaty, cut off grain sales and high-technology exports to the USSR, and led a 65-nation boycott of the 1980 Moscow Olympic Games.

Secondly, the invasion provoked covert US military retaliation. The CIA launched Operation Cyclone, channeling billions of dollars in military hardware (including Stinger anti-aircraft missiles) to the Afghan mujahideen to bleed Soviet forces. This total breakdown in diplomatic trust paved the way for the election of Ronald Reagan in 1980, who decisively abandoned détente in favor of confrontational rearmament and the Strategic Defense Initiative.
