WALK-IN LESSON PLANS • ENQUIRIES 1 & 2 NON-SPECIALIST PRIMARY TEACHER GUIDE
Enquiry 1: Young Queen Victoria, Steam Railways and Shrinking Britain (1837–1860) TB p.4 • WB pp.3–4
Key Enquiry: How did steam railways and penny post shrink Queen Victoria’s Britain?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This opening Key Stage 2 enquiry introduces Queen Victoria’s accession aged eighteen in 1837, her marriage to Prince Albert, and the transport and communication revolution of the 1840s. Pupils explore Isambard Kingdom Brunel’s Great Western Railway bridges, Box Tunnel, and iron steamships, discover why fast trains forced every British town onto shared Greenwich "Railway Time", and examine Rowland Hill’s 1840 Penny Black stamp and red V.R. pillar postboxes.
Learning Objectives:
  • Describe how eighteen-year-old Queen Victoria came to the throne in 1837 and championed steam travel in 1842.
  • Explain how Isambard Kingdom Brunel and "Railway Mania" connected Britain’s towns and ships.
  • Show how standard "Railway Time" and the 1840 Penny Black united families across the country.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Between 1837 and 1860, steam railways and the Penny Post made Queen Victoria’s Britain feel much smaller because journeys and letters that once took days now took hours! After Victoria praised her 1842 ride into Paddington, navvies and Brunel linked towns with 6,000 miles of track under one shared Railway Time, while 1840 Penny Black mail trains let rich and poor families stay close for just one penny.”
Enquiry 2: Chimneys, Cotton Mills and Children Underground (1837–1850) TB p.5 • WB pp.5–6
Key Enquiry: Why was factory and coal-mine work so dangerous for Victorian children?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This second enquiry investigates Britain’s rapid industrial urbanisation (reaching an urban majority in the 1851 Census) and the lived reality of working-class children in textile mills and coal mines before effective reform. Pupils explore why rural families moved to Manchester ("Cottonopolis"), Yorkshire, and South Wales, examine the jobs of cotton-mill "piecers" and "scavengers", and analyse 1842 Royal Commission evidence on underground "trappers", "hurriers", and Humphry Davy’s safety lamp.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain why country families moved to smoky Victorian factory towns and why parents needed children’s wages.
  • Describe the daily dangers faced by child "piecers" and "scavengers" inside steam-powered cotton mills.
  • Use 1842 Royal Commission evidence to explain the jobs of "trappers" and "hurriers" in coal mines.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Between 1837 and 1850, factory and coal-mine work put Victorian children in daily danger because twelve-hour shifts left exhausted children working beside unguarded machines and dark rock seams. Poor families in booming Cottonopolis needed every penny, so mill scavengers crawled beneath clattering power looms breathing thick cotton dust, while pit trappers and chained hurriers hauled heavy coal corves past roof falls and explosive firedamp gas!”

Enquiry 3: Workhouses, Ragged Schools and Champions of Reform (1834–1880) TB p.6 • WB pp.7–8
Key Enquiry: How did Victorian reformers rescue children from workhouses, pits, and streets?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This third enquiry examines how Victorian society responded to poverty and child exploitation between 1834 and 1880. Pupils investigate why destitute families dreaded family separation, gruel, and oakum-picking inside 1834 Poor Law Union Workhouses (highlighted in Charles Dickens’s 1838 Oliver Twist), trace how the 7th Earl of Shaftesbury championed the 1842 Mines Act and Factory Acts backed by paid Factory Inspectors, and explore volunteer Ragged Schools alongside Dr Thomas Barnardo’s East End homes.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain why poor Victorian families dreaded entering a Union Workhouse after the 1834 Poor Law.
  • Show how Lord Shaftesbury, the 1842 Mines Act, and Factory Inspectors protected working children by law.
  • Describe how Ragged Schools and Dr Thomas Barnardo rescued homeless children on city streets.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Between 1834 and 1880, writers, politicians, and charities worked together to rescue Victorian children by changing both hearts and laws! Charles Dickens’s Oliver Twist shamed grim workhouses, Lord Shaftesbury’s 1842 Mines Act and paid Factory Inspectors banned young children from pits and capped mill shifts, and free Ragged Schools plus Dr Thomas Barnardo’s Homes gave destitute street children warm beds, meals, and trade skills.”
Enquiry 4: Inside a Victorian Classroom — Slates, Drill and the 1870 School Act (1870–1901) TB p.7 • WB pp.9–10
Key Enquiry: What was daily life really like inside a strict Victorian classroom?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This fourth enquiry compares late-Victorian elementary schooling (1870–1901) directly with pupils’ own classroom experience today. Pupils trace the three legal steps to universal primary schooling (Forster’s 1870 Education Act building Board Schools, Mundella’s 1880 compulsory attendance Act for ages 5–10, and 1891 abolishing weekly "school pence"), experience chanting the "Three Rs" with Welsh slates and steel copperplate dip pens, and weigh strict cane-and-Dunce’s-Cap discipline alongside separate boys’/girls’ lessons and playground iron hoops.
Learning Objectives:
  • Trace how the 1870, 1880, and 1891 Education Acts won free compulsory schooling for every British child.
  • Describe how Victorian pupils learned the "Three Rs" using Welsh slates, inkwells, and Object Lessons.
  • Compare strict Victorian discipline, separate gender lessons, and playground games with school today.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Stepping inside an 1885 Victorian Board School would feel very different from our classroom today because sixty pupils sat in silent bolted rows beneath high windows chanting the Three Rs! Instead of colourful whiteboards and kind help, left-handers were forced to write copperplate ink right-handed, mistakes risked the bamboo cane or shaming Dunce’s Cap, and boys and girls used separate doors for woodwork or needlework before bowling iron hoops outside.”

Enquiry 5: Workshop of the World — The 1851 Crystal Palace and Dazzling Inventions (1851–1901) TB p.8 • WB pp.11–12
Key Enquiry: Why did the 1851 Crystal Palace and new inventions amaze the world?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This fifth enquiry explores Britain at its peak as the "Workshop of the World" from the 1851 Great Exhibition to 1901. Pupils step inside Prince Albert and Joseph Paxton’s prefab Crystal Palace in Hyde Park (visited by six million people on shilling-day trains, funding South Kensington’s museums), investigate everyday breakthroughs (Cooke and Wheatstone’s electric telegraph, Alexander Graham Bell’s 1876 telephone, Sir Joseph Swan’s 1878 incandescent lightbulb at Cragside, flushing toilets, and photography), and celebrate two remarkable Victorian women: computing pioneer Ada Lovelace and Jamaican-Scottish nurse Mary Seacole.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain why Prince Albert’s 1851 Great Exhibition in the Crystal Palace amazed six million visitors.
  • Show how electric telegraphs, Bell’s telephone, and Joseph Swan’s lightbulb changed daily life.
  • Evaluate the achievements of mathematician Ada Lovelace (1843) and nurse Mary Seacole (1855).
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Between 1851 and 1901, Victorian Britain amazed the world with dazzling inventions that still shape our lives because engineers turned steam and electricity into everyday marvels! Showcased to six million visitors at the 1851 Crystal Palace, Victorian ingenuity flashed instant news by electric telegraph and Bell’s 1876 telephone, lit Cragside with Joseph Swan’s 1878 lightbulb, cleaned homes with flushing toilets, and foresaw computers through Ada Lovelace’s 1843 algorithm.”
Enquiry 6: Rich and Poor Homes, Seaside Holidays and Victoria’s 63-Year Legacy (1850–1901) TB p.9 • WB pp.13–14
Key Enquiry: How much had daily life changed across Britain by January 1901?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This synoptic finale evaluates continuity, progress, and continuing inequality across Queen Victoria’s 63-year reign up to her death in January 1901. Pupils contrast wealthy suburban villas (velvet parlours, indoor flushing bathrooms, and downstairs servants) with cramped working-class "back-to-back" terraces (tin baths and shared courtyard taps), explore the birth of mass working-class leisure (1871 Bank Holidays Act, cheap excursion trains to Blackpool/Margate piers, bathing machines, Punch and Judy, 1871 FA Cup football, and 1885 safety bicycles replacing penny-farthings), and reach a balanced historian’s verdict on the 1897 Diamond Jubilee and 1901 end of an era.
Learning Objectives:
  • Contrast daily home life inside a wealthy Victorian villa with a working-class back-to-back terrace.
  • Explain how 1871 Bank Holidays, excursion trains, football, and safety bicycles created modern leisure.
  • Reach a balanced synoptic judgment on how Britain changed across Queen Victoria’s reign (1837–1901).
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Across Queen Victoria’s 63-year reign from 1837 to 1901, Britain transformed almost beyond recognition, although stark inequality still remained! Horse-drawn villages and eight-year-old pit trappers gave way to steam railways, Penny Post, Joseph Swan’s electric lightbulb, the 1842 Mines Act, free compulsory Board Schools, and joyful 1871 Bank Holiday seaside trips—yet millions of poor families still lived in crowded back-to-back houses.”
