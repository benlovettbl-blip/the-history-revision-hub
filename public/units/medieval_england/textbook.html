From the bloody shield-wall on Senlac Hill in 1066 to the burning manor houses of the 1381 Peasants’ Revolt, medieval England was forged through fierce struggles over royal power, religious authority, and human freedom. Follow how Norman castles and the Domesday survey conquered a kingdom, why Thomas Becket’s murder shook Christendom, how rebellious barons forced King John to seal Magna Carta, and how the Black Death shattered feudal serfdom.

KEY ENQUIRY “Why did William of Normandy win the Battle of Hastings in 1066?”
STAGE 1 The Succession Void January 1066

On 5 January 1066, King Edward the Confessor died after reigning for twenty-four years. Edward left England in an existential crisis: he had died childless, leaving no direct heir to the throne. In Anglo-Saxon England, royal blood did not guarantee succession; the royal council, known as the Witan, held the constitutional right to elect the next monarch. Within twenty-four hours of Edward’s burial in Westminster Abbey, the Witan elected England’s wealthiest nobleman, Harold Godwinson, Earl of Wessex. Harold was crowned King Harold II, but his coronation ignited an international dynastic bloodbath.

STAGE 2 The Three Claimants 1066

Harold’s crown was immediately challenged by two formidable foreign rulers. In Normandy, Duke William claimed that Edward the Confessor had promised him the English throne in 1051, and that Harold Godwinson had sworn a sacred oath on holy relics in 1064 supporting William’s claim. William secured the blessing of Pope Alexander II, carrying a papal banner into battle. Simultaneously in Scandinavia, the fearsome Viking king Harald Hardrada of Norway staked a claim based on an earlier agreement between his ancestors and former Danish rulers of England. Surrounded by enemies, King Harold mobilized England’s armies for an unavoidable two-front invasion.

STAGE 3 The Forced March & Stamford Bridge September 1066

The first blow fell in the north. In September 1066, Harald Hardrada landed 300 Viking longships along the Yorkshire coast. King Harold performed a superhuman military feat: he marched his army 190 miles north from London in just four days. On 25 September, Harold surprised the Vikings at Stamford Bridge. In a savage battle, Hardrada was killed with an arrow through the throat, and the Viking army was so utterly annihilated that only 24 longships were needed to carry the survivors home. But Harold’s triumph was fatally brief. Two days later, Duke William’s invasion fleet landed unopposed at Pevensey Bay on the southern coast.

STAGE 4 The Shield Wall at Senlac Hill 14 October 1066

Rather than resting his exhausted troops in London, Harold immediately force-marched his men 250 miles back south. On the morning of 14 October 1066, the armies collided on Senlac Hill near Hastings. Harold deployed his men in a dense, interlocking shield wall across the ridge, with elite two-handed axemen (housecarls) in front and peasant militia (the fyrd) behind. For five gruelling hours, the Saxon shield wall held firm. Norman cavalry charged uphill into a blizzard of javelins and axes, while Norman archers shot blind into the shield wall without breaking it.

STAGE 5 The Feigned Retreat & Anglo-Saxon Defeat October 1066

The turning point came in the late afternoon. Sensing panic on his left flank where Breton soldiers retreated, William turned disaster into tactical genius. He ordered repeated feigned retreats: Norman cavalry charged the hill, then pretended to flee in terror. Tempted by the prospect of victory, undisciplined Saxon fyrd militia broke rank and surged downhill in pursuit. Immediately, Norman horsemen wheeled around, surrounded the scattered Saxons on open ground, and butchered them. With the shield wall fractured, Norman archers angled arrows high into the air. Harold was killed—traditionally struck in the eye with an arrow—and the Anglo-Saxon army disintegrated.

KEY ENQUIRY “How did a minority Norman elite of fewer than 10,000 soldiers control two million hostile Anglo-Saxons?”
STAGE 1 The Motte & Bailey Blitz 1066–1068

When William was crowned on Christmas Day 1066, he faced an existential emergency: an invading elite of just ten thousand Normans had to subjugate and control a hostile conquered population of approximately two million Anglo-Saxons. William’s immediate military solution was an unprecedented blitz of Motte and Bailey castles across England. Built in just eight to fourteen days using forced Saxon labour, over 500 Castles Built Fast established immediate military control. A steep earth mound—the High Earth Mounds (motte)—stood up to ten metres high, topped with a wooden watchtower keep, while below lay the bailey protected by Quick Timber Walls and a deep defensive ditch.

STAGE 2 The Scorched-Earth Harrying of the North 1069–1070

Where castles failed to deter rebellion, William deployed merciless state terror. In the winter of 1069, northern English Rebels Attack York and slaughtered the Norman garrison with Danish allies. William marched north in fury, ordering the systematic destruction of Yorkshire and the northern shires. Norman cavalry conducted total war by Burning Every Crop, slaughtering livestock, salting agricultural soil, and destroying farming tools. Chronicler Orderic Vitalis recorded that over 100,000 Starve to Death in the ensuing famine, leaving vast areas entirely desolate and broken for over a generation.

STAGE 3 Stone Keeps & Portchester Castle 1070–1100

As Norman rule stabilized, temporary timber watchtowers were replaced with massive stone keeps to project permanent imperial dominance. Along the south coast, the Normans commandeered massive 3rd-century ancient Roman walls, constructing a formidable four-storey thick limestone keep inside the Roman fort enclosure.

The fortress guarded a deep warship harbour in Portsmouth Harbour, securing William’s cross-Channel supply lines to Normandy and deterring French invasion fleets. The towering stone walls projected terrifying royal authority over defeated Anglo-Saxons across southern England.

STAGE 4 The Great Domesday Book Audit 1085–1086

Physical conquest had to be cemented by administrative surveillance. At Midwinter 1085, Fearing Viking Invasion by King Cnut IV of Denmark, William gathered his council at Gloucester and commissioned an exhaustive nationwide economic survey. Royal commissioners were sent across every English shire, Counting Every Cow and Pig, meadow, woodland, and mill, demanding oaths under threat of severe penalty. Completed in 1086, the Domesday Book ensured the Crown extracted maximum fiscal revenue, guaranteeing every landholder was Paying Every Penny in Tax.

STAGE 5 Feudal Hierarchy & Land Dispossession 1086

The Domesday audit laid bare the most complete redistribution of land and power in English history. Local surveys such as the Hundred of Fareham Survey demonstrate how Anglo-Saxon thegns were systematically dispossessed. Virtually all English Landlords Kicked Out were replaced by approximately two hundred Norman tenants-in-chief as Barons Take the Manors. This created a rigid feudal pyramid: William held all land by divine right, leasing manors to Norman barons in exchange for military knights, binding the kingdom in a tight web of feudal vassalage.

KEY ENQUIRY “Why did the conflict between King Henry II and Archbishop Thomas Becket erupt into murder in Canterbury Cathedral?”
STAGE 1 Worldly Chancellor to Archbishop 1154–1162

In 1154, the energetic young King Henry II appointed his close drinking companion and brilliant courtier Thomas Becket as Chancellor of England. Becket lived as the King’s Hunting Friend, reveling in luxury with Silk Robes and Feasting, and executing royal policy ruthlessly. When Archbishop Theobald died in 1161, Henry devised an audacious strategy: by appointing Becket as Archbishop of Canterbury, he believed he had secured an unquestioning royal pawn who would fuse church and crown authority, Hoping for an Easy Puppet.

STAGE 2 Becket’s Metamorphosis & Spiritual Allegiance 1162

The moment Becket was consecrated Archbishop in June 1162, he underwent an astonishing personal and spiritual transformation. Becket immediately resigned the Chancellorship, Throws Off Silk Robes of worldly power, and adopted a life of severe monastic asceticism, wearing a Scratchy Goat Hairshirt infested with lice beneath his vestments and washing the feet of thirteen paupers daily. Becket declared himself Loyal Only to the Pope and the universal Catholic Church, setting up an unavoidable collision course with the Crown.

STAGE 3 The Constitutions of Clarendon & ‘Criminous Clerks’ 1164

The flashpoint between King and Archbishop was the legal scandal of 'criminous clerks'. Anyone who could read a Latin psalm claimed 'benefit of clergy', meaning corrupt Priests Escaping Punishment for robbery, rape, and murder faced lenient church courts instead of royal gallows by simply Reading a Latin Verse. Henry summoned the barons and bishops to Clarendon in January 1164, issuing the Constitutions of Clarendon where the King Demands Royal Trials and punishment for defrocked priests. Becket furiously refused to seal the document.

STAGE 4 Murder in Canterbury Cathedral 1170

Following six years of bitter exile in France, Becket returned to Canterbury in December 1170 and immediately excommunicated bishops who supported Henry. When news reached Normandy at Christmas, the King Shouts in Fury: 'What miserable drones have I nourished that allow their lord to be treated with such contempt?' Hearing this, Four Knights Gallop Away across the icy Channel, bursting into Canterbury Cathedral at dusk on 29 December 1170. When Becket refused to yield, their Swords Smashed at the Altar, severing his skull and spraying his brains across the stones.

STAGE 5 Barefoot Royal Penance & Papal Compromise 1174

Becket’s brutal assassination horrified Christian Europe. Pope Alexander III canonised him as a martyr within three years, and pilgrims flooded Canterbury seeking miraculous cures after sensational reports of Miracles at the Tomb. Facing papal interdict, rebellions, and personal torment, Henry performed abject penance in July 1174: he walked barefoot through Canterbury’s streets and knelt before Becket’s shrine, suffering public Whipping with Birch Rods by eighty monks. In the eventual compromise, Church Courts Kept Intact their privileges, preserving clerical immunity for centuries.

KEY ENQUIRY “Was Magna Carta a revolutionary victory for human freedom or a selfish act of baronial extortion?”
STAGE 1 ‘Softsword’ & The Loss of Normandy 1199–1204

When King John inherited the Angevin Empire in 1199, he succeeded his heroic elder brother, Richard the Lionheart. But while Richard had spent England’s wealth on crusades, John was an administrative micromanager who lacked military genius. In 1204, King Philip II of France outmanoeuvred John and captured Normandy—the ancestral homeland of the English aristocracy. Mocked by his barons as "John Softsword," John spent the next decade obsessively raising astronomical sums of money to finance an army to win Normandy back, pushing royal feudal taxation to breaking point.

STAGE 2 The Papal Interdict & Extortion 1205–1213

John’s financial desperation led him into a catastrophic feud with Pope Innocent III over who should appoint the next Archbishop of Canterbury. When John refused to accept Stephen Langton, the Pope placed England under an Interdict in 1208. For five agonizing years, church doors were locked, church bells fell silent, babies could not be baptized in church, and the dead were buried in unhallowed ditches. John retaliated by confiscating all church lands and revenues, pocketing over £100,000. In 1209, the Pope excommunicated John. To avoid a French invasion, John capitulated in 1213: he surrendered England as a papal fiefdom, agreeing to pay the Pope an annual tribute of 1,000 marks.

STAGE 3 The Baronial Rebellion 1214–1215

John’s gamble collapsed in July 1214 at the Battle of Bouvines in Flanders. John’s expensive coalition of mercenaries was smashed by the French king, and John’s dream of reconquering Normandy was permanently extinguished. When John returned to England demanding yet another crushing scutage tax from barons who had refused to fight in France, the aristocracy revolted. Led by Robert Fitzwalter—who styled himself "Marshal of the Army of God"—a coalition of northern and eastern barons renounced their feudal homage, marched south, and captured the city of London in May 1215. Cornered and bankrupt, John was forced to negotiate.

STAGE 4 The Clauses of Runnymede 15 June 1215

On 15 June 1215, in the water-meadow of Runnymede beside the River Thames near Windsor, King John met the armed rebel barons. John affixed the Great Seal of England to a parchment document comprising 63 revolutionary clauses: Magna Carta (The Great Charter). The charter curtailed royal autocracy: it stopped the king from seizing baronial lands, protected widows from forced remarriages, standardized weights and measures, and prohibited arbitrary taxation without baronial consent. Crucially, Clause 39 established the foundational bedrock of English common law: "No free man shall be seized or imprisoned... except by the lawful judgement of his equals or by the law of the land."

STAGE 5 Civil War & The Myth of Liberty 1215–1225

Magna Carta brought immediate war rather than peace when Pope tears up the treaty at King John’s request. Furious barons launched a rebellion, with a French prince invited in as Prince Louis marched troops into London to claim the English crown.

John waged a scorched-earth campaign until disaster struck, famously leaving the crown jewels lost in the Wash tidal estuary. John’s sudden death in October 1216 allowed the legendary William Marshal to reissue Magna Carta, securing the crown for nine-year-old Henry III.

KEY ENQUIRY “How did the demographic shock of the Black Death lead to the peasant uprising of 1381?”
STAGE 1 Arrival of the Plague at Melcombe Regis 1348

In June 1348, the Black Death landed on English shores when a Sailor Ship at Weymouth (Melcombe Regis) carried plague-carrying fleas from Gascony. The pandemic spread with terrifying speed. Victims developed agonizing egg-sized black swelling Buboes Under the Arms and in the groin, spat dark blood, and suffered acute fever, perishing within three to five days. By 1350, with Nearly Half the Country Dead, entire villages lay deserted, churchyards overflowed into mass trenches, and medieval society stood shattered.

STAGE 2 Labour Shortage & The Economic Revolution 1349–1351

The catastrophic population collapse triggered an unprecedented economic revolution across the English countryside. Entire farming communities stood silent with empty village cottages, while overgrown fields were left abandoned with unharvested wheat rotting on the ground for lack of reapers.

For the first time in medieval history, surviving peasant labourers possessed immense economic bargaining power. Desperate landowners competed fiercely for workers, resulting in workers demanding double pay and boldly refusing unpaid feudal bondage, permanently shattering centuries of customary manorial control.

STAGE 3 The Statute of Labourers & Sumptuary Resistance 1351–1363

Outraged by rising wages and their loss of feudal control, the landowner-dominated Parliament struck back decisively. The 1351 Statute of Labourers decreed that wages frozen by lords must remain strictly at pre-plague levels, making wage negotiation a punishable crime.

Runaway serfs faced imprisonment and having an iron "F" burned on foreheads for falsity to brand them as fugitive outlaws. Furthermore, 1363 Sumptuary Laws ensured commoners were banned from wearing fur, silk, or fine dyed cloth, entrenching bitter social resentment that exploded in rebellion thirty years later.

STAGE 4 The Regressive Poll Taxes & Brentwood Spark 1377–1381

Simmering tensions erupted when the royal government faced bankruptcy from the costly Hundred Years’ War, passing a crippling French War Debt Bill. Unlike property taxes, the third Poll Tax of 1381 demanded a flat Twelve Pence from Every Head aged over fifteen—equivalent to two weeks' hard wages for a labourer. Mass tax evasion followed, and when royal commissioner John Bampton arrived on 30 May 1381, he was a Tax Collector Chased with Pitchforks by furious Essex villagers at Brentwood, igniting armed rebellion.

STAGE 5 Showdown at Smithfield & The End of Serfdom 1381

Under leaders Wat Tyler and radical priest John Ball, over 30,000 Peasants Swarm the Capital, burning tax rolls and storming the Tower of London. On 15 June 1381, fourteen-year-old King Richard II met the rebels at Smithfield. Tensions flared and Tyler Stabbed at the Peace Talks by the Mayor of London. With bows drawn, the bold Boy King Promises Freedom, declaring himself their leader and pledging to abolish serfdom. Though the revolt was brutally crushed, feudal bondage was terminally undermined and withered away over the next century.
