How Did Printing and the Royal Society Challenge Old Medical Dogma?

Stage 1
c1440–1476

Gutenberg’s Movable-Type Press

Broke the Church Monopoly on Books

Johannes Gutenberg’s press (c1440) and Caxton’s 1476 London shop used movable metal type to mass-print exact books—ending monastic control over medical texts.

Humanism
Stage 2
c1500

Humanism Questions Ancient Texts

Fresh Greek Translations Exposed Errors

Renaissance Humanists read original Greek medical manuscripts, spotting medieval copying mistakes and urging doctors to test Galen’s claims with their own eyes.

Society
Stage 3
1660–1662

Royal Society: Nullius in Verba

Chartered by King Charles II in 1662

Founded in London in 1660 and chartered by Charles II in 1662, the Royal Society chose the motto Nullius in Verba—“Take nobody’s word for it.”

Peer Journals
Stage 4
March 1665

Philosophical Transactions

World’s First Scientific Journal (1665)

Secretary Henry Oldenburg launched Philosophical Transactions in 1665, speeding the transmission of ideas as European scientists shared and debated experiments in print.

Microscopes
Stage 5
1665–1676

Early Microscopes & Microbes

Hooke’s Micrographia (1665) Magnified Cells

Robert Hooke’s Micrographia (1665) showed magnified cells and fleas, while Leeuwenhoek spotted tiny microbes (“animalcules”)—though nobody yet linked them to disease.

New Science, Old Sickroom Cures

Communication & Method Leapt Ahead of Cures

Printing and the Royal Society shattered blind faith in Galen, yet without germ theory, everyday doctors still relied on humours and miasma at the bedside.

💡 The Big Story in One Sentence

Gutenberg’s press, Humanism, microscopes, and the Royal Society’s “Nullius in Verba” replaced blind faith in Galen with live experiments—even though bedside cures had not yet caught up.

1. Printing Press (c1440) Gutenberg’s press mass-producing exact books and breaking Church control.
2. Royal Society (1660) London science society chartered by Charles II to test ideas by experiment.
3. Philosophical Transactions First scientific journal (1665), sharing new medical experiments across Europe.
