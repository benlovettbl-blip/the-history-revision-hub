Icicles hang thick over the ward windows. I have to work while wearing an extra knitted sweater and my heavy overcoat. 1 (a) Describe one feature of how artillery and shrapnel caused severe injuries on the Western Front.(2 marks)
Examiner Model Answer (2/2 Marks)

Feature: High-explosive artillery shells and shrapnel balls were the greatest cause of battlefield trauma, inflicting roughly 58% of all British wounds on the Western Front.

Detail: Exploding shells hurled jagged steel fragments at high velocity that shattered limb bones into compound fractures and dragged mud-soaked khaki wool uniform and manure bacteria (Clostridium perfringens) deep into lacerated muscle, triggering lethal gas gangrene.

1 (b) Describe one feature of how blood transfusions were carried out on the Western Front.(2 marks)
Examiner Model Answer (2/2 Marks)

Feature: From 1915, medical officers replaced dangerous vein-to-vein transfusions with indirect syringe-cannula and citrated bottle transfusions to treat haemorrhagic shock.

Detail: Building on Richard Lewisohn’s 1915 sodium citrate anticoagulant and Rous and Turner’s 1916 citrate-glucose solution, Captain Oswald Robertson created the first pre-battle blood depot at Cambrai in November 1917, storing 22 units of universal Group O blood on ice for up to 26 days.

How useful are Sources A and B for an enquiry into the challenges of caring for wounded soldiers on the Western Front?

Explain your answer, using Sources A and B and your knowledge of the historical context.(8 marks)
Examiner Model Answer (8/8 Marks • Grade 9)

Source A is highly useful for an enquiry into the challenges of caring for casualties because its content vividly captures how mass offensives overwhelmed medical capacity at Casualty Clearing Stations (CCSs). Reverend Davies notes that on 1 July 1916—the opening day of the Battle of the Somme—1,500 wounded men poured into his CCS, packing huts, tents, and bare ground while another thousand arrived daily by 3 July, leaving many to die before surgeons could operate. This aligns closely with contextual knowledge: British planners expected roughly 10,000 daily Somme casualties, yet suffered 57,470 on Day 1 alone (including 19,240 killed), swamping CCS surgical teams designed to hold 200–1,000 patients and forcing strict three-tier triage. In terms of provenance, as a contemporaneous private diary written on the spot by an army chaplain tending the dying on 1–3 July 1916, Davies was an eyewitness with no propaganda motive to conceal operational breakdown, making Source A candid evidence of mass-casualty congestion—though as a non-surgeon he does not detail clinical obstacles inside the theatre such as gas gangrene or blood loss.

Source B is equally useful because it illuminates a completely different environmental challenge further down the Chain of Evacuation: bitter winter conditions in coastal Base Hospitals. Writing in December 1917, VAD nurse Edith Smith describes gruelling 3.30 a.m. ward shifts where icicles hung inside windows, pipes froze solid forcing strict water rationing, sponges and hot-water bottles froze, and spilled water turned instantly to sheet ice. Contextually, the winters of 1916–17 and late 1917 were among the coldest in Northern France in decades; Base Hospitals at Boulogne, Étaples, and Rouen often expanded into draughty canvas marquees and uninsulated huts where Volunteer Aid Detachment (VAD) nurses performed exhausting sanitation and nursing duties. Regarding provenance, a personal letter to her sister bypassed press optimism to give an honest picture of daily nursing hardship, though wartime field censorship prevented naming her exact location and her coastal perspective cannot show frontline stretcher-bearer or RAP perils.

Overall, both sources are highly useful in combination: Source A exposes the acute surgical bottleneck created by industrial-scale Somme casualties at forward CCS hubs, while Source B reveals how extreme weather and gruelling nursing conditions impeded recovery even at rear Base Hospitals.

Section B: Medicine in Britain, c1250–present (36 Marks)
3. Explain one way in which public and government responses to the Great Plague in London (1665) were similar to responses to the cholera epidemic in London (1854).(4 marks)
Examiner Model Answer (4/4 Marks • Grade 9)

One significant similarity was that during both epidemics, public authorities relied heavily on clearing street filth to combat perceived miasma ("bad air") because Germ Theory had not yet been discovered.

During the 1665 Great Plague in the Medical Renaissance, King Charles II and the Mayor of London’s Plague Orders commanded householders to sweep the streets outside their doors, remove dung-heaps, and burn barrels of pitch, tar, and sweet herbs in the belief that decaying waste released poisonous miasmatic vapours that corrupted the humours.

Similarly, during the 1854 Soho cholera outbreak in nineteenth-century Britain, the General Board of Health and most physicians stubbornly rejected John Snow’s proof that cholera spread via sewage-contaminated water from the Broad Street pump, insisting instead on whitewashing walls and flushing street gullies to disperse foul miasmatic smells.

4. Explain why understanding of the causes of disease improved rapidly in the period c1860–c1900. You may use the following in your answer: • Louis Pasteur’s Germ Theory (1861) • Robert Koch You must also use information of your own.(12 marks)
Stimulus points: Louis Pasteur's Germ Theory (1861) • Robert Koch
Examiner Model Answer (12/12 Marks • Grade 9)

Understanding of the causes of disease transformed between c1860 and c1900 because experimental microbiology demolished centuries of belief in miasma and spontaneous generation, proving conclusively that specific microscopic pathogens caused specific human illnesses.

First, Louis Pasteur’s publication of Germ Theory in 1861 provided the foundational scientific breakthrough by proving that airborne micro-organisms caused decay rather than being produced by rotting matter ("spontaneous generation"). Commissioned to investigate souring beet alcohol and wine in France, Pasteur used boiled nutrient broth inside heat-bent swan-neck glass flasks: broth open to dusty air rapidly turned cloudy with microbes, whereas flasks whose curved necks trapped airborne dust remained sterile for years until tilted. Although Pasteur initially studied food and drink fermentation rather than human patients, his controlled experiments demolished spontaneous generation and inspired British physicist John Tyndall (1870s) and surgeon Joseph Lister (1865) to argue that invisible airborne germs caused wound sepsis.

Secondly, German physician Robert Koch turned Pasteur’s general theory into the precise science of human bacteriology during the 1870s and 1880s. Whereas liquid broths made it almost impossible to separate teeming mixtures of microbes, Koch pioneered solid agar jelly culture plates grown in Petri dishes and stained transparent bacteria with chemical aniline dyes so individual species stood out vividly. Using these laboratory techniques, Koch isolated the exact bacillus causing anthrax (1876), tuberculosis (1882), and cholera (1883–84)—confirming John Snow’s 1854 waterborne discovery thirty years later. By establishing "Koch’s Postulates", he gave scientists across Europe a repeatable four-step method that enabled "microbe hunters" to identify the bacteria responsible for diphtheria, typhoid, pneumonia, plague, and tetanus before 1900.

Thirdly, rapid progress depended upon major advances in industrial microscope technology, photomicrography, and Franco-German government funding. Earlier seventeenth- and eighteenth-century lenses suffered severe blurring and colour distortion, preventing doctors from distinguishing bacteria consistently. Mid-nineteenth-century optical improvements by Joseph Jackson Lister and Carl Zeiss (coupled with Ernst Abbe’s oil-immersion lenses capable of 1,000x crisp magnification) and Koch’s use of microscopic photography allowed bacteriologists to publish indisputable visual proof of microbes in medical journals. At the same time, fierce national rivalry after the 1870–71 Franco-Prussian War prompted the French and German governments to pour state tax grants into rival research institutes for Pasteur in Paris and Koch in Berlin, accelerating discoveries far beyond what lone individuals could achieve.

In conclusion, understanding improved rapidly because Pasteur’s experimental breakthrough, Koch’s staining and solid-agar methodology, and high-powered optical technology backed by state funding worked together to prove specific germ causation beyond doubt.

Question 5 Examiner Model Answer (20/20 Marks • Grade 9)

To a substantial extent, the Theory of the Four Humours formed the dominant intellectual framework for educated medical diagnosis between c1250 and c1700, upheld by reverence for Galen and university teaching. However, arguing that it was the 'main' influence across all of society overstates academic theory: throughout both the Medieval and Renaissance eras, religious belief in divine punishment and environmental fear of miasma exercised an equally powerful—and during major epidemics, even stronger—hold over popular and official explanations of disease.

On the one hand, the Theory of the Four Humours (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile), originated by Hippocrates and systematised by Claudius Galen via the Theory of Opposites, dominated learned medicine for over four centuries. Throughout Medieval England (c1250–c1500), every university-trained physician studied Galen’s texts in Latin, using urine charts (uroscopy), pulse readings, and Zodiac calendars to diagnose internal humoral imbalance. Crucially, even when Renaissance anatomists Andreas Vesalius (1543) and William Harvey (1628) disproved Galen’s anatomical and circulatory errors, humoral pathology survived almost intact at the bedside until 1700—as seen in 1685 when royal physicians bled, blistered, and purged King Charles II to restore his humours—because doctors still lacked microscopes strong enough or Germ Theory to replace it.

On the other hand, Christian theology and supernatural explanations were arguably an even more pervasive influence on how the vast majority of ordinary people understood why sickness struck, particularly in c1250–c1500. Because the Medieval Catholic Church controlled universities, monastic libraries, and parish life—and endorsed Galen precisely because his belief in a single 'Creator' fitted Christian scripture—ordinary villagers and kings alike viewed disease above all as God’s punishment for sin, a test of faith, or demonic malice. During the 1348–49 Black Death, chroniclers, bishops, and flagellants overwhelmingly blamed God’s wrath against human pride, and even during the 1665 Great Plague in Protestant Renaissance London, bills of fasting, public prayer days, and 'Lord have mercy upon us' door crosses showed that divine providence remained central to explaining catastrophe, even if astrology and witchcraft declined slightly by 1700.

Furthermore, environmental miasma theory and emerging late-Renaissance clinical empiricism provided distinct alternative explanations that bridged elite and popular thought. Both medieval town councils (cleaning muck-heaps in 1348) and Renaissance authorities (lighting street coal fires in 1665 to purify poisoned air) attributed epidemic pestilence not to an individual’s internal dietary humours, but to external foul vapours rising from swamps, cesspits, and rotting corpses. Moreover, by the late seventeenth century, the 'English Hippocrates' Thomas Sydenham (Observationes Medicae, 1676) directly challenged humoral orthodoxy by arguing that diseases like scarlet fever and measles were distinct external species attacking the body depending on atmospheric conditions rather than personal humoral quirks, marking the beginning of the shift away from Galen.

In conclusion, while the Theory of the Four Humours remained the bedrock of formal physician training and individual bedside diagnosis across c1250–c1700, it never operated in isolation. It survived precisely because the Medieval Church sanctified Galen, while in moments of epidemic crisis—from 1348 to 1665—religious belief in God’s judgement and fear of miasmatic air were at least equally decisive in shaping how society explained illness.
