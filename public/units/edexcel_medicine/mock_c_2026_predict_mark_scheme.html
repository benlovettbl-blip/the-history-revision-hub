To prepare for the anticipated casualties before the Cambrai offensive, we established a forward blood depot at Casualty Clearing Station No. 48. Twenty-two units of universal Group O blood were collected in advance into sterilised glass bottles containing sodium citrate and dextrose (glucose) solution, packed in insulated wooden ammunition cases with ice and sawdust for up to twenty-six days. 1 (a) Describe one feature of the support work of the First Aid Nursing Yeomanry (FANY) on the Western Front.(2 marks)
Examiner Model Answer (2/2 Marks)

Feature: From January 1916, British women volunteers in the First Aid Nursing Yeomanry (FANY) drove and maintained motor ambulance convoys transporting wounded soldiers between railway sidings, canal barges, and coastal Base Hospitals at Calais and St Omer.

Detail: Trained in motor mechanics as well as first aid, FANY volunteers repaired heavy ambulance engines in freezing blackout conditions, freeing male RAMC orderlies for frontline stretcher service, and also operated mobile bath lorries, soup kitchens, and cinema vans for troops.

1 (b) Describe one feature of a Regimental Aid Post (RAP) on the Western Front.(2 marks)
Examiner Model Answer (2/2 Marks)

Feature: A Regimental Aid Post was the first medical post behind the firing line, typically located within 200 yards of the frontline trench inside a dugout, cellar, or ruined building.

Detail: Staffed by a single Regimental Medical Officer (RMO) and stretcher bearers with no surgical theatre, the RAP patched up lightly wounded men to return to duty while giving serious casualties tourniquets, field dressings, morphine, and anti-tetanus injections before evacuation to an Advanced Dressing Station.

Explain your answer, using Sources A and B and your knowledge of the historical context.(8 marks)
Examiner Model Answer (8/8 Marks • Grade 9)

Source A is exceptionally useful for an enquiry into the development of blood transfusion and frontline storage because it records the world’s first pre-battle blood bank in action at the Battle of Cambrai (November 1917). Robertson details collecting 22 units of universal Group O blood (building on Karl Landsteiner’s 1901 ABO discovery) preserved in sodium citrate and Rous-Turner dextrose (glucose) inside ice-packed ammunition boxes for up to 26 days, saving 11 of 20 shock-stricken soldiers otherwise doomed to die on the operating table. Contextually, before 1915 rapid clotting forced doctors to connect a live donor vein-to-vein or via Keynes/Robertson syringe-cannulas right beside the patient—impossible during chaotic CCS surges—whereas citrate-glucose refrigeration finally separated donor from recipient in both space and time. Regarding provenance, writing in the British Medical Journal (June 1918) as the pioneering US Army researcher attached to British CCS No. 48, Robertson provides authoritative clinical data intended to persuade RAMC surgical teams to adopt resuscitation blood depots across the Western Front in 1918, though his trial sample of 20 patients at one CCS cannot show how widely depots spread by the Armistice.

Source B is highly useful as corroborating material evidence of the practical field apparatus that made indirect frontline blood storage and transfusion possible in 1917–18. The photograph clearly displays a graduated glass bottle marked "500 C.C. BLOOD" and "150 C.C. CITRATE" connected to rubber tubing, rubber-bulb pressure pump, and needle cannula, housed beside a rugged compartmentalised wooden "TRANSFUSION SET" carrying case. This aligns with historical knowledge: Richard Lewisohn’s 1915 discovery of safe sodium citrate anticoagulation and Rous and Turner’s 1916 glucose preservative allowed standardised portable kits like Captain Robertson’s bottle apparatus to be carried directly into CCS resuscitation ("shock") wards. In terms of provenance, as an official wartime medical-equipment record photograph, it accurately documents exact apparatus design and measurements, though a static studio equipment photo cannot show the clinical perils of haemolysis if blood was mismatched or allowed to warm when ice ran short.

In conclusion, Sources A and B work together powerfully: Source B reveals the physical anticoagulant bottle and portable kit engineering, while Source A proves its life-saving clinical impact on shock casualties at Cambrai.

Section B: Medicine in Britain, c1250–present (36 Marks)
3. Explain one way in which methods used to prevent the spread of the Black Death (1348) were similar to methods used to prevent the spread of the Great Plague in London (1665).(4 marks)
Examiner Model Answer (4/4 Marks • Grade 9)

One clear similarity was that during both plagues, authorities and citizens relied on public religious penance alongside clearing street filth to ward off God’s wrath and foul miasmatic air.

During the 1348–49 Black Death in Medieval England, bishops ordered public fasting, barefoot prayer processions, and confession of sins to appease God, while King Edward III instructed London officials to clear human waste and butcher offal from streets and rivers to stop corrupt air spreading pestilence.

Similarly, during the 1665 Great Plague in Renaissance London, King Charles II proclaimed national days of public fasting and humiliation while the Mayor’s Plague Orders employed rakers to clear street muck and lit coal fires to purify miasma—showing strong continuity in spiritual and environmental prevention before Germ Theory.

4. Explain why there was rapid progress in public health and the prevention of disease in the modern period (c1900–present). You may use the following in your answer: • government lifestyle campaigns • mass vaccination programmes You must also use information of your own.(12 marks)
Stimulus points: Government lifestyle campaigns • Mass vaccination programmes
Examiner Model Answer (12/12 Marks • Grade 9)

A key factor driving rapid progress in modern disease prevention was the British government's complete abandonment of its traditional 'laissez-faire' policy in favour of active, state-funded compulsory vaccination campaigns. In the mid-twentieth century, the government recognised that it had an institutional duty to safeguard the physical health of its citizens, a shift that was heavily accelerated by the creation of the National Health Service (NHS) in 1948. This state-directed approach first succeeded on a national scale during the Second World War (1940–42) when the Ministry of Health launched the first free national immunisation campaign against diphtheria—fearing crowded air-raid shelters would spark epidemics—which slashed diphtheria deaths from roughly 3,000 a year (out of ~50,000–60,000 annual cases) to virtually zero within a generation. The success of this administrative model enabled the state to systematically introduce subsequent national programmes against polio (1956), tetanus (1961), measles (1968), and the MMR vaccine (1988), effectively eradicating highly infectious epidemics that had plagued Britain for centuries.

Concurrently, the state utilised its legislative power to prevent disease by launching national lifestyle campaigns and environmental regulations to combat chronic, non-communicable illnesses. Throughout the twentieth century, epidemiological research proved how personal choices regarding tobacco, diet, and exercise caused major killer diseases like lung cancer and heart disease. Consequently, the government abandoned optional, local public health measures and used its legal authority to force behavioural changes on a national scale. Following the catastrophic Great Smog of London in 1952, which killed thousands of vulnerable citizens, the government passed the landmark Clean Air Acts of 1956 and 1968 to legally ban coal smoke and regulate urban air quality. Furthermore, the state implemented heavy taxes on tobacco, mandated plain packaging, and passed the compulsory 2007 ban on smoking in enclosed public workplaces to prevent lung cancer, demonstrating a highly active and successful legislative approach to public health.

Furthermore, this rapid progress was accelerated by groundbreaking scientific discoveries in biochemistry and virology, which provides a distinct third factor beyond the stimulus points. Throughout the twentieth century, the development of electron microscopes and advanced cell cultures allowed scientists to study viral structures and manufacture highly stable vaccines in industrial laboratories. This led directly to the creation of the Salk polio vaccine in 1955 and the MMR vaccine in 1988, giving the government the exact clinical tools required to immunise millions of children. Today, this scientific progress continues with the development of the HPV vaccine to actively prevent cervical cancer. As a result of this integration of state-funded delivery systems and advanced biotechnology, the focus of modern medicine permanently shifted from expensive hospital cures to proactive, mass disease prevention.

Question 5 Examiner Model Answer (20/20 Marks • Grade 9)

To a very large extent, I disagree with the statement that the university-trained physician was the most important person providing healthcare in medieval England (c1250-c1500). While physicians held the highest professional and social status in medieval society, their practical impact was severely restricted to the wealthy elite. For the vast majority of the population, the actual burden of daily care and biological treatment was carried out by female family members in the home using traditional herbal remedies, supplemented by practically trained community healers like apothecaries and barber-surgeons.

On the one hand, for the wealthy elite and royalty, the university-trained physician was regarded as the most important and prestigious medical figure. The significance of the physician lay in their extensive academic medical training, which lasted between seven and ten years at Church-supervised universities such as Oxford, Cambridge, Paris, or Montpellier. This training was entirely book-based, requiring students to read and memorise Latin translations of ancient Greek and Roman texts by Hippocrates and Galen, rather than gaining hands-on clinical experience. When diagnosing patients, physicians rarely touched them; instead, they checked planetary alignments using an astrological Almanac and examined the colour, thickness, and smell of urine to identify internal humoural imbalances. They would then write a diagnostic brief directing lower-status manual practitioners to perform treatments. While their high fees made them inaccessible to 95% of medieval society, they were highly important in establishing a professional, 'rational' (natural) framework of medicine that moved away from purely supernatural explanations.

On the other hand, for the overwhelming majority of ordinary medieval people, the most important providers of healthcare were female family members using traditional herbal remedies in the home. Because university-trained physicians were exceptionally rare and expensive, the home was the true centre of medieval medicine. Women, such as mothers and wives, possessed a vast, practical knowledge of local plants, herbs, and home recipes passed down through generations. They prepared mixtures containing active natural ingredients-like garlic, onions, honey, and wine-which were highly effective. Modern scientific analysis of medieval recipes, such as those found in the Bald's Leechbook, has proven that these herbal remedies possessed genuine antibacterial properties that actively destroyed pathogens and promoted wound healing. Consequently, for the average peasant, these women were far more important than physicians because they provided both accessible daily 'care' (comfort and nutrition) and biologically active 'treatment'.

Furthermore, other practically trained, affordable healers played a far more significant role in the daily treatment of the sick than university physicians. Ordinary citizens who required physical procedures directly consulted barber-surgeons and apothecaries. Apothecaries mixed and sold herbal medicines, spices, and simple chemical compounds. Because they did not attend university, they were cheap and highly accessible. For physical treatments, peasants relied on barber-surgeons. Barber-surgeons were trained through practical apprenticeships rather than books, using sharp tools to perform haircuts alongside minor surgical procedures like lancing boils, pulling teeth, and carrying out bloodletting (phlebotomy) to rebalance the humours. Additionally, the Church operated around 1,100 charity hospitals in England by 1500. Run by monks and nuns, these hospitals did not provide medical cures, but they were highly important centres of spiritual care, shelter, and hot meals for the poor, elderly, and travellers.

In conclusion, I disagree with the statement because it confuses professional prestige with practical utility. The university-trained physician possessed prestigious medical training and held a monopoly over academic knowledge, but their clinical usefulness was almost non-existent due to their reliance on flawed classical texts and their high cost. The true backbone of medieval healthcare was decentralised and grassroots. For the general population, local apothecaries, barber-surgeons, and, above all, women in the home preparing herbal remedies were the most important providers of care and treatment, as they offered the only accessible, affordable, and biologically effective medicine of the Middle Ages.

Stimulus points: Germ Theory, 1861 • James Watson and Francis Crick mapping DNA
Question 6 Examiner Model Answer (20/20 Marks • Grade 9)

Louis Pasteur’s publication of Germ Theory in 1861 has a compelling claim to be the single greatest turning point in understanding the causes of disease between c1800 and the present, because it shattered a 2,000-year dead end of humoral, miasmatic, and spontaneous-generation beliefs. Nevertheless, a nuanced judgement must recognise two crucial qualifications: first, Pasteur’s 1861 paper required Robert Koch’s bacteriological methods (1876–84) before doctors accepted specific germ causation in humans; and second, Germ Theory could only explain infectious illnesses, meaning the 1953 discovery of DNA and twentieth-century lifestyle epidemiology were equally vital turning points for hereditary and non-communicable diseases.

In strong support of the statement, Pasteur’s 1861 swan-neck flask experiments marked the decisive watershed between pre-scientific speculation and modern microbiology. In c1800–1860, British medicine was trapped in bitter debates between miasmatists (who blamed foul-smelling air from slums) and believers in 'spontaneous generation' (who claimed decaying flesh created microbes rather than vice versa), which prevented even John Snow’s brilliant 1854 Soho cholera map from winning immediate scientific consensus. By proving that boiled broth remained sterile until exposed to dust-laden air—and showing in the late 1860s that specific microbes caused silkworm disease—Pasteur established the revolutionary principle that microscopic external organisms invade living tissue. Without this 1861 conceptual breakthrough, neither Lister’s antiseptics nor modern bacteriology and virology could have emerged.

However, Pasteur’s 1861 publication was an incomplete turning point on its own until Robert Koch transformed it into practical human pathology in the late 1870s and 1880s, and twentieth-century technology extended it to viruses. Because Pasteur was a French chemist studying beer, wine, and vinegar rather than a physician, influential British doctors like Henry Charlton Bastian fiercely resisted Germ Theory throughout the 1860s and 1870s. It took Koch’s solid agar cultures, chemical aniline dyes, oil-immersion photomicrography, and 'Koch’s Postulates' to isolate the exact human bacteria causing anthrax (1876), tuberculosis (1882), and cholera (1883–84), finally silencing British miasmatists. Later, the invention of the electron microscope in the 1930s allowed scientists to visualise sub-microscopic viruses causing influenza, polio, and measles.

Furthermore, in the modern period (c1900–present), two entirely separate breakthroughs—molecular genetics (DNA, 1953) and statistical lifestyle epidemiology—opened causal frontiers where Germ Theory had zero explanatory power. Microbes do not cause cystic fibrosis, sickle-cell anaemia, Huntington’s disease, or inherited BRCA breast cancer: understanding those illnesses required Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins’s X-ray crystallography and James Watson and Francis Crick’s 1953 double-helix model of DNA, culminating in the Human Genome Project (1990–2003). Equally, Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill’s 1950–54 epidemiological studies proving smoking caused lung cancer—alongside research into diet, alcohol, and air pollution—explained the chronic lifestyle killers that replaced infectious epidemics in post-war Britain.

In conclusion, I largely agree that Pasteur’s 1861 Germ Theory was the biggest overarching turning point since c1800, because overturning miasma and spontaneous generation established the empirical, laboratory-driven scientific method upon which Koch, virology, and public health built. Yet it was not the complete answer: Koch made Germ Theory clinically specific to human bacteria, while Watson, Crick, and Franklin’s 1953 DNA breakthrough and Doll and Hill’s epidemiology were essential second-wave turning points for hereditary and lifestyle disease.
