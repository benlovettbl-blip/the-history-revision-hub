Because the Church ran universities and book copying, questioning Galen was treated as a religious crime (heresy).
Q3 A conjunction of Saturn, Jupiter, and Mars
Believed to create poisonous air that descended to earth as deadly, foul-smelling miasma.
Q4 Bad air corrupted by rotting waste, swamps, corpses, or filth that poisoned the body
Led wealthy people to carry sweet-smelling flower posies and light scented fires to clear the air.
Q5 Publicly whipping themselves with leather scourges tipped with iron spikes
Hoped to stop God’s anger and end the plague through voluntary bodily suffering.
Q6 Blood, Phlegm, Yellow Bile (Choler), and Black Bile (Melancholy)
Good health was believed to exist when all four fluids were in natural balance inside the body.
Q7 Hot and Wet, linked with the season of Spring and the element of Air
Too much blood was believed to cause fevers, red skin, and an overly optimistic (sanguine) mood.
Q8 The Theory of Opposites
Treated an excess of one quality with its direct opposite (e.g. cold phlegm treated with hot pepper).
Q9 Bloodletting (phlebotomy)
Aimed to remove excess hot, wet blood to cool down fevers and restore internal humoural balance.
Q10 Opening a vein (with a fleam), Cupping (heated glass cups), and Leeches
Chosen depending on the patient’s age, illness, and how physically weak they were.
Q11 Laxatives (to empty bowels) and Emetics (to make patients vomit)
Believed to physically flush poisonous humours and corrupt bile out of the digestive tract.
Q12 The Regimen Sanitatis (Rule of Health)
Originated in Salerno and urged people to balance air, exercise, sleep, diet, excretion, and emotion.
Q13 “Care Not Cure”
Hospitals aimed to provide prayer, warmth, rest, and clean food rather than active medical treatments.
Q14 Monks and nuns, funded through Church charity and wills left by wealthy citizens
Run as religious houses where daily prayer, Christian charity, and quiet rest took first place.
Q15 The University-Trained Physician
Studied for up to 10 years in Latin reading Galen; affordable only for the king and wealthy nobles.
Q16 Between one-third and one-half of the population (approx. 1.5 to 2 million people)
Spread inland rapidly along trade roads, reaching London by autumn 1348 and killing over a third of the country.
Q17 Renaissance Humanism
Encouraged people to test ideas through scientific experiments rather than blindly accepting old books.
Q18 The movable-type printing press (Johannes Gutenberg)
Stopped monk copying mistakes and allowed anatomical books with drawings to be mass-produced across Europe.
Q19 “Nullius in Verba” ("Take nobody’s word for it")
Stressed that scientific theories had to be proven by repeatable experiments, not ancient tradition.
Q20 Philosophical Transactions
Allowed scientists and doctors across Europe to read, test, and share new medical experiments.
Q21 He revived careful bedside observation of patients rather than relying on old textbooks
Stressed recording patient symptoms closely at the bedside instead of debating abstract medical theories.
Q22 Argued diseases were distinct species that could be grouped into families like plants
Shifted medicine away from unique humoural imbalances toward identifying specific, recurring illnesses.
Q23 Measles and Scarlet Fever
Showed that illnesses previously lumped together as general fevers had distinct symptoms and rashes.
Q24 Andreas Vesalius
Professor of Surgery at Padua; transformed anatomical science through direct human dissection.
Q25 De Motu Cordis (On the Motion of the Heart and Blood)
Proved mathematically and through experiments that blood circulates continuously in one closed loop.
Q26 Royal Physician to the King
His high royal status gave him money and protection to carry out pioneering experiments on the heart.
Q27 That the liver constantly made new blood from food, which was burned up by the body like fuel
Galen taught that veins carried blood from the liver, while arteries carried separate vital spirit from the heart.
Q28 Calculated the heart pumped 540 pounds of blood an hour—far more than the body’s weight
Proved the liver could not possibly make that much blood; the same blood had to be recycled constantly.
Q29 Pushed blood along a vein with a finger, proving one-way valves stop blood flowing backward
Showed that blood in veins flows only toward the heart, blocked by tiny pocket valves from reversing.
Q30 Approximately 100,000 people (around 20% of London’s population)
The worst epidemic in England since 1348, causing mass panic across the crowded capital city.
Q31 Punishment from God, bad planet alignments, and poisonous bad air (miasma)
Shows deep continuity; Renaissance doctors still had not discovered bacteria or that fleas spread plague.
Q32 Locked inside for 28 days, watched by guards, with a red cross painted on the door
Doors were painted with a red cross and "Lord Have Mercy on Us", with burials done only after dark.

Enquiries 5–8: Era 3 18th & 19th Century Medicine & Public Health (c1700–c1900) • Qs 33–64 Official 2-Mark Standard: 1m Fact + 1m Explanation
Q33 The Theory of Spontaneous Generation
Believed bacteria were the result of rotting matter rather than the cause of decay and disease.
Q34 Microscopic living germs (microbes) in the air, disproving Spontaneous Generation
Showed that airborne microbes caused liquids to spoil, laying the foundation for modern medicine.
Q35 Boiled broth in S-shaped flasks; the broth stayed sterile until the glass neck was broken
Showed germs fell into open liquids from dust in the air, rather than appearing out of nothing.
Q36 Robert Koch
Used industrial chemical dyes to stain clear bacteria and grew pure colonies on solid agar jelly.
Q37 Tuberculosis (1882) and Cholera (1883)
Proved definitively that specific microbes caused specific human illnesses, finally disproving miasma.
Q38 Koch’s Postulates
A strict scientific standard: isolate the microbe, grow it pure, test in an animal, and retrieve it again.
Q39 Vaccines for Chicken Cholera, Anthrax (1881), and Rabies (1885)
Discovered that weakened or aged pathogens stimulated protective antibodies without killing the host.
Q40 National rivalry led both governments to pour huge funding into rival medical research teams
Each country competed to prove scientific superiority and discover cures to save soldiers’ lives.
Q41 Inoculation (variolation)
Patients caught a mild dose of real smallpox, but risked dying or triggering a full epidemic.
Q42 Lady Mary Wortley Montagu
Had her children inoculated after seeing it in Turkey, popularizing it among the British aristocracy.
Q43 Milkmaids who caught mild cowpox never caught the deadly smallpox virus
Jenner realized that catching mild cowpox gave people lifelong biological protection against smallpox.
Q44 Infected him with cowpox pus from a milkmaid, then exposed him to smallpox; he did not get sick
James developed mild cowpox but completely resisted smallpox infection, proving vaccination worked.
Q45 They stood to lose large profits made by charging wealthy families high fees for inoculation
Money and self-interest led many private doctors to publish pamphlets attacking vaccination.
Q46 The Crimean War (1853–1856)
Newspaper reports of shocking hospital neglect and dying British soldiers caused national outrage.
Q47 Approximately 42% (over 4 in 10 patients died)
Soldiers were dying from typhus, cholera, and dysentery caused by blocked sewers beneath hospital wards.
Q48 Cleaned sewers, washed bedding, scrubbed wards, boiled drinking water, and opened windows
Dramatic proof that cleanliness, fresh air, and proper sanitation saved lives in hospitals.
Q49 Pain (shock), Infection (gangrene), and Bleeding (blood loss)
Surgeons had to amputate limbs in under a minute while conscious patients were held down by assistants.
Q50 James Young Simpson
Tested chloroform fumes with his assistants in Edinburgh, waking up safely under the dinner table.
Q51 Queen Victoria used chloroform during the birth of Prince Leopold
Silenced Church critics who claimed the Bible demanded women feel pain during childbirth.
Q52 Death rates rose because surgeons operated deeper and slower in dirty, unsterilized conditions
Surgeons wore bloodstained coats and didn’t wash hands; longer operations carried more deadly bacteria deep inside.
Q53 Joseph Lister
Understood that wound rotting was caused by living airborne germs, not chemical reactions.
Q54 Asiatic Cholera (striking the port of Sunderland)
Caused violent diarrhoea, vomiting, dehydration, and blue skin, often killing people within hours.
Q55 “Laissez-faire” (leave alone)
Local taxpayers and councils refused to pay higher taxes to clean streets or lay water pipes.
Q56 Edwin Chadwick
Used statistics to prove overcrowding, uncollected sewage, and bad water caused widespread illness.
Q57 A chemical drug that seeks out and destroys bacteria without harming healthy human cells
Replaced harsh chemicals like carbolic acid with targeted drugs that killed only specific germs.
Q58 Salvarsan 606 (an arsenic compound)
Tested over 600 chemical compounds before finding that number 606 killed the syphilis bacteria.
Q59 Prontosil (the first sulpha drug)
A bright red dye that killed streptococcus bacteria, saving Domagk’s daughter from an arm amputation.
Q60 Aneurin (Nye) Bevan
Overcame fierce opposition from private doctors by allowing hospital consultants to keep private beds.
Q61 Had to pay out of their own pocket, use charity hospitals, or go without treatment
The 1911 National Insurance Act covered only working men, leaving wives and children unprotected.
Q62 Returned from holiday to find penicillium mould had dissolved staphylococcus bacteria on an agar dish
Realized the green mould produced a natural chemical juice that killed dangerous bacteria.
Q63 Howard Florey and Ernst Chain
Revived Fleming’s forgotten paper and extracted stable penicillin powder in their Oxford lab.
Q64 Injected eight mice with lethal bacteria; the four given penicillin survived while the other four died
Proved definitively that penicillin worked inside a living body as a powerful antibiotic.

Enquiries 9–12: Era 4 Modern Medicine & The Western Front (c1900–present) • Qs 65–96 Official 2-Mark Standard: 1m Fact + 1m Explanation
Q65 A battlefield bulge surrounded on three sides by the enemy
Left British troops exposed to German artillery fire from higher ground on surrounding ridges.
Q66 It protected the English Channel ports used for supplies and reinforcements
If Ypres fell, the German army could cut off supply links between Britain and the front line.
Q67 57,000 casualties (over 19,000 killed)
The worst single day in British military history, completely overwhelming frontline medical stations.
Q68 An underground hospital in chalk tunnels (Thompson’s Cave)
Contained 700 beds, operating rooms, water, and power, completely safe from shellfire.
Q69 Massed deployment of over 450 British tanks
Broke German lines and created the conditions for the first operational blood bank.
Q70 Standing for days in cold, waterlogged mud and tight wet boots
Slowed blood flow, causing feet to go numb, blister, swell, and turn gangrenous.
Q71 Rubbing feet with whale oil and changing into dry socks twice daily
Officers held daily foot inspections; soldiers worked in pairs to dry each other’s feet.
Q72 Amputation of toes or the whole foot
Necessary to stop spreading gangrene and fatal blood poisoning once tissue died.
Q73 High-explosive artillery shells and shrapnel
Exploding steel shell casings threw jagged fragments that shattered bones and ripped muscle.
Q74 The soil had been heavily manured for centuries, filling it with deadly bacteria
Manured soil contained tetanus and gas gangrene bacteria that multiplied in deep wounds without oxygen.
Q75 Gas Gangrene
Soil bacteria produced poisons that turned muscle black, killing tissue within hours and causing death in a day.
Q76 Anti-tetanus serum (ATS)
Massively reduced deaths from tetanus (lockjaw) caused by soil bacteria entering open wounds.
Q77 The Brodie Helmet
Made of pressed steel with a wide rim to deflect falling shrapnel and dirt.
Q78 Chlorine Gas
A greenish-yellow choking gas that damaged the lungs, causing soldiers to drown in bodily fluids.
Q79 Phosgene Gas
Smelled like mouldy hay and acted slowly, often killing an exposed soldier within 48 hours.
Q80 Mustard Gas
An oily liquid that stayed in the soil for weeks, causing severe skin blisters, blindness, and lung burns.
Q81 To move, sort, and treat wounded soldiers in stages from the battlefield to safety
Ensured casualties were treated quickly without clogging up frontline medical stations.
Q82 Within 200 metres of the frontline trench, in a dugout or ruined cellar
Positioned close to the fighting so wounded men could receive basic first aid within minutes.
Q83 Basic first aid (bandages, pain relief, splints); no surgery could be done
Patched up walking wounded to fight, or sent stretcher cases back to dressing stations.
Q84 The Dressing Station (Advanced or Main Dressing Station)
Run by the Field Ambulance; gave fresh dressings, tetanus jabs, and hot drinks.
Q85 7 to 12 miles behind the front line, near railways and outside shell range
Positioned near railheads so stabilized wounded could be sent by train to coastal hospitals.
Q86 Triage: sorting wounded into walking cases, urgent surgery, and dying
Focused limited surgeons and operating tables on men with the best chance of survival.
Q87 Near French and Belgian ports (Calais, Boulogne, Le Havre, Rouen)
Close to the sea so long-term casualties could be loaded directly onto hospital ships for Britain.
Q88 Driving motor ambulance convoys under shellfire between medical stations
FANY drivers were the first women to drive British military vehicles on the Western Front.
Q89 Cutting away all dead, damaged, and infected tissue around the wound
Removed the dead flesh that dangerous gas gangrene bacteria needed to grow.
Q90 The Carrel-Dakin Method
Flushed a mild antiseptic bleach solution through rubber tubes directly into deep wounds.
Q91 The chemical solution spoiled quickly and had to be freshly mixed every 6 hours
Hard to prepare during large battles when thousands of casualties arrived at once.
Q92 Only 20% survived (an 80% death rate)
Bone ends ground together in bumpy wagons, tearing large blood vessels and causing fatal shock.
Q93 Robert Jones (nephew of Hugh Owen Thomas)
Set up training centres for medics; survival rates for femur fractures jumped from 20% to 82%.
Q94 Discovered different human blood groups (A, B, and O)
Showed why earlier transfusions often caused blood to clump together and kill the patient.
Q95 Blood Group O
Essential for battle casualties because Group O blood could be given safely without testing.
Q96 Sodium Citrate
Stopped blood clotting without poisoning the wounded patient receiving the transfusion.
