1 (b) Describe one feature of trench fever on the Western Front.(2 marks)
Examiner Model Answer (2/2 Marks)

Feature: Trench fever was a debilitating relapsing five-day illness transmitted between soldiers by body lice living in the seams of woollen khaki uniforms.

Detail: Causing violent headaches, shivering fevers, and agonising shin and back pains that sidelined casualties for weeks, its louse-excreta transmission was proven in 1918, prompting the army to expand Divisional bathhouses, steam disinfestors, and Serbian barrels.

How useful are Sources A and B for an enquiry into the effects of poison gas attacks and the methods used to protect soldiers on the Western Front?

Explain your answer, using Sources A and B and your knowledge of the historical context.(8 marks)
Examiner Model Answer (8/8 Marks • Grade 9)

Source A is highly useful for an enquiry into the physical trauma and early makeshift response to poison gas because it provides direct clinical evidence from May 1915, weeks after Germany’s first chlorine release at Second Ypres (22 April 1915). The RAMC officer records men stumbling into the Advanced Dressing Station clutching their throats, gasping violently as fluid filled their stripped lungs (internal suffocation), and having inflamed eyes irrigated with simple bicarbonate of soda solution. This matches historical context: caught completely unprepared for chemical warfare in spring 1915, troops initially improvised urine- or bicarbonate-soaked cotton pads, while ADS staff could offer only oxygen cylinders, eye-washes, and rest. Its provenance as a contemporaneous May 1915 ADS medical logbook gives it high clinical candour free from press spin, though being written in May 1915 means it covers only chlorine and cannot reflect deadlier phosgene (December 1915) or blistering mustard gas (July 1917).

Source B is equally useful for showing how rapidly the British Army developed mass-produced protective equipment to counter suffocating gas clouds by mid-1916. Photographed near Ovillers on the Somme in July 1916, the British Vickers gunners wear Phenate-Hexamine (PH) flannel hoods tucked tightly inside their tunic collars, fitted with circular glass eyepieces and a one-way rubber exhalation tube. Contextually, over 26 million Hypo and PH chemical-impregnated hoods were issued in 1915–16 to neutralise chlorine and phosgene, keeping fatal British gas deaths below 6,000 (~1% of total fatalities) despite intense psychological terror. Evaluating provenance, however, Second Lieutenant Ernest Brooks took this staged official War Office photograph to reassure British newspaper readers that troops were well protected; a still photograph hides how PH eyepieces misted up, perished in heavy rain, and were already being superseded from August 1916 by Edward Harrison’s far superior charcoal-canister Small Box Respirator.

Together, Sources A and B are strongly complementary: Source A captures the acute respiratory agony and helpless early treatment of spring 1915 chlorine casualties, while Source B demonstrates the War Office’s rapid technological adaptation by July 1916.

Section B: Medicine in Britain, c1250–present (36 Marks)
3. Explain one way in which ideas about the cause of illness in the medieval period (c1250–c1500) were similar to ideas about the cause of illness in the Renaissance period (c1500–c1700).(4 marks)
Examiner Model Answer (4/4 Marks • Grade 9)

One clear similarity was the enduring belief across both periods that epidemic disease was spread by miasma—poisonous, foul-smelling air rising from decaying matter and filth.

In Medieval England (c1250–c1500), both university physicians and town authorities believed that rotting butcher waste, stagnant cesspits, and unburied corpses released corrupt vapours that poisoned the body when inhaled; during the 1348 Black Death, King Edward III ordered London’s streets cleared of filth and citizens carried sweet-smelling herbs.

Similarly, in the Medical Renaissance (c1500–c1700), despite new anatomical discoveries by Vesalius and Harvey, people still lacked Germ Theory and blamed the 1665 Great Plague on miasmatic air—prompting London’s Mayor to order giant coal fires burned day and night in the streets while plague doctors stuffed beak masks with aromatic lavender and spices.

4. Explain why there was rapid progress in the development and mass production of penicillin in the years c1938–c1945. You may use the following in your answer: • Howard Florey and Ernst Chain • US Government funding and the Second World War You must also use information of your own.(12 marks)
Stimulus points: Howard Florey and Ernst Chain • US Government funding and the Second World War
Examiner Model Answer (12/12 Marks • Grade 9)

The primary scientific driver behind the rapid progress of penicillin was the systematic biochemical work of Howard Florey and Ernst Chain at Oxford University, who succeeded in turning Alexander Fleming's 1928 accidental laboratory observation into a purified, stable, and highly effective drug. While Fleming had discovered that the Penicillium notatum mould killed staphylococci bacteria, he lacked the chemical expertise to isolate the active substance and abandoned it as a minor local antiseptic. In 1938, Florey and Chain revived the research, and by 1940, Chain successfully purified the active compound. Consequently, they demonstrated penicillin's curative power on infected mice, followed by successful trials on human patients, such as police officer Albert Alexander in 1941. This clinical proof demonstrated that penicillin was non-toxic and could destroy bacterial pathogens inside the bloodstream, establishing the scientific foundation necessary to justify transition to mass production.

This scientific breakthrough was scaled up globally because of unprecedented US Government funding and direct state intervention under the urgent pressures of the Second World War. When Florey travelled to the USA in July 1941 to seek manufacturing aid, British chemical companies were fully occupied with munitions production. Recognising the immense military value of an infection-killing antibiotic for treating battlefield casualties and preventing fatal gas gangrene, the US government bypassed traditional market constraints and poured millions of dollars in federal grants into pharmaceutical firms. Consequently, this state-funded mobilisation allowed American factories to purchase expensive industrial infrastructure, including giant deep-fermentation tanks. This massive financial and logistical intervention led directly to a manufacturing revolution, allowing the supply of penicillin to scale up from virtually zero to over 2.3 million life-saving doses in time to treat Allied forces on D-Day in June 1944.

Furthermore, progress was accelerated by international scientific collaboration and advances in agricultural technology, which provided a distinct third factor beyond the initial stimulus points. To maximise production yields, researchers at a government-backed laboratory in Peoria, Illinois, systematically searched for more productive strains of the Penicillium mould, discovering a highly potent, high-yield strain on a cantaloupe melon in 1943. By combining this new strain with the deep-fermentation technology, scientists multiplied the production rate of pure penicillin millions of times over. This meant that bacterial infections, which had previously been the leading cause of death following minor cuts or surgeries, became completely curable on a mass scale, permanently transforming modern global healthcare and establishing penicillin as the world's first mass-produced antibiotic.

Question 5 Examiner Model Answer (20/20 Marks • Grade 9)

To a moderate extent, I agree that Edward Jenner's development of the smallpox vaccine in 1796 represents a monumental turning point in the prevention of disease. By utilising empirical observation to prove that cowpox infection conferred immunity against smallpox, Jenner created the world's first secure, scientific immunisation technique. However, while his discovery was intellectually revolutionary, its practical significance was heavily limited in the short term by public conservatism and medical opposition. Ultimately, the transition to truly effective, nationwide disease prevention was only achieved when the British government abandoned its traditional laissez-faire policy and passed compulsory public health legislation, most notably the 1875 Public Health Act, which systematically tackled urban filth.

On the one hand, Jenner's 1796 smallpox vaccine was a major turning point because it provided a safe, scientific alternative to the dangerous practice of inoculation. Inoculation, which had been introduced to Britain from Turkey in the early eighteenth century, involved deliberately scratching live smallpox pus into a healthy person's skin. While this often conferred mild immunity, it was highly dangerous: it frequently caused full-blown, fatal infections and actively turned inoculated individuals into contagious vectors, sparking new epidemics. By vaccinating young James Phipps with benign cowpox and subsequently proving he was immune to smallpox, Jenner demonstrated that prevention did not require risking the patient's life. Consequently, Jenner's work provided a safe, reliable, and reproducible medical tool that eventually enabled the World Health Organisation to eradicate smallpox globally, representing a profound milestone in immunology.

However, the immediate preventative significance of Jenner's discovery was heavily undermined by deep-seated public conservatism, medical self-interest, and the absence of a scientific framework to explain how vaccines worked. Because Jenner published his findings in 1798 without being able to explain why cowpox prevented smallpox-as Germ Theory was not published until 1861-many doctors and citizens regarded vaccination with extreme suspicion. Anti-vaccination campaigns published satirical cartoons of people turning into cows, and the Royal Society refused to publish his research, forcing him to print it privately. Furthermore, inoculation was a highly lucrative business for professional doctors, who actively lobbied against the free adoption of Jenner's vaccine to protect their profits. As a result of this resistance, smallpox remained a major urban killer for decades after Jenner's breakthrough, demonstrating that a scientific discovery alone is insufficient to prevent disease without administrative force.

Furthermore, the primary driver of rapid, nationwide disease prevention during the nineteenth century was not medical discovery, but the British government's complete abandonment of its laissez-faire administrative policy. Spurred by John Snow's 1854 proof that cholera was water-borne and Bazalgette's massive London sewer construction, Parliament passed the landmark 1875 Public Health Act. Unlike previous optional acts, the 1875 Act made sanitation compulsory. Local councils nationwide were legally forced to provide clean, filtered water, build modern underground drainage, clear rubbish, and employ medical officers. Consequently, by systematically removing the filthy, contaminated water and organic waste that caused water-borne epidemics, the government dramatically reduced the national mortality rates of diseases like cholera and typhoid. This administrative intervention saved far more lives than Jenner's vaccine did in the nineteenth century, proving that state power was the true engine of prevention.

In conclusion, while Jenner's vaccine was a brilliant theoretical breakthrough that laid the foundation for modern immunology, it was only a partial turning point c1700-c1900. Without the scientific validation of Louis Pasteur's Germ Theory (1861) and, crucially, the administrative enforcement of the compulsory 1853 Vaccination Act, Jenner's work remained underutilised. The most significant turning point in disease prevention during this era was the compulsory 1875 Public Health Act. By utilising state power to enforce urban sanitation, the government actively addressed the environmental causes of epidemics, demonstrating that administrative intervention was far more effective at protecting public health than isolated scientific breakthroughs.

Question 6 Examiner Model Answer (20/20 Marks • Grade 9)

The discovery of the double-helix structure of DNA in 1953 was undoubtedly the most fundamental conceptual breakthrough in twentieth-century biology, unlocking an entirely new category of hereditary and genetic disease causation that Germ Theory could never explain. However, judging significance across the whole period c1900–present requires weighing theoretical depth against real-world mortality: epidemiological research linking lifestyle and environmental factors—such as tobacco smoking, diet, and air pollution—to cancer and heart disease explained the biggest everyday killers of modern Britain, while high-tech diagnostic imaging transformed clinical identification in living patients.

On the one hand, the mapping of DNA in Cambridge in 1953 by James Watson and Francis Crick—relying crucially on Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins’s high-resolution X-ray crystallography ('Photograph 51') at King’s College London—revolutionised understanding of non-infectious illness. Whereas Pasteur and Koch had proven external microbes caused contagious diseases, DNA revealed the internal chemical code passed from parents to children inside every cell nucleus. Powered by supercomputers and robotic sequencers, this culminated in the international Human Genome Project (1990–2003), which mapped all ~20,000–25,000 human genes. Consequently, doctors identified the exact faulty genes or chromosomal errors causing cystic fibrosis, sickle-cell anaemia, Huntington’s disease, Down’s syndrome, and hereditary BRCA1/BRCA2 breast and ovarian cancer risks—opening a completely new frontier in preventative genetic screening.

On the other hand, statistical epidemiology into lifestyle and environmental causes had a far larger, more immediate impact on understanding—and preventing—the mass killers of twentieth- and twenty-first-century Britain. Once antibiotics and childhood vaccines conquered infectious epidemics after 1945, chronic non-communicable diseases like lung cancer, coronary heart disease, stroke, and Type 2 diabetes became the leading causes of death. In 1950 and 1954, British epidemiologists Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill proved statistically through patient interviews and a survey of 40,000 British doctors that heavy cigarette smoking directly caused the twentieth-century lung cancer epidemic, later extending proof to passive second-hand smoke. Similarly, medical research linked the 1952 Great Smog to fatal bronchitis, alcohol misuse to liver cirrhosis, and high-sugar diets and inactivity to cardiovascular disease—giving citizens and governments actionable causal knowledge that saved millions of lives through the 1956 Clean Air Act and 2007 smoking ban, whereas knowing a genetic defect like cystic fibrosis still has not produced a simple universal cure.

Furthermore, breakthroughs in diagnostic science and imaging technology were indispensable both for making DNA research possible and for diagnosing illness inside individual patients. Without Franklin’s X-ray diffraction technology, electron microscopes (1930s—revealing viruses far smaller than bacteria), and automated DNA sequencers, genetics could not have advanced. Equally, in everyday hospital medicine, blood-testing biochemistry, endoscopes, Godfrey Hounsfield’s CT scanner (1972), and MRI/PET-CT scans enabled clinicians to pinpoint internal tumours, blocked arteries, and strokes long before symptoms turned fatal.

In conclusion, while the 1953 discovery of DNA was the most profound scientific paradigm shift of the modern era—explaining hereditary illness at the molecular level for the first time in human history—it worked alongside, rather than eclipsing, epidemiological discovery of lifestyle causes and high-tech diagnostics. DNA unlocked the blueprint of genetic vulnerability, but Doll and Hill’s lifestyle epidemiology explained why millions of Britons actually fell ill with lung cancer and heart disease.
