Enquiry 1 Eyewitness Voice WSPU Oration & Holloway Prison Record (1913)

"Freedom or Death": Emmeline Pankhurst’s Speech & Holloway Prison Courage (1913)

Emmeline Pankhurst (Hartford Speech, 13 Nov 1913 • WSPU Prison Records)
Historical Context: For nearly fifty years, Millicent Fawcett’s peaceful Suffragists (NUWSS) presented polite petitions that male MPs repeatedly ignored. Founding the militant Suffragettes (WSPU) in Manchester in 1903 under the motto 'Deeds, Not Words,' Emmeline Pankhurst endured hunger strikes in Holloway Prison—where Suffragettes smuggled tiny pencils and tissue-paper notes inside hollowed shoe heels—before delivering her famous 'Freedom or Death' speech while on temporary prison release under the 1913 'Cat and Mouse' Act.

Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Emmeline Pankhurst (WSPU)[EM-uh-leen PANK-hurst]
Founder of the militant Women’s Social and Political Union ('Suffragettes') in 1903 under the slogan 'Deeds, Not Words'.
Millicent Fawcett (NUWSS)[MIL-ih-sent FAW-sit]
Leader of the peaceful, constitutional 'Suffragists', building over 500 branches nationwide through marches, petitions, and lobbying MPs.
Cat and Mouse Act (1913)[KAT und MOWS]
Government law releasing weak hunger-striking Suffragettes from prison until they regained enough strength for police to re-arrest them.
Representation of the People Act (1918)[en-FRAN-chize-ment]
Wartime reform granting votes to all men aged 21+ and 8.4 million women aged 30+ who met property rules (equalised at age 21 in 1928).

Inside Holloway Prison in London, Suffragette prisoners hid stubby pencils inside the hollowed wooden heels of their boots to scribble secret diaries on rough brown prison paper. Weakened by hunger strikes under the 1913 'Cat and Mouse' Act, fifty-five-year-old Emmeline Pankhurst stepped onto a stage in Hartford, Connecticut, on 13 November 1913. She spoke like a front-line commander.

"Tonight I am not here to advocate woman suffrage. I am here as a soldier who has stepped briefly off the field of battle. Strange as it seems, I have come to explain what civil war looks like when it is waged by women.

Suppose the men of Hartford had a grievance, and they laid that grievance before their parliament, and parliament stubbornly refused to listen. What would be the proper, peaceful course for the men of Hartford to take? At the very next election, the men of Hartford would vote that parliament out! But let the men imagine that they had no votes at all. What would men do then? You chose between freedom or death in 1776.

You have two hungry babies wanting to be fed. One baby is patient and waits quietly until its mother is ready. The other baby is impatient. It cries lustily, screams, kicks, and makes everybody uncomfortable until it is fed. Well, we know perfectly well which baby is attended to first. That is the whole history of politics."

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 How did Suffragette prisoners inside Holloway Prison manage to write secret diaries, and why did Emmeline Pankhurst say women could not simply vote out a stubborn parliament?
2 Look closely at Emmeline Pankhurst’s "two hungry babies" story. Which baby gets fed first, and what point is she making about politics?
3 Do you agree with Pankhurst’s "two hungry babies" argument that loud, disruptive protest gets results faster than waiting politely—or can making people uncomfortable sometimes backfire?

Enquiry 2 True Historical Story True Historical Micro-Story (5 July 1948)

"No Bill by the Bed": Thirteen-Year-Old Sylvia Diggory & Aneurin Bevan Launch the NHS (5 July 1948)

The History Revision Hub (True Historical Story • Park Hospital Davyhulme & NHS Archive Records)
Historical Context: Before 5 July 1948, falling seriously ill could bankrupt a British working-class family: insured fathers saw a panel doctor, while mothers and children often paid cash fees they could scarcely afford or went without care. Inspired by Sir William Beveridge’s 1942 report against the 'Five Giant Evils' (Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor, Idleness), Clement Attlee’s Labour government and former Welsh coalminer Aneurin Bevan launched the world’s first universal National Health Service free at the point of need.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Aneurin ('Nye') Bevan[ah-NYE-rin BEV-un]
Former Welsh coalminer and Labour Minister of Health who steered the National Health Service to launch on 5 July 1948.
Sylvia Diggory (Davyhulme)[DAY-vee-hyoom]
Thirteen-year-old schoolgirl treated for acute kidney/liver illness at Park Hospital, Davyhulme, who greeted Bevan as the symbolic first NHS patient.
Five Giant Evils (Beveridge, 1942)[BEV-er-ij]
Economist Sir William Beveridge’s names for Britain’s five social crises: Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor, and Idleness.
Free at the Point of Use[YOO-nih-vur-sul]
The founding NHS rule that medical care is funded together through taxes and National Insurance, so no patient faces a fee when sick.

As church clocks struck midnight entering Monday 5 July 1948, something vanished from every hospital ward across Britain. The doctor’s bill book was gone forever. Until that Sunday night, working-class mothers in Manchester or Glasgow dreaded calling a doctor for a sick child. Every home visit cost precious shillings needed for coal and bread. Millions of older people squinted through cheap glasses chosen by guesswork off market stalls.

That bright Monday morning at Park Hospital in Davyhulme, near Manchester, thirteen-year-old Sylvia Diggory lay against crisp white pillows. She was fighting a dangerous liver and kidney infection. Footsteps echoed down the polished ward floor. Looking up, Sylvia saw a broad-shouldered Welshman with silver-streaked hair stop beside her bed. It was Health Minister Aneurin Bevan, a former coalminer’s son from South Wales.

Smiling beside two ward nurses, Bevan shook thirteen-year-old Sylvia’s hand as the first patient of the brand-new National Health Service. He asked gently if she felt comfortable, and told reporters outside that lifting money fear from every sickbed made Britain a fairer land.

Guided by Sir William Beveridge’s wartime plan to defeat the Five Giant Evils—Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor, and Idleness—Clement Attlee’s government had kept its word. Within weeks, millions of citizens rushed to test eyes and mend teeth without paying a single penny at the door.

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 What vanished from every British hospital ward at midnight on 5 July 1948, and why had falling ill been so frightening for working-class families before that night?
2 Why did Health Minister Aneurin Bevan stop beside thirteen-year-old Sylvia Diggory’s bed at Park Hospital, and what happened across Britain within weeks?
3 Aneurin Bevan said that "lifting money fear from every sickbed made Britain a fairer land." How does knowing you will never face a doctor’s bill when you are ill change everyday life for a family?

"London Is the Place for Me": Lord Kitchener on the Windrush Gangplank & Sam King MBE (22 June 1948)

Lord Kitchener (Aldwyn Roberts, Pathé Newsreel, 22 June 1948) & Sam King MBE (IWM Oral History)
Historical Context: On 22 June 1948—just thirteen days before the NHS opened—the troopship HMT Empire Windrush docked at Tilbury in Essex carrying hundreds of Caribbean citizens answering bombed-out Britain’s acute labour shortage. Under the 1948 British Nationality Act, every passenger held full legal British citizenship. On the deck stood twenty-six-year-old Trinidadian calypso poet Aldwyn Roberts ('Lord Kitchener') and Jamaican Royal Air Force wartime veteran Sam King.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Calypso (Lord Kitchener)[kuh-LIP-soh]
Witty, rhythmic Trinidadian sung poetry blending West African griot storytelling with topical news—sung live at Tilbury Docks by Aldwyn Roberts ('Lord Kitchener').
HMT Empire Windrush (22 June 1948)[EM-pyre WIND-rush]
Former German liner turned British troopship bringing Caribbean Commonwealth citizens to help rebuild post-war transport, housing, and hospitals.
British Nationality Act (1948)[KOM-un-weltth]
Law confirming that all 800 million people across Britain and its Empire/Commonwealth held equal status as 'Citizens of the United Kingdom and Colonies'.
Pardner Savings Club[PARD-ner]
Traditional Caribbean cooperative savings pool allowing Windrush families refused bank loans to combine weekly wages and buy homes of their own.

As morning mist lifted off the Thames at Tilbury Docks on 22 June 1948, Pathé newsreel cameras rolled beside the gangplank of HMT Empire Windrush. Smiling beneath a sharp felt hat, twenty-six-year-old Trinidadian poet Aldwyn Roberts—known as 'Lord Kitchener'—stepped up to the microphone. Right there on deck, he sang the joyous calypso he had written at sea.

"London is the place for me,
London this lovely city,
You can go to France or America, India, Asia or Australia,
But you must come back to London city!"

Standing nearby in a neat suit was Jamaican Royal Air Force engineer Sam King, returning to the country he had already defended against Hitler. "In 1944 I answered the poster in Kingston saying 'The Mother Country Needs You' and served in the RAF. After peace came, bombed-out Britain needed hands to run the trains, rebuild houses, and staff the new hospitals. We were not strangers arriving uninvited—our passports said 'Citizen of the United Kingdom and Colonies.'"

Yet optimism soon met a cold shock. Across London and Birmingham hung handwritten window cards that broke no British law in 1948. "Room to Let: No Irish, No Blacks, No Dogs." Shut out by landlords and banks, Windrush pioneers pooled weekly wages in 'pardner' clubs to buy houses and staffed Bevan’s brand-new NHS wards. After the 1958 Notting Hill attacks, Trinidadian editor Claudia Jones launched the joyful Caribbean Carnival.

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 Contrast the joyful mood of Lord Kitchener singing 'London Is the Place for Me' on the Windrush gangplank with the handwritten window cards passengers soon saw in London and Birmingham.
2 Why did Sam King insist that the Windrush passengers were 'not strangers arriving uninvited' when they stepped ashore in June 1948?
3 When landlords and banks shut them out—and after the 1958 Notting Hill attacks—how did Windrush pioneers build a lasting home and community in Britain anyway?

Enquiry 4 True Historical Story True Civil Rights Micro-Story (Bristol, 1963–1968)

"Your Two O’Clock Appointment Is Coloured": Guy Bailey, Paul Stephenson & Bristol (1963)

The History Revision Hub (True Historical Story • Grounded in Guy Bailey, Roy Hackett & Paul Stephenson Testimonies)
Historical Context: In 1963, over 3,000 Black Britons lived in Bristol, yet the state-owned Bristol Omnibus Company enforced a rigid 'colour bar' refusing to hire a single Black or Asian driver or conductor despite desperate staff shortages. Organised by Roy Hackett, Owen Henry, Audley Evans, Prince Brown, spokesperson Paul Stephenson, and eighteen-year-old applicant Guy Bailey, a peaceful sixty-day boycott reshaped British law.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Paul Stephenson & Guy Bailey[STEE-ven-sun / GAI BAY-lee]
26-year-old youth worker and 18-year-old applicant whose clever phone-interview test exposed the Bristol Omnibus Company’s racial hiring ban in April 1963.
Raghbir Singh[RAHG-beer SING]
Sikh university graduate hired on 17 September 1963 (alongside Jamaican Norman Samuels) as Bristol’s first non-white bus conductor after the boycott won.
Race Relations Acts (1965 & 1968)[lej-is-LAY-shun]
Landmark UK laws banning racial discrimination first in public places like pubs and buses (1965), and then in jobs and housing (1968).
Jayaben Desai (Grunwick, 1976–78)[JAH-yuh-ben deh-SAI]
East African Asian factory worker ('lions in saris') who led a famous two-year strike for workplace dignity and union rights in North London.

Every evening in spring 1963, the Bristol Evening Post printed urgent adverts begging for bus drivers and conductors. Yet whenever a Black citizen applied, clerks claimed every post had just been filled. Twenty-six-year-old youth worker Paul Stephenson decided to set a trap.

Phoning the Bristol Omnibus Company in his crisp London-Essex voice, Stephenson booked an interview for bright eighteen-year-old Boys’ Brigade warehouseman Guy Bailey. He never mentioned that Bailey was born in Jamaica. When Guy arrived in his best pressed suit, the clerk froze and called through the open door. "Your two o’clock appointment is here—and he’s coloured." Within sixty seconds, the manager told Guy every job was gone.

Caught red-handed, boss Ian Patey admitted the company barred Black crews. Inspired by Rosa Parks in Montgomery, Roy Hackett and Paul Stephenson called a citywide boycott on 29 April 1963. Not one Black Bristolian rode a bus. Students marched through town, MP Tony Benn cycled in support, and cricketer Sir Learie Constantine shamed the company on television.

On 28 August 1963—the exact afternoon Dr Martin Luther King Jr spoke in Washington—the company gave in! Weeks later, Sikh graduate Raghbir Singh clipped his first bus tickets. The next year, when Bristol’s Bay Horse pub refused to serve Stephenson half a pint because of his skin, his sit-in made headlines again. Together, those protests drove Britain’s 1965 and 1968 Race Relations Acts and inspired Jayaben Desai’s Grunwick strikers.

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8

2 Who joined together behind the Bristol Bus Boycott, and how did Stephenson’s 1963 bus campaign and his Bay Horse pub sit-in the next year change British law?
3 On 28 August 1963—the exact afternoon Dr Martin Luther King Jr spoke in Washington—the Bristol bus company finally gave in. What makes peaceful protests like boycotts and sit-ins so powerful when unfair rules are still legal?

Enquiry 5 Eyewitness Voice CND Founding Speech & First Aldermaston March (1958)

"Four Abreast in the Rain": Bertrand Russell’s Warning & The Road to Aldermaston (1958)

Bertrand Russell (Central Hall Westminster, 17 Feb 1958) & Easter 1958 Aldermaston Accounts
Historical Context: After Britain tested its first hydrogen bomb in 1957, a secret Cabinet study (the 1955 Strath Report) privately admitted just ten Soviet H-bombs would kill twelve million Britons and collapse medical care completely. While Whitehall hid that report for nearly fifty years, eighty-five-year-old philosopher Bertrand Russell, Canon John Collins, and artist Gerald Holtom launched the Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament (CND) in February 1958—sending thousands marching through Easter rain to Aldermaston.

UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Aldermaston[AWL-der-mah-stun]
Site of Britain’s Atomic Weapons Research Establishment in Berkshire, target of CND’s famous four-day 52-mile Easter marches from 1958 onward.
Bertrand Russell (CND)[BUR-trund RUS-ul]
Nobel Prize-winning British philosopher and mathematician who co-founded the Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament at age eighty-five in February 1958.
Gerald Holtom’s Peace Symbol[HOL-tum]
Designed in February 1958 for the first Aldermaston march by combining naval flag-semaphore letters 'N' and 'D' (Nuclear Disarmament) inside a circle.
Strath Report vs Protect and Survive[STRATH]
The secret 1955 Whitehall report admitting 10 H-bombs would kill 12 million Britons, contrasted with 1980 public leaflets advising families to hide under kitchen doors.
Greenham Common (1981–2000)[GREE-num KOM-un]
Women’s peace camp outside RAF Greenham Common where 30,000 women linked hands around the nine-mile base fence in December 1982 against US cruise missiles.

Inside locked Whitehall safes lay the secret 1955 Strath Report. It warned ministers that just ten hydrogen bombs on British cities would leave twelve million people dead and poison the island. Yet in public, leaflets told families to paint windows white and hide under kitchen doors.

On 17 February 1958, five thousand citizens packed Central Hall Westminster to launch the Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament. Stepping up with shock-white hair, eighty-five-year-old thinker Bertrand Russell spoke with quiet fire. "We are gathered tonight not for any party advantage, nor on behalf of Moscow or Washington. We meet for the continued survival of the human race. One mistake on a radar screen, or one panic among armed leaders, could end everything in a single afternoon. All the art, science, cities, and children that centuries have built would vanish."

Seven weeks later, on Good Friday 1958, artist Gerald Holtom handed out black-and-white signs bearing a brand-new badge. Inside a circle stood the naval flag letters for 'N' and 'D'—Nuclear Disarmament. Walking four abreast from Trafalgar Square, marchers set off along fifty-two miles of road toward the bomb plant at Aldermaston. Students, vicars, jazz bands, and parents pushing prams trudged through freezing April rain, sleet, and blisters. Britain kept its bomb, yet that rainy Berkshire march shaped peaceful protest right down to the 30,000 women linking hands around Greenham Common in 1982.

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 What shocking gap lay between the secret 1955 Strath Report locked in Whitehall safes and the public leaflets given to British families?
2 Why did eighty-five-year-old Bertrand Russell warn five thousand listeners that everything could vanish "in a single afternoon," and who walked the fifty-two miles to Aldermaston seven weeks later?
3 The story notes that "Britain kept its bomb" even after 52-mile marches in freezing rain and 30,000 women linking hands around Greenham Common in 1982. Does a peaceful protest still matter even if a government does not immediately change its mind?
