Ramparts of Chalk and Gold: Inside Maiden Castle Hillfort

The History Revision Hub (from Mortimer Wheeler's Dorset Excavations)
Historical Context: Before Roman legions conquered Britain, Iron Age Celtic tribes built Europe's largest earth hillforts, engineered snug chimney-free roundhouses, and twisted pure gold into gleaming torcs.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Hillfort[HIL-fort]
A hilltop settlement ringed by steep earth banks, deep ditches, and wooden fences to guard families and grain.
Ramparts[RAM-parts]
Steep defensive walls of heaped chalk and earth surrounding an Iron Age hillfort like giant waves.
Wattle and Daub[WOT-ul and DAWB]
Woven hazel sticks plastered with sticky clay, mud, and straw to build warm roundhouse walls.
Torc[TORK]
A heavy, twisted neck ring of gold or bronze worn by proud Celtic leaders and warriors.

Long before Roman boots marched into Dorset, Celtic builders crowned a high ridge with Maiden Castle hillfort. Digging with antler picks and iron shovels, workers heaped up triple chalk ramparts higher than houses!

Winding gates forced any raider to twist and turn below tall wooden watchtowers. High on the banks, guards kept piles of smooth beach pebbles ready for leather slings.

Inside those steep walls stood hundreds of roundhouses with clay wattle-and-daub walls and cone straw roofs. Clever builders left no chimney hole, since wind would blow red sparks into dry thatch! Instead, gentle smoke drifted up to cure meat in the rafters and chase bugs away. Down by the fire, children spun heavy quern stones to grind wheat into flour.

Celtic Britons were proud craftworkers, never rough cavemen. Smiths twisted pure gold wires into heavy neck torcs and set red glass into bronze shields. Meanwhile, wooden ships carried Cornish tin and wheat across the Channel, tempting greedy eyes in Rome!

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 How did Maiden Castle's maze-like gateways and pebble piles protect families living inside the hillfort?
2 Why was leaving NO chimney hole in a thatched roundhouse roof actually a clever piece of Iron Age engineering?
3 Why does this story say Celtic Britons were 'never rough cavemen'—and how did Britain's wealth catch Rome's eye?

Enquiry 2 True Historical Story

Hobnailed Boots and War Elephants at Camulodunum (AD 43)

The History Revision Hub (from Cassius Dio Book LX & Suetonius)
Historical Context: Mocked by proud senators in Rome, Emperor Claudius gambled everything in AD 43 on conquering island Britain with 40,000 legionaries and a secret weapon.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Emperor Claudius[EM-per-er KLAW-dee-us]
The Roman ruler who launched the AD 43 invasion of Britain to win military glory and rich mines.
Testudo[tes-TOO-doh]
The Roman 'tortoise' formation where legionaries locked curved shields overhead and in front against arrows.
Caratacus[ka-RAT-uh-kus]
The brave British tribal prince who fought the invading Roman legions at the River Medway in AD 43.
Camulodunum[kam-yoo-loh-DOO-num]
The powerful British capital at modern Colchester where Claudius accepted eleven kings' surrender.

In marble halls in Rome, proud men whispered nastily behind Emperor Claudius's back. Claudius walked with a limp, stammered when nervous, and had never led an army in war!

One bold prize could prove every critic wrong. Claudius planned to conquer rich Britain, where even great Julius Caesar had turned back! In AD 43, General Plautius sailed over with four legions of forty thousand troops.

Landing at Richborough in Kent, soldiers marched twenty miles a day in nailed sandals. At the River Medway, brave prince Caratacus fought back for two fierce days. Still, calm Roman troops locked curved shields into tortoise walls and pushed on to the Thames.

There the army waited until Claudius sailed in from Italy with a huge shock. Towering grey war elephants marched ashore, scaring Celtic chariot horses with loud trumpet blasts! After just sixteen days in Britain, Claudius rode proudly into Camulodunum, modern Colchester, as eleven kings bowed to Rome.

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 Why was Emperor Claudius so eager to win a famous battlefield victory in AD 43?
2 Why did General Plautius pause near the River Thames instead of marching straight into Camulodunum?
3 General Plautius and 40,000 soldiers did the hard marching and fighting, while Claudius stayed in Britain for just sixteen days—do you think Claudius really earned all the glory?

Enquiry 3 Eyewitness Voice Roman Historical Portrait

Roman History (Book LXII) & Annals (Book XIV) on AD 60–61

Cassius Dio & Tacitus (adapted for KS2 from Public Domain translations)
Historical Context: Because the Iceni tribe left no written books, everything we know about how Queen Boudicca looked and spoke comes from Roman historians Tacitus and Cassius Dio.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Boudicca[BOO-dik-uh]
The fearless Queen of the Iceni tribe who led a massive rebellion against Roman rule in AD 60–61.
Iceni[eye-SEE-nee]
The proud British Celtic tribe of East Anglia ruled by King Prasutagus and Queen Boudicca.
Tawny[TAW-nee]
A warm golden-brown or fiery orange-red colour—how Cassius Dio described Boudicca's long hair.
Londinium[lun-DIN-ee-um]
The busy Roman trading port that grew into modern London, burned to red clay by Boudicca's army.

Queen Boudicca of the Iceni stood tall and fierce before her gathered warriors! Her bright eyes flashed with anger, and her bold voice rang across the plain. Thick tawny-red hair fell right down to her hips.

Around her neck shone a heavy twisted torc of pure gold. Over a bright checked tunic, she pinned a thick woollen cloak with a bronze brooch. Gripping a tall spear in her fist, she filled every onlooker with awe.

Riding in a wooden war chariot beside her two daughters, Boudicca called each tribe to arms! My husband King Prasutagus stayed a true friend to Rome, she cried aloud. Dying, he left half his wealth to Emperor Nero so our home would stay safe.

Instead, greedy Roman men stole Iceni land, demanded cruel taxes, beat me with whips, and hurt my daughters! Today I fight not for gold, she shouted, but as an angry woman avenging lost freedom! Let us win together, or die free rather than live as Roman slaves!

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 Which vivid details in the story make Queen Boudicca look so unforgettable in front of her warriors?
2 What cruel Roman actions drove Queen Boudicca to call the tribes to fight?
3 Boudicca shouted, 'Today I fight not for gold, but as an angry woman avenging lost freedom!' Why do you think her speech made so many warriors ready to follow her?

Enquiry 4 Eyewitness Voice Eyewitness Wooden Tablet

Vindolanda Wooden Ink Tablets 291 & 346 (c. AD 100)

Claudia Severa & a Frontier Friend (translated from Latin, British Museum)
Historical Context: Just south of Hadrian's Wall at Vindolanda Fort, wet oxygen-free mud preserved hundreds of wafer-thin wooden ink postcards—including the oldest surviving Latin handwriting by a woman!
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Vindolanda[vin-doh-LAN-duh]
A Roman frontier fort in Northumberland where wet mud preserved hundreds of thin wooden ink letters.
Claudia Severa[KLAW-dee-uh seh-VAIR-uh]
A Roman commander's wife whose birthday note holds the oldest known Latin handwriting by a woman.
Sulpicia Lepidina[sul-PIK-ee-uh lep-ih-DEE-nuh]
Claudia Severa's close friend and wife of the fort commander Flavius Cerialis at Vindolanda.
Frontier[frun-TEER]
The guarded northern border zone of Roman Britain where Hadrian's Wall was built in AD 122.

Claudia Severa sends warm greetings to her dearest friend Lepidina! On 11 September, sister, for my birthday party, I warmly invite you to visit our fort. You will make my day truly happy by arriving safely!

Give kind greetings to your husband Cerialis. My husband Aelius and my little son send him their best wishes too.

At the bottom corner, Claudia took the reed pen into her own hand! There she wrote the oldest Latin words by a woman ever found. I shall expect you, sister! Farewell, my dearest soul, as I hope to prosper, and hail!

In wet mud nearby at Vindolanda lay a second wooden postcard for a chilly border guard. I have sent you warm woollen socks from Sattua! Inside your parcel are two pairs of strong sandals and two pairs of underpants. Greet your friends Elpis and Tetricus, with whom I pray you live happily!

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 Why did Claudia Severa take the reed pen into her own hand at the bottom of her wooden birthday invitation?
2 What does the second wooden postcard tell us about the weather for a border guard at Vindolanda?
3 How do the Vindolanda tablets change our picture of Roman forts along the northern frontier?

Enquiry 5 Eyewitness Voice Eyewitness Roman Letter

Moral Letters to Lucilius — Letter 56: On Quiet and Study (c. AD 64)

Seneca the Younger (adapted for KS2 from R. M. Gummere's 1917 translation)
Historical Context: Roman towns like Aquae Sulis (Bath) built grand steaming bathhouses where people exercised, gossiped, and washed—as poor philosopher Seneca discovered when he rented rooms right upstairs!
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Aquae Sulis[AK-wee SOO-lis]
The famous Roman spa town at modern Bath in Somerset, built around natural hot mineral springs.
Strigil[STRIJ-il]
A curved bronze scraper used by Roman bathers to scrape olive oil, sweat, and dirt off their skin.
Hypocaust[HY-poh-kawst]
Clever Roman underfloor heating where hot furnace air flowed beneath floors raised on brick pillars.
Seneca[SEN-ih-kuh]
A famous Roman writer and thinker who wrote a funny letter complaining about noisy public baths.

Pity me, my dear friend! My rented rooms sit right above a noisy Roman bathhouse! Just picture every loud racket that can make a quiet writer hate his own ears.

Down in the yard, strong men swing heavy lead weights and groan with effort. Every time they let out a breath, they hiss and wheeze! Next comes a loud slap as a helper rubs olive oil onto bare shoulders. Then he scrapes sweat and dirt off the skin with a curved bronze strigil.

Worst of all, a ball-player runs in and yells his score at top volume! Soon a noisy quarrel starts, and guards catch a thief stealing a bather's warm cloak. Nearby, a man who loves his own voice sings loudly against the wet stone walls!

Watch out too for swimmers who leap into the hot pool with a giant splash! Above all that din, snack-sellers shout out hot sausages, sweet honey cakes, and steaming pies!

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 List four noisy people or jobs Seneca could hear from the Roman bathhouse underneath his floorboards!
2 How did Romans clean their skin at the baths before modern bars of scented soap were common?
3 What does Seneca's funny complaint prove about why townsfolk in Roman Britain visited bathhouses like Aquae Sulis every day?

Enquiry 6 True Historical Story True Archaeological Story

The Chest in the Field: Burying the Hoxne Hoard (c. AD 410 & 1992)

The History Revision Hub (from British Museum Hoxne Hoard Records)
Historical Context: When Roman legions marched away by AD 410, anxious families buried chests of silver underground—leaving one lost hammer in 1992 to uncover Britain's greatest Roman treasure.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Emperor Honorius[EM-per-er hoh-NOR-ee-us]
The Western Roman Emperor who wrote in AD 410 warning British towns they must defend themselves.
Hoxne Hoard[HOKS-un HORD]
Britain's largest Roman gold and silver treasure chest, buried in Suffolk around AD 410 and found in 1992.
Visigoths[VIZ-ih-goths]
Fierce invading armies who attacked Italy itself, forcing Rome to recall legions from Britain.
Legacy[LEG-uh-see]
Lasting gifts left behind by the past—like straight roads, '-chester' towns, Latin letters, and our calendar.

Around AD 410, nearly four hundred years of Roman rule in Britain came to an end. Fierce armies attacked Italy, so Roman soldiers sailed away across the sea. Emperor Honorius wrote warning British towns that they must now guard themselves!

Without troops watching the shore, Picts and Saxon raiders struck closer every summer. At Hoxne in Suffolk, one rich family decided to hide their best silver underground.

With great care, they lined a locked oak chest with small boxes and soft cloth. Inside went nearly fifteen thousand gold and silver coins, plus seventy-eight named silver spoons! Beside gold bracelets sat a gleaming silver pepper pot shaped like a noble Roman lady. The family buried that chest deep in the earth, yet nobody ever returned.

Over fifteen hundred years passed by. In 1992, farmer Peter Whatling lost his hammer in a muddy Suffolk field! His friend Eric Lawes used a metal detector, heard a loud beep, and called diggers in to lift Britain's greatest Roman treasure!

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 Why did a rich family at Hoxne in Suffolk decide to bury their locked oak chest underground around AD 410?
2 What clues in the story show the Hoxne family packed their treasure chest with great care instead of throwing it in a hole in a panic?
3 The family buried their oak chest planning to come back one day, yet nobody ever returned—what do you think happened to them, and what Roman legacies did they leave behind in Britain?

GUIDE KEY
The Romans in Britain · Support for Families & Tutors

Parent, Carer & Tutor Conversation Guide

Model Talking Points
No Specialist History Knowledge Needed: Children never need word-for-word answers—praise curiosity and listen for these core ideas during your 3-minute chat!
Enquiry 1: High on Maiden Castle p. 2
Q1: Raiders had to twist slowly through steep earth banks while defenders high above hurled smooth beach pebbles from leather slings.
Q2: A chimney hole would pull wind through and blow sparks into dry straw, whereas gentle smoke drifting through thatch cured meat and kept insects out.
Q3: They built huge triple-walled hillforts, clever roundhouses, and twisted gold torcs, while ships carrying Cornish tin and wheat across the Channel tempted greedy eyes in Rome.
Enquiry 2: Claudius's War Elephants p. 3
Q1: Proud men in Rome whispered behind his back because he limped, stammered, and had never led an army, and conquering rich Britain would prove every critic wrong.
Q2: He waited so Emperor Claudius could sail over from Italy with towering war elephants and ride in proudly to claim the final victory himself!
Q3: Claudius planned the invasion and brought the shocking war elephants to scare Celtic horses, yet it was General Plautius's marching troops and shield walls that fought past Caratacus at the River Medway.
Enquiry 3: Portrait of Boudicca p. 4
Q1: She was tall with flashing eyes, a bold voice, thick tawny-red hair down to her hips, a heavy twisted gold torc, a bright checked tunic with a woollen cloak, and a tall spear.
Q2: Even though King Prasutagus left half his wealth to Emperor Nero to keep his family safe, greedy Roman men stole Iceni land, demanded cruel taxes, beat Boudicca with whips, and hurt her daughters.
Q3: She rode beside her two daughters showing she was fighting to protect her family and freedom after Roman cruelty, not just to win treasure.
Enquiry 4: Vindolanda Birthday Post p. 5
Q1: After the main letter was written, she took the reed pen herself to add a warm personal goodbye—'I shall expect you, sister! Farewell, my dearest soul!'—to show how much Lepidina meant to her.
Q2: It was cold and chilly up in Northumberland, so someone sent him a parcel packed with warm woollen socks, two pairs of strong sandals, and two pairs of underpants!
Q3: Forts were not lonely camps of silent soldiers—officers' wives, young children, and friends celebrated birthdays and sent parcels just like families today.
Enquiry 5: Living Above the Baths! p. 6
Q1: Groaning weightlifters, slapping oil-rubbers, a shouting ball-player, a caught cloak-thief, booming bath-singers, splashing jumpers, and yelling sausage- and cake-sellers!
Q2: They sweated in warm steam rooms heated by underfloor hypocausts, rubbed olive oil onto their skin, and scraped oil and dirt off with a curved bronze strigil.
Q3: A Roman bathhouse was far more than a bathroom—it was a lively leisure centre for sport, hot snacks, gossip, music, and meeting friends.
Enquiry 6: Secret of the Hoxne Hoard p. 7
Q1: Roman soldiers had sailed away to defend Italy and Emperor Honorius warned British towns to guard themselves, leaving families afraid of Pict and Saxon raiders.
Q2: They lined a locked oak chest with small boxes and soft cloth, neatly packing nearly fifteen thousand coins, seventy-eight named silver spoons, gold bracelets, and a silver lady pepper pot—and Eric Lawes wisely called diggers in so it was lifted carefully.
Q3: They may have fled from raiders or never lived to return, yet Roman Britain still left lasting gifts like straight roads, '-chester' towns, Latin letters, and our calendar.
Bonus 6-Story Quick Recall Check (Ask Aloud After Reading!) 18 Interactive Quiz Qs via Page QR Codes
E1: Why did an Iron Age thatched roundhouse have no chimney hole, and how was Maiden Castle defended? → Leaving no chimney stopped wind blowing sparks into dry straw while smoke cured meat, and Maiden Castle used steep chalk ramparts, maze gates, and slingshot pebbles.
E2: Why did Emperor Claudius invade Britain in AD 43, and what surprise animals marched with him into Camulodunum? → Claudius wanted military glory to silence critics who whispered behind his back (and wanted Britain's rich mines), marching into Camulodunum with towering war elephants.
E3: Why did Queen Boudicca lead the Iceni rebellion in AD 60–61, and what made her stand out in front of her warriors? → Greedy Romans stole Iceni land, demanded cruel taxes, whipped Boudicca, and hurt her daughters; she stood out with tawny-red hair to her hips, a heavy gold torc, a checked tunic, and a tall spear.
E4: Who wrote the famous 11 September birthday invitation found at Vindolanda, and what chilly-weather clothes were posted to a soldier nearby? → Claudia Severa wrote the birthday invitation to Sulpicia Lepidina, and another tablet listed woollen socks, two pairs of sandals, and two pairs of underpants.
E5: In Seneca's letter, what three activities besides washing happened inside a busy Roman bathhouse, and what tool scraped off olive oil? → People lifted weights, played ball games, sang, and bought hot sausages and cakes—and bathers scraped olive oil off with a curved bronze strigil.
E6: Why was the Hoxne Hoard buried around AD 410, and how was it accidentally rediscovered in Suffolk in 1992? → A wealthy family hid their silver coins, spoons, and lady pepper pot from raiders after Roman legions left in AD 410, and Eric Lawes found it in 1992 while searching for a lost farm hammer.
