Enquiry 1 Eyewitness Diary, Letter & Speech Roman Water Commissioner’s Account (c. AD 97)

De Aquaeductu Urbis Romae (Inspecting the Aqueducts of Rome)

Sextus Julius Frontinus (c. AD 97 • Roman Water Commissioner)
Historical Context: Sextus Julius Frontinus (c. AD 40–103) governed Roman Britain before Emperor Nerva appointed him Water Commissioner of Rome in AD 97. Walking every mile of stone channels, settling tanks, and pipes himself, Frontinus recorded with pride how Roman engineering kept cities clean.
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Aqueduct[AK-wuh-dukt]
A gravity-fed Roman stone channel—carried through rock tunnels and across arched bridges—bringing fresh spring water to towns.
Settling Tank[SET-ling TANK]
A deep stone basin along an aqueduct where mud, sand, and pebbles sank before clean water entered city pipes.
Cloaca Maxima[kloh-AH-kuh MAK-sih-muh]
Rome’s huge stone-vaulted main sewer that flushed bath waste and street dirt out into the River Tiber.
Miasma[my-AZ-muh]
The ancient and medieval belief that disease was spread by breathing foul-smelling "bad air" from swamps and rot.

"For more than four hundred years after Rome was founded, our citizens drank muddy water drawn from the River Tiber or local wells. Today, nine great stone aqueducts carry pure mountain spring water into our city. Driven solely by gentle gravity, the water flows through rock tunnels and across high arched bridges day and night."

"As Water Commissioner of Rome, I have walked and inspected every mile of these channels myself. Near the city walls, each aqueduct passes through deep stone settling tanks. There, pebbles and river mud sink to the bottom so the water enters our pipes clear and sweet."

"Inside Rome, public fountains supply rich senators and poor street-sellers alike. Great bathhouses steam with hot water, while army forts enjoy clean drinking supplies. Even the overflow water from the fountains is never wasted. Day and night, it rushes beneath the streets through the great Cloaca Maxima sewer, scouring away the filth and bad smells that once bred sickness."

"Look at all these practical structures serving millions of lives. Go ahead and compare them, if you dare, with the idle Pyramids of Egypt!"

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. Why does Frontinus boast that Rome’s stone aqueducts are far greater than the famous Pyramids of Egypt?
2. Even though Romans knew nothing about microscopic germs, how did fearing "bad air" (miasma) make their cities healthier?
3. What hidden health dangers still lurked inside Roman bathhouses and communal latrines?

Enquiry 2 True Historical Story True Historical Story (Medieval London, 1349)

Lanterns at Midnight: Medieval Gong-Fermors & King Edward III’s Fury

The History Revision Hub (Grounded in Corporation of London Letter-Books)
Historical Context: While wealthy medieval monasteries like Canterbury Priory enjoyed clean piped spring water behind high stone walls, crowded medieval towns relied on shallow wells and backyard cesspits. When the Black Death struck in 1349, King Edward III raged at the state of London’s streets.
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Gong-Fermor[GONG-fer-mer]
A medieval night-worker paid high wages to dig out deep backyard cesspits after dark and cart the waste away.
Shambles[SHAM-buhlz]
Medieval open-air street butcheries where animals were slaughtered and meat was sold from wooden stalls.
Cesspit[SESS-pit]
An underground pit beneath a privy where waste collected, often leaking through soil into nearby drinking wells.
Rakers[RAY-kerz]
Town street-cleaners hired by medieval councils to shovel dung and rubbish onto carts.

Behind the quiet stone walls of Canterbury Priory, medieval monks washed their hands at gleaming taps fed by clean spring pipes. Step outside into the narrow lanes of fourteenth-century London, however, and your boots sank into ankle-deep muck.

Nearly fifty thousand people lived packed above workshops with no Roman sewers to flush waste away. Householders tipped chamber-pots into open street gutters. At the Shambles meat market, butchers tossed animal guts onto rotting heaps. Beneath crowded backyards, leaky cesspits seeped slowly through damp soil into neighbours’ drinking wells.

Only after the nine o'clock curfew bell rang did the "gong-fermors" start work. Holding lanterns and wooden buckets, these tough night-men climbed down into foul cesspits. All night long, they hauled dripping carts outside the city walls.

When the Black Death hit London in 1349, King Edward III rode past heaps of street dung and lost his temper. Royal doctors warned him that foul "bad air" bred plague. The angry King sent a strict order to the Mayor of London. "Have all filth carted away without delay, upon pain of heavy fine!" he wrote. Town rakers shovelled hard, yet rat fleas kept spreading death unseen.

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. Who were the "gong-fermors," and why were they only allowed to work after the nine o'clock curfew bell?
2. Does King Edward III’s furious 1349 order show that medieval people loved dirt, or that town councils lacked Roman infrastructure?
3. Look at the c. 1165 Canterbury Priory map: why were monasteries so much cleaner than medieval London’s streets?

Red Crosses on Drury Lane: The Diary of Samuel Pepys in 1665

Samuel Pepys (June–September 1665 • Magdalene College, Cambridge)
Historical Context: By 1665, London’s population had swollen to nearly half a million people crammed into overhanging timber alleys without sewers. Navy official Samuel Pepys stayed working in London throughout the scorching summer of 1665 as the Great Plague killed almost 100,000 Londoners.
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Quarantine[KWOR-uhn-teen]
The strict 1665 Plague Order locking an infected household inside for forty days behind a painted red cross.
Bills of Mortality[BILZ uv mor-TAL-ih-tee]
Weekly printed parish lists recording how many Londoners had died of plague and other illnesses.
Watchman[WOCH-muhn]
A guard posted outside a red-cross plague house day and night to stop anyone escaping.
Tenements[TEN-uh-muhnts]
Crowded multi-storey wooden houses divided up among poor city families.

"7 June 1665: Today, much against my will, I walked down Drury Lane in fierce summer heat. On two or three doors I saw a bright red cross painted a foot high, with the words: 'Lord have mercy upon us.' Parish watchmen stood outside to keep the sick families locked indoors for forty days."

"It was the very first plague door I had ever seen, and my stomach turned with dread. Fearing I had breathed poisoned air, I hurried into a shop to buy strong roll-tobacco. Smelling and chewing the bitter leaf calmed my fear."

"16 August 1665: Lord! How sad a sight it is to see London’s streets almost empty. Two shops out of every three stand boarded up. Passers-by cross the street in terror whenever they spot a shut door."

"3 September 1665: Grass now grows between the stones in the royal courts at Whitehall. Nightly dead-carts rumble toward deep plague pits, and the weekly Bill of Mortality has passed six thousand dead. Coal fires burn at street corners for three days and nights to cleanse the air, yet still the plague rages through poor parishes."

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. What warning was painted onto infected doors in Drury Lane, and why did Samuel Pepys immediately chew strong tobacco?
2. Comparing 1665 with 1349, what had improved in city organisation, and what medical belief had NOT changed at all?
3. Why did the 1665 plague kill far more people in crowded poor parishes outside the city walls than among wealthy nobles?

"Filthy River, Filthy River": Punch Magazine, Faraday’s Card & The Great Stink

Punch Magazine ("Father Thames" Verse) & Michael Faraday (The Times, 1855)
Historical Context: As Victorian factories packed 2.5 million people into London and new flushing toilets emptied straight into old storm drains, the River Thames became a giant open sewer—while water companies still pumped drinking water out of it! Punch magazine and scientist Michael Faraday shamed Parliament as the crisis boiled toward the "Great Stink" of 1858.
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Laissez-Faire[less-ay-FAIR]
"Leave alone"—the early Victorian belief that government should keep taxes low and not interfere in town health.
Abominations[uh-bom-ih-NAY-shunz]
Disgusting, foul waste and pollution dumped into the river.
Cesspool[SESS-pool]
A stagnant pit of raw liquid sewage; Punch mocked the entire River Thames as one giant cesspool.
Opaque[oh-PAYK]
Cloudy and impossible to see through because of thick brown sewage sediment.

By the 1850s, new flushing toilets in London homes were pouring raw waste straight into old drains and out into the River Thames. Worse still, private water companies pumped drinking water from that very same river! Punch magazine mocked "Father Thames" in sharp verse.

"Filthy river, filthy river,
Foul from London to the Nore,
What art thou but one vast gutter,
One tremendous common shore?"
"All her foul abominations,
Into thee the City throws.
Thou, vile cesspool, art the liquor,
Whence is made the beer we drink!"

On 7 July 1855, scientist Michael Faraday rode a Thames steamboat between London Bridge and Hungerford Bridge. Tearing up white cards, he dropped pieces into the pale brown water at every pier. Before each white card sank even one inch below the surface, thick sewage hid it completely from sight! Faraday wrote to The Times warning that a hot summer would bring a dreadful lesson. Three summers later, the scorching heatwave of July 1858 baked the river mud right beside Westminster. Even lime-soaked curtains could not block "The Great Stink," forcing gasping MPs to fund new sewers in eighteen days.

1. Why does the Punch poem call the River Thames "one vast gutter" and "vile cesspool"?
2. Describe the simple visual experiment Professor Michael Faraday carried out from a Thames steamboat in July 1855.
3. Look at John Leech’s 1858 Punch cartoon on the cover: which three deadly "offspring" is Father Thames introducing to London?

Enquiry 5 True Historical Story True Historical Detective Story (Soho, 1854)

Beer, Nappies & the Pump Handle: Dr John Snow’s Soho Investigation

The History Revision Hub (Grounded in John Snow’s 1855 Report & Rev. Whitehead’s Inquiry)
Historical Context: When a sudden cholera outbreak killed more than 500 neighbours around Broad Street in Soho in ten days (August–September 1854), top Victorian doctors blamed foul-smelling air ("miasma"). Local physician Dr John Snow knocked on doors, mapped every death, and proved cholera lurked in contaminated drinking water.
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Epidemiology[ep-ih-dee-mee-OL-uh-jee]
The scientific detective work of mapping where and how diseases spread across a community.
Cholera[KOL-uh-ruh]
A deadly water-borne bacterial infection causing rapid dehydration, spread by sewage in drinking water.
Outlier[OWT-ly-er]
An unusual clue that breaks the normal pattern—like brewery workers surviving right beside the deadly pump.
Water-Borne[WAW-ter-born]
Spread by swallowing contaminated water rather than breathing foul-smelling air.

At the end of August 1854, terror gripped the crowded Soho lanes around Broad Street in London. Within ten days, over five hundred neighbours died agonising deaths from cholera. Yellow warning flags flapped in the breeze while terrified families piled furniture onto handcarts to flee. Almost every top doctor in Britain blamed "miasma"—bad air rising from street drains.

Quiet local doctor John Snow doubted that theory. Convinced that victims swallowed cholera in dirty water, Snow walked Soho’s cobbles with a notebook in hand. Drawing a black bar for every death on a street map, he uncovered a deadly bullseye clustering tightly around the Broad Street water pump.

Two striking outliers clinched Snow’s detective case. Right next to the pump stood the Lion Brewery, yet zero of its seventy workers had died. Why? They drank a daily allowance of brewery beer and never touched pump water! Nearby, a workhouse holding over five hundred inmates had its own private well and lost barely five residents. Digging later revealed a cracked brick cesspit at 40 Broad Street, where Frances Lewis had rinsed her sick baby’s nappies just three feet from the well! On 8 September, parish officials removed the pump handle.

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. If foul air ("miasma") drifting across Soho had really caused cholera, what SHOULD have happened to the 70 workers at the Lion Brewery?
2. How had cholera bacteria originally entered the Broad Street drinking well?
3. Even after parish officials took the handle off the Broad Street pump on 8 September 1854, why did the government’s Medical Board STILL reject Snow’s idea?

Enquiry 6 True Historical Story True Historical Story (1858–1875)

318 Million Bricks: Joseph Bazalgette & the Triumph Over Urban Disease

The History Revision Hub (Grounded in Metropolitan Board of Works Archives)
Historical Context: Provoked by the Great Stink of July 1858, Parliament hired Chief Engineer Joseph Bazalgette (1819–1891) to build 82 miles of giant brick intercepting sewers beneath London. Combined with Pasteur and Koch’s Germ Theory and the 1867 working-class vote, engineering and law finally conquered British cholera.
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Bazalgette[baz-uhl-JET]
Sir Joseph Bazalgette, the tireless Victorian civil engineer who built London’s 82 miles of brick intercepting sewers.
Intercepting Sewers[in-ter-SEP-ting SOO-erz]
Giant underground brick tunnels running east across London to catch waste before it reached the River Thames.
Portland Cement[PORT-luhnd suh-MENT]
Super-strong, water-resistant cement tested batch by batch by Bazalgette so his brick tunnels never crumbled.
Compulsory[kuhm-PUL-suh-ree]
Required by law for every town council—unlike the weak voluntary 1848 Public Health Act.

Inside Parliament in July 1858, heavy curtains soaked in lime could no longer block the stench rising off the baking River Thames. Gasping MPs passed a three-million-pound law in just eighteen days. They handed this huge task to Chief Engineer Joseph Bazalgette.

Tireless and bold, Bazalgette checked thousands of plans himself and tested every batch of strong Portland cement. Beneath London’s busy streets, teams of diggers built eighty-two miles of giant brick main sewers. Over a thousand miles of smaller street drains fed into them. Built with 318 million bricks, the tunnels caught London’s waste before it reached the river. Huge steam engines at Crossness—and coastal pumps like Eastney in Portsmouth—sent waste far out on the falling tide.

MPs had built Bazalgette’s sewers to stop "bad smells." Yet wherever the brick tunnels opened, water-borne cholera vanished! Soon lab science proved why. Louis Pasteur published Germ Theory in 1861, and Robert Koch found the cholera germ in 1883. Once town workers won the vote in 1867, Parliament passed the 1875 Public Health Act. Clean water and closed sewers became the law for every British town.

Step 3 • Talk Together (3 Discussion Questions) Parent / Tutor Guide: Page 8
1. Why does Bazalgette’s sewer network show that people can sometimes solve a crisis for the WRONG scientific reason?
2. Why did Bazalgette insist on using 318 million bricks bonded with tested Portland cement and extra-wide oval tunnels?
3. Why did it take science, engineering, AND working-class men winning the vote in 1867 to pass the compulsory 1875 Public Health Act?

Parent, Carer & Tutor Conversation Guide & Answer Key Water & Sanitation Through Time
No Specialist History Knowledge Needed: Pupils do not need word-for-word answers—praise curiosity and listen for these core historical ideas during your 3-minute chat!
Enquiry 1 (p. 2): Frontinus: Roman Aqueducts
Q1: Pyramids were giant tombs for dead kings, whereas aqueducts served living people every day—bringing clean drinking water, filling baths, and flushing sewers.
Q2: Wanting to banish foul smells led Roman engineers to pipe pure mountain water IN and flush waste OUT through stone drains.
Q3: Romans piped water through poisonous lead pipes, shared unchlorinated bathwater with sick bathers, and passed around shared latrine sponge-sticks.
Enquiry 2 (p. 3): Gong-Fermors & Filth
Q1: They were well-paid night-workers who emptied deep backyard cesspits by bucket and cart after dark so citizens did not have to endure the smell by day.
Q2: It proves medieval kings and councils hated filth, passed fines, and hired rakers—but without Roman taxes or underground sewers, growing towns overwhelmed hand-carts.
Q3: Monasteries had wealth, strict daily rules, literacy, and isolated land upstream, whereas crowded towns packed animals, workshops, cesspits, and wells together.
Enquiry 3 (p. 4): Pepys’s Plague Diary
Q1: A foot-high red cross and "Lord have mercy upon us" marked quarantined houses; Pepys smelled and chewed tobacco hoping to block out "bad air" (miasma).
Q2: Government organisation was much stricter—40-day quarantine, watchmen, and printed Bills of Mortality—yet doctors still blamed miasma and lit street coal fires.
Q3: King Charles II and rich courtiers fled to clean country estates, while poor families stayed trapped in rat-infested timber tenements.
Enquiry 4 (p. 5): Father Thames & Faraday
Q1: London dumped all its raw toilet and slaughterhouse waste into the tidal Thames while still drawing water for drinking and brewing from it.
Q2: He dropped torn white cards into the river at each pier; the sewage-choked water was so opaque the white cards vanished less than one inch down.
Q3: Diphtheria, Scrofula, and Cholera—warning MPs that river pollution bred killer urban diseases.
Enquiry 5 (p. 6): Dr Snow & Broad Street
Q1: They breathed the exact same Soho air all day right beside Broad Street, so many should have died—yet zero died because they drank beer instead of pump water!
Q2: At 40 Broad Street, Frances Lewis rinsed the nappies of her sick baby into a cracked brick cesspit just three feet away from the underground well.
Q3: Pasteur’s Germ Theory (1861) and Koch’s microscope proof (1883) did not exist yet, so Snow could prove WHERE cholera spread statistically, but could not show the germ.
Enquiry 6 (p. 7): Bazalgette’s Brick Sewers
Q1: MPs funded the £3 million sewers in 1858 to stop foul miasma smells at Westminster, yet physically separating sewage from drinking water ended cholera anyway!
Q2: Waterproof Portland cement stopped underground leaks, and building generously sized tunnels meant London’s Victorian sewers still work beneath the city today.
Q3: Pasteur and Koch proved germs caused disease, Bazalgette proved sewers worked, and new working-class voters forced politicians to make clean water compulsory.
