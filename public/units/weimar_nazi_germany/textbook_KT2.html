Key Historical Actors & Essential Concepts (1919–1933)

1919–1933
KEY HISTORICAL INDIVIDUALS & ACTORS
Adolf Hitler (1889–1945) Leader of the NSDAP

Charismatic orator whose courtroom trial (1924), Mein Kampf ideology, and legal ballot strategy positioned the NSDAP to exploit the Depression.

Ernst Röhm (1887–1934) Chief of Staff of the SA

Built the brown-shirted Sturmabteilung to 400,000 men by 1932 (and 3 million by 1934), battling communist Red Front Fighters.

Joseph Goebbels (1897–1945) Berlin Gauleiter & Propaganda Chief

Masterminded modern electioneering—mass torchlight rallies, posters, radio, and tailored slogans for farmers, business, and youth.

Heinrich Brüning (1885–1970) Centre Party Chancellor (1930–32)

Nicknamed the "Hunger Chancellor" for deflationary benefit cuts and reliance on Hindenburg’s Article 48 emergency decrees.

Franz von Papen (1879–1969) Conservative Chancellor & Vice-Chancellor

Aristocrat with virtually no Reichstag support ("Cabinet of Barons") who engineered the January 1933 deal thinking he could "tame" Hitler.

Kurt von Schleicher (1882–1934) Army General & Last Weimar Chancellor

Tried and failed to split the NSDAP via Gregor Strasser in Dec 1932 before resigning on 28 January 1933.

ESSENTIAL DISCIPLINARY CONCEPTS & TERMINOLOGY
25-Point Programme (1920) Founding NSDAP manifesto demanding abolition of Versailles, Lebensraum, strong central rule, and stripping Jews of citizenship.
Führerprinzip ("Leader Principle") Total personal authority of Hitler over party policy and regional Gauleiters, confirmed at Bamberg (1926).
Mein Kampf ("My Struggle", 1925) Hitler’s Landsberg prison book setting out Aryan racial hierarchy, antisemitism, anti-Bolshevism, and eastern Lebensraum.
SS (Schutzstaffel, 1925) Black-uniformed personal bodyguard led from 1929 by Heinrich Himmler, fiercely loyal to Hitler rather than Röhm.
KPD (German Communist Party) Grew to 100 Reichstag seats in Nov 1932, terrifying industrialists (Thyssen, Krupp) and middle-class voters into backing Hitler.
Mittelstand Shopkeepers, artisans, civil servants, and white-collar clerks squeezed between big department stores and trade unions.
Article 48 Decrees Rose from 5 in 1930 to 66 in 1932 while Reichstag sitting days collapsed from 94 to 13, destroying parliamentary habits.
Backstairs Intrigue (1932–33) Secret elite manoeuvring among Hindenburg, Oskar von Hindenburg, Papen, and Schleicher that handed Hitler the Chancellorship.

KEY ENQUIRY “How did Hitler turn a tiny Munich fringe group into the violent Nazi Party?”
STAGE 1 The Furrier's Son in the Sterneckerbräu: Anton Drexler and the German Workers’ Party Sep 1919
“How did military surveillance lead directly to party membership?”

In September 1919, army intelligence agent Adolf Hitler was ordered to watch fringe political groups in Munich in Bavaria. Visiting small Munich beer-cellar meetings at the Sterneckerbräu, he checked on the tiny DAP (German Workers' Party) founded by railway locksmith Anton Drexler. At the time, the group had just fifty members and little money. When a speaker urged Bavaria to break away from Germany, Hitler jumped up and gave a fiery nationalist reply. Impressed, Anton Drexler invited him to join in 1919 as committee member number 555; Hitler soon took charge of propaganda and turned small beer-hall talks into packed rallies.

STAGE 2 The Twenty-Five Point Programme: Anti-Versailles Grievances and Populist Rhetoric Feb 1920
“How did the 25-Point Programme combine nationalist and socialist appeals?”

On 24 February 1920, before two thousand listeners inside Munich’s Hofbräuhaus, Hitler and Anton Drexler unveiled the Twenty-Five Point Programme (25-Point Programme). Combining fierce nationalism with populist anti-capitalist pledges, the manifesto demanded the abolition of the hated Treaty of Versailles, the union of all Germans into a Greater Germany, old-age pensions, and state profit-sharing in giant industrial trusts. Above all, Point 4 placed racial anti-Semitism at the heart of party doctrine. It declared that no person of Jewish blood could be a member of the German nation or hold German citizenship, and demanded non-citizens be expelled during food shortages. Blending anti-Versailles fury, worker welfare promises, and anti-Jewish scapegoating gave the early Nazi Party its broad appeal.

The Early Development of the Nazi Party — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 The Hooked Cross: Rebranding the NSDAP, the Völkischer Beobachter and the Swastika Standard 1920–1921
“Why was visual branding crucial for attracting working-class recruits?”

In summer 1920, Hitler rebranded the German Workers' Party (DAP) as the NSDAP (National Socialist German Workers' Party) to attract both nationalists and workers. Knowing the pull of bold visual theatre, he designed a red, white, and black banner carrying the crooked black swastika emblem. In December 1920, wealthy nationalist donors helped buy the Munich newspaper Völkischer Beobachter, giving Hitler a print voice for anti-Weimar propaganda and racial conspiracy theories. Backed by uniformed insignia, party newspapers, and beer-hall rallies drawing thousands of paying spectators, Hitler became the party’s dominant public figure.

STAGE 4 The Autocratic Dictator: Hitler Seizes the Führerprinzip and Ousts Drexler Jul 1921
“How did personal oratory secure complete dominance over party rivals?”

By July 1921, sharp rivalries broke out between Hitler and the founding committee when Drexler sought a merger with rival nationalist groups. Hitler issued a dramatic resignation ultimatum, knowing the party faced ruin without its star speaker. Committee leaders gave in and voted to grant Hitler sole power. Founder Anton Drexler was pushed into a powerless honorary role as Hitler enforced the Führerprinzip ("Leadership Principle") across the party. Democratic voting ended; every local branch and party official now owed unquestioned personal obedience to Hitler's commands from above.

STAGE 5 The Brownshirt Brawlers: Ernst Röhm and the Formation of the Sturmabteilung Aug 1921
“What role did organized paramilitary violence play in protecting meetings?”

As rallies of the National Socialist German Workers' Party (NSDAP) grew, meetings faced fierce heckling from communists and socialists. In August 1921, Hitler organised the Sturmabteilung (SA or Brownshirts) from jobless ex-soldiers, brawlers, and Freikorps veterans wearing brown surplus army uniforms. Led by Captain Ernst Röhm, the Brownshirts gained hidden army weapons, guarded Nazi speakers, attacked left-wing offices, and beat up communist newspaper sellers across Munich. By 1922, over six thousand armed SA men gave Hitler a violent private army dominating Bavaria's streets.

KEY ENQUIRY “How did Hitler turn the failed 1923 Munich Putsch into a new path to power?”
STAGE 1 Pistol Shots in the Bürgerbräukeller: Von Kahr Held at Gunpoint in the Munich Putsch 8 Nov 1923
“Why did Hitler’s November 1923 Munich Beer Hall coup collapse?”

In November 1923, amid national fury over the Ruhr occupation and hyperinflation, Hitler plotted to copy Mussolini’s 1922 March on Rome by launching a coup in Munich. To win military backing, he enlisted First World War hero General Erich Ludendorff to lead a march on Berlin. On 8 November 1923, Hitler and 600 armed SA men stormed the Bürgerbräukeller Beer Hall. Firing a pistol into the ceiling, Hitler forced Bavarian leader Gustav von Kahr at gunpoint to back his revolt. Next day, armed police gunfire blocked the Nazi march in Munich, leaving 16 Nazis dead and crushing the coup.

STAGE 2 Bloodshed at the Feldherrnhalle: Bavarian Police Fire and the Collapse of the Coup 9 Nov 1923
“How did Hitler turn his 1924 Munich treason trial into propaganda?”

Put on trial for High Treason in Munich in February 1924, Hitler turned the courtroom into a national propaganda platform. Right-wing Weimar judges sympathised with his nationalist, anti-Weimar views. They let him attack Weimar democracy at length and defend his coup as patriotic duty before reporters. Front-page press coverage turned Hitler from a Bavarian agitator into a national right-wing martyr. Although treason normally carried life in prison, biased judges gave Hitler a lenient five-year sentence. Eligible for early parole, he walked free after barely nine months in comfortable rooms at Landsberg.

The Munich Putsch and the Lean Years — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 A Treason Trial as a Stage: Sympathetic Judges, Landsberg Fortress and Mein Kampf 1924
“What core ideas and strategy did Hitler set out in Landsberg Fortress?”

Held in spacious quarters during his Landsberg Fortress detention, Hitler welcomed party visitors and dictated Mein Kampf ('My Struggle') to deputy Rudolf Hess. The book set out his core beliefs: Aryan racial supremacy, fierce hatred of Jews and communism, and total obedience under the Führerprinzip. Hitler also demanded Lebensraum ("living space") conquered across Eastern Europe and Russia. Crucially, the failed putsch taught him to abandon armed revolt. Instead, he focused on pursuing power through legal constitutional elections rather than armed rebellion, winning votes to dismantle Weimar democracy from within.

STAGE 4 The Bamberg Conference: Crushing Strasser’s Left Wing and Pledging the Legal Path to Power 1925–1926
“Why did the Führer demand absolute obedience and legal election tactics?”

Freed from Landsberg Fortress in December 1924, Hitler persuaded Bavarian authorities to lift the party ban and re-founded the NSDAP in February 1925 on a legal electoral path. Launching nationwide Party reorganisation from Munich headquarters, he built specialist branches for women, students, teachers, and youth alongside his elite black-shirted Schutzstaffel (SS) personal bodyguard sworn directly to him. To crush an ideological split between southern nationalists and northern socialist-leaning Nazis who wanted land and factory seizures, Hitler summoned regional leaders to the February 1926 Bamberg Conference. Speaking for almost five hours, he enforced the Führerprinzip, defeated Gregor Strasser, won Joseph Goebbels over as Berlin Gauleiter, and locked every regional branch into unquestioning personal obedience.

STAGE 5 The Electoral Wilderness: 2.6% of the Reichstag Vote in the Golden Twenties May 1928
“Why did Stresemann-era recovery reduce the Nazis to 2.6% in May 1928?”

Between 1924 and 1929, Stresemann's economic stabilization and American Dawes Plan loans brought Weimar prosperity, starving extremists of votes. In the May 1928 Reichstag election, the Nazis won barely 800,000 votes — just 2.6% of the vote and 12 seats — staying a tiny fringe party behind moderate democrats. Even so, these "lean years" built a strong national party structure. Hitler divided Germany into thirty-five electoral regions, each run by a loyal Gauleiter, and grew membership past 100,000 disciplined activists. When the Wall Street Crash struck in October 1929, the party stood ready to exploit national crisis.

KEY ENQUIRY “Why did the Great Depression turn the Nazis into Germany’s largest party by 1932?”
STAGE 1 Black Thursday in New York: The Wall Street Crash and the Recall of American Loans Oct 1929
“How did the American stock crash shatter Germany’s banking system?”

On 29 October 1929, the Wall Street Crash in New York wrecked international financial markets and hit Germany harder than any other industrial nation. Because Weimar recovery rested on short-term American credit under the Dawes Plan, anxious US banks recalled their loans at once, freezing credit and trade. Recalled US loans wiped out German capital reserves and triggered a banking collapse. Major banks shut down, over 50,000 businesses went bankrupt, and factory output dropped by forty percent. Stresemann’s warning that Germany was "dancing on a volcano" came true as confidence in Weimar rule collapsed.

STAGE 2 The 'Hunger Chancellor': Heinrich Brüning, Article 48 and Six Million Jobless 1930–1932
“Why did Brüning’s austerity policies deepen economic misery?”

By winter 1932, mass unemployment left six million registered unemployed workers (6 million jobless) without pay. Desperate crowds queued for hours outside municipal labour exchanges and soup kitchens while homelessness soared across industrial cities. When the Grand Coalition split over dole pay in March 1930, parliamentary rule broke down. President Hindenburg appointed Centre Party leader Heinrich Brüning, whose deflationary cuts to wages, pensions, and dole pay earned him the nickname "Hunger Chancellor". When MPs rejected his austerity cuts, Brüning dissolved parliament and governed through Article 48 emergency presidential decrees, sidelining Reichstag consent.

The Growth of Nazi Support — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 Street Warfare and the Red Threat: The KPD Surge and Middle-Class Panic 1930–1932
“How did fear of a Communist revolution drive middle-class voters toward the Nazis?”

As misery deepened, German politics polarised sharply between extreme left and right. Millions of jobless workers backed the Communist Party (KPD), which passed 100 Reichstag seats. Daily street brawls between communist fighters and Nazi stormtroopers terrified property owners and sparked the terror of a violent communist revolution across middle-class Germany. Alongside the 'Hitler over Germany' campaign, Hitler appealed to frightened Mittelstand shopkeepers and farmers by promising protection against big department stores and Marxist seizure. Fearing a Bolshevik rising too, wealthy industrialists like steel magnate Fritz Thyssen poured substantial funds into Nazi coffers.

STAGE 4 Propaganda by Air and Loudspeaker: Joseph Goebbels Packages the Hitler Cult 1930–1932
“How did Goebbels’ posters, radio, and flights project Nazi momentum?”

Appointed Reich propaganda chief in 1930 while Heinrich Brüning offered only cuts, Joseph Goebbels transformed modern election campaigning. He flooded towns with striking posters, loudspeaker vans, and simple slogans promising jobless workers 'Work and Bread' while appealing directly to hurt national pride. Embracing modern aviation in his "Hitler over Germany" flights, Goebbels chartered planes so Hitler could speak in four or five cities in a single day. Backed by radio campaigns and mass rallies, the Nazi movement projected youthful energy and unstoppable momentum, presenting Hitler as Germany's saviour.

STAGE 5 The Electoral Breakthrough: 230 Seats and the Largest Party in the Reichstag Jul 1932
“Why did the Nazis become the largest party yet remain excluded from power?”

Public anger at the 'Hunger Chancellor' lifted Nazi strength from 107 seats in 1930 to a huge electoral breakthrough in July 1932. Capturing 13.4 million votes (37.3%) and 230 Reichstag seats, the Nazis more than doubled their tally and became the largest party in parliament. When Hitler demanded the Chancellorship, Hindenburg refused, privately dismissing him as a "vulgar corporal". Yet because Nazis and Communists held a combined anti-democratic majority, no workable democratic coalition could govern, leaving Weimar deadlocked and exposed to backroom conservative intrigue.

KEY ENQUIRY “Why did Germany’s conservative elites hand Hitler the Chancellorship in January 1933?”
STAGE 1 Cracks in the Brown Front: Strasser's Mutiny, Lost Seats and Party Bankruptcy Nov 1932
“Why did the November 1932 election suggest Nazi momentum was fading?”

Parliamentary rule unravelled when President Hindenburg sacked Brüning in May 1932 after endless Article 48 decrees failed to end the slump. Power shifted away from elected MPs to Hindenburg and his conservative palace circle. Although the Nazis won 230 seats in July, fresh elections in November 1932 dealt Hitler a sharp setback. In November 1932, the Nazi Party lost two million votes and thirty-four seats as voters tired of street violence. Drained of campaign cash, the party faced internal crisis in December 1932 when Gregor Strasser urged joining a coalition before Nazi support faded altogether.

STAGE 2 The Failed Political General: Kurt von Schleicher’s Divisive Chancellor Gamble Dec 1932
“Why did Schleicher’s policies alienate powerful conservative barons?”

In June 1932, Hindenburg appointed conservative nobleman Franz von Papen Chancellor of an unelected "Cabinet of Barons" with almost zero Reichstag support. Hoping to win Nazi backing, Papen lifted the ban on the SA and deposed Prussia’s elected socialist state government. Opposed by ninety percent of MPs after November's vote, Papen resigned in December 1932 (soon plotting return as Vice-Chancellor) after General Kurt von Schleicher warned the army could not stop civil war. Yet Schleicher failed to split the Nazis and outraged Junker landowners by proposing to break up bankrupt Prussian estates.

Hitler’s Appointment as Chancellor — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 A Secret Meeting at Banker Schröder's Villa: Franz von Papen Plots His Revenge Jan 1933
“How did Papen’s personal rivalry with Schleicher lead to political collusion?”

Appointed Chancellor on 2 December 1932, Kurt von Schleicher tried to build a broad coalition by offering the Vice-Chancellorship to Gregor Strasser. Hitler swiftly forced Strasser to resign and reasserted strict party discipline, leaving Schleicher isolated without a Reichstag majority. Schleicher also lost Hindenburg's trust when Prussian landowners attacked his land plans for jobless workers. Seeking revenge through backstairs intrigue, Franz von Papen met Hitler secretly on 4 January 1933 at banker Kurt von Schröder’s Cologne villa. There Papen offered to make Hitler Chancellor with himself as Vice-Chancellor.

STAGE 4 The Backstairs Conspiracy: Tricking President Hindenburg into Squeezing Hitler into a Corner Jan 1933
“Why did conservative elites believe they could control and manipulate Hitler?”

Having failed earlier with the 'Cabinet of Barons', Franz von Papen worked behind closed doors in the presidential palace. Teaming up with Hindenburg’s son Oskar and Otto Meissner before 30 January 1933, he argued a Hitler-Papen cabinet was the only way to avoid civil war. Papen calmed Hindenburg’s doubts by stressing that only three of eleven cabinet ministers were Nazis while conservative ministers held eight posts. Boasting that within two months they would push Hitler into a corner until he squeaked, conservative elites fatally misjudged him.

STAGE 5 Torches Through the Brandenburg Gate: Adolf Hitler Appointed Chancellor 30 Jan 1933
“How did the appointment mark both legal transition and triumphant celebration?”

Backed by a formal petition urging him to appoint Hitler as Chancellor from leading bankers and industrialists, Hindenburg gave way. On 30 January 1933, Hitler was sworn in at the Berlin Chancellery. He was appointed legally under the rules of the Weimar Constitution by Conservative elites who thought they could control him. That evening, 25,000 uniformed stormtroopers marched in torchlight parades through the Brandenburg Gate while cheering crowds watched Hitler at the Chancellery window. From Munich, General Ludendorff sent Hindenburg a grim telegram warning that this "accursed man" would cast Germany into national ruin.
