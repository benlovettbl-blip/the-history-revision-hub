2
Explain why the Weimar Republic faced significant political instability in the years 1919–23. (12)
You may use the following in your answer:
  • The Treaty of Versailles
  • The Kapp Putsch
You must also use information of your own.

First, widespread fury over the June 1919 Treaty of Versailles permanently undermined the legitimacy of the new Weimar democracy. Condemned across Germany as a harsh Diktat, the treaty imposed Article 231 (“War Guilt”), £6.6 billion in reparations, and severe territorial losses. Right-wing nationalists exploited this humiliation through the Dolchstosslegende (“stab-in-the-back” myth), claiming the undefeated German army had been betrayed by the democratic “November Criminals” who signed the Armistice, turning millions against the Republic.

Second, armed uprisings from both Left and Right exposed the Republic’s lack of loyal military authority. After relying on anti-democratic Freikorps paramilitaries to crush the Communist Spartacist Uprising in January 1919, the government faced revolt when it tried to disband those brigades under Versailles limits. In March 1920, Wolfgang Kapp and 5,000 Freikorps seized Berlin in the Kapp Putsch; the regular army (Reichswehr) refused to fire on them, forcing ministers to flee and rely on a Berlin workers’ general strike to survive.

Finally, sustained right-wing political terrorism and the 1923 crises kept Weimar authority dangerously fragile. Between 1919 and 1922, extremist groups such as Organisation Consul committed 376 political murders—assassinating Armistice signatory Matthias Erzberger in 1921 and Foreign Minister Walther Rathenau in 1922—while sympathetic conservative judges handed down lenient sentences, culminating in Hitler’s Munich Putsch during the catastrophic 1923 hyperinflation crisis and demonstrating how deeply armed paramilitaries challenged democratic rule.

3
Explain why the German economy recovered in the years 1924–29 (Additional Revision Question). (12)
You may use the following in your answer:
  • Gustav Stresemann
  • The Rentenmark
You must also use information of your own.

First, Gustav Stresemann’s decisive currency reform halted the 1923 hyperinflation crisis and restored domestic confidence. Appointed Chancellor in August 1923, Stresemann called off passive resistance in the occupied Ruhr and introduced a strictly limited temporary currency, the Rentenmark, in November 1923, backed by German industrial and agricultural land. Once prices stabilised, he established the independent Reichsbank and introduced the permanent gold-backed Reichsmark in August 1924, providing a secure monetary foundation for trade and savings.

Second, the August 1924 Dawes Plan restructured crushing reparations and injected vital American capital into German industry. Negotiated with US banker Charles Dawes, the agreement lowered annual reparations instalments to affordable levels, secured French troop withdrawal from the Ruhr by 1925, and provided an 800-million-gold-mark American loan. Over the next five years, nearly $3 billion in US loans modernised German coal mines, chemical plants, and factories, pushing industrial production past pre-war 1913 levels by 1928.

Finally, Stresemann’s diplomatic achievements strengthened international trust and eased long-term financial burdens on the German budget. Signing the 1925 Locarno Pact to guarantee western borders and joining the League of Nations in 1926 rehabilitated Germany among Western powers, encouraging further foreign investment and enabling the 1929 Young Plan to cut total reparations to £2 billion payable over 59 years, even though reliance on short-term US credit left Germany “dancing on a volcano”.

SECTION B

Enquiry: The persecution of disabled people and minorities in Nazi Germany in the years 1933–39

Source B: From a secret report written by a local public health official in Saxony, December 1935.
“The implementation of the Law for the Prevention of Hereditarily Diseased Offspring is proceeding smoothly, though we must confront lingering traditional prejudices. In this district, we have processed over eighty applications for compulsory sterilisation this year alone. While some families initially protest out of ignorance, the majority are brought to understand that eliminating hereditary defects is a biological duty to the state. The local courts have been highly cooperative, accepting medical recommendations without delay. However, we require more secure facilities to prevent patients from fleeing before the surgical procedures are completed.”

Source C: An official Nazi propaganda poster published in Neues Volk by the Office of Racial Policy in 1938. The German text translates to: “60,000 Reichsmarks is what this person suffering from a hereditary defect costs the People’s community during his lifetime. Fellow citizen, that is your money too.”

Interpretation 1: From The Nazi State and Society by Dr Julian Kroll, published in 1998.
“The persecution of minorities and disabled people under the Nazi regime was fundamentally driven by a deeply ingrained biological and racial ideology. From the moment they seized power, the Nazis utilized the legal system and public health networks to implement eugenics policies. The German medical profession was highly complicit, willingly submitting thousands of patients for compulsory sterilisation. The general public largely conformed to these measures because they had been thoroughly indoctrinated by racial hygiene propaganda in schools and media. This pseudoscience convinced ordinary citizens that sterilising the disabled was essential to protect the purity of the Aryan gene pool.”

Interpretation 2: From The Economics of Terror by Professor Arthur Kitson, published in 2004.
“While racial rhetoric was always present, the driving force behind the escalating persecution of disabled people in the 1930s was economic utility. Following the Great Depression, the Nazi state sought to justify its welfare cuts by portraying the vulnerable as a parasitic financial burden on the hard-working taxpayer. Propaganda deliberately targeted the cost of care to build resentment among workers who were themselves enduring long hours and frozen wages. Active resistance to these policies was minimal because the police state swiftly silenced dissent and kept compulsory procedures behind closed doors.”

3(a) Exemplar Answer (8 marks):
Source B is highly useful for investigating how Nazi sterilisation policies operated locally between 1933 and 1939. Its content reveals systematic enforcement of the Law for the Prevention of Hereditarily Diseased Offspring, recording over eighty compulsory sterilisations in a single Saxony district and close cooperation from Hereditary Health Courts “accepting medical recommendations without delay”. Its provenance—a confidential December 1935 report by a Saxony public health official—enhances its value because internal bureaucratic reporting candidly admits family protests and patients attempting to flee, requiring “secure facilities”. Contextually, after the Sterilisation Law was passed in July 1933, between 300,000 and 400,000 people with conditions such as hereditary deafness, depression, or epilepsy were forcibly sterilised by 1939.

Source C is equally useful for showing how Nazi propaganda used economic scapegoating to build public consent for persecuting disabled people. Published in Neues Volk in 1938, the poster contrasts a healthy standing figure with a seated disabled man and claims a person with a hereditary condition “costs the People’s community 60,000 Reichsmarks”, telling taxpayers “that is your money too”. Originating from the NSDAP Office of Racial Policy, its purpose was to dehumanise disabled citizens as “useless eaters” draining resources needed for rearmament under the Four-Year Plan, reinforcing mathematics textbook exercises taught across German schools in the late 1930s.

3(b) Exemplar Answer (4 marks):
The main difference is that Interpretation 1 argues persecution was driven primarily by racial and biological eugenics ideology widely accepted by an indoctrinated medical profession and public seeking to protect “the purity of the Aryan gene pool”. By contrast, Interpretation 2 argues persecution was driven mainly by economic utility, portraying disabled citizens as a financial burden to justify post-Depression welfare cuts while relying on police-state intimidation to prevent open dissent.

3(c) Exemplar Answer (4 marks):
The interpretations differ because the historians place weight on different types of contemporary evidence. Interpretation 1 gives weight to administrative and medical evidence like Source B, which frames compulsory sterilisation as a pseudo-scientific “biological duty” against “hereditary defects” carried out willingly by doctors and courts. Conversely, Interpretation 2 emphasises state propaganda sources like Source C, which explicitly calculate care in Reichsmarks to stir taxpayer resentment against unproductive citizens.

3(d) Exemplar Answer (16 + 4 SPaG marks):
Interpretation 2 offers a persuasive explanation that economic utility and financial scapegoating strongly shaped Nazi persecution of disabled people between 1933 and 1939. Recovering from the Great Depression and prioritising rearmament under Göring’s 1936 Four-Year Plan, the regime slashed state asylum budgets and branded vulnerable patients “useless eaters”. Source C directly supports Kitson’s view by warning workers that one hereditarily ill citizen cost the Volksgemeinschaft 60,000 Reichsmarks. Under Robert Ley’s German Labour Front (DAF), where strikes were banned and wages frozen while working hours rose, encouraging taxpayers to resent welfare spending helped manufacture outward conformity.

However, Interpretation 2 understates the foundational role of racial hygiene ideology highlighted in Interpretation 1. Eugenics formed the core of Hitler’s worldview in Mein Kampf, not a mere excuse for budget cuts. Passed in July 1933—within months of Hitler taking power—the Law for the Prevention of Hereditarily Diseased Offspring established Hereditary Health Courts that forcibly sterilised between 300,000 and 400,000 men and women by 1939. As Source B confirms, doctors and judges eagerly enforced “biological duty”, while Bernhard Rust’s Nazified school curriculum made Race Studies compulsory.

Furthermore, pre-war persecution across 1933–39 proves racial purity and police terror outweighed pure financial calculation. Dissent was deterred by the Gestapo and concentration camps, where between 1936 and 1938 the regime interned thousands of Roma and Sinti, homosexual men prosecuted under revised Paragraph 175, and so-called “asocials” (vagrants, alcoholics, and work-shy citizens) in camps such as Sachsenhausen and Buchenwald. Simultaneously, ideological hatred drove the 1935 Nuremberg Laws stripping German Jews of citizenship and culminated in the November 1938 Kristallnacht pogrom, after which Jews were fined 1 billion Reichsmarks and barred from German economic life—destroying productive businesses solely to purify the racial state.

In conclusion, I partially agree with Interpretation 2. Economic propaganda blaming disabled citizens for draining taxpayers’ money was a cynical weapon used to win public consent and silence sympathy, alongside Gestapo intimidation. Ultimately, however, Interpretation 1 identifies the deeper root cause: biological racial ideology drove Nazi persecution of disabled people and minorities throughout 1933–39, with economic arguments serving an essentially ideological crusade.
