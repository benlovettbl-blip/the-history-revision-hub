WALK-IN LESSON PLANS • ENQUIRIES 1 & 2 NON-SPECIALIST PRIMARY TEACHER GUIDE
Enquiry 1: Ice Age Hunters & Sunken Doggerland (c. 800,000–4000 BC) Textbook p.4 • Workbook pp.3–4
Key Enquiry: How did Stone Age hunters survive—and how did Britain become an island?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This opening Key Stage 2 enquiry introduces prehistory across the Palaeolithic (Old Stone Age, c. 850,000–10,000 BC) and Mesolithic (Middle Stone Age, c. 10,000–4000 BC). Primary pupils explore the Happisburgh footprints, Creswell Crags cave shelters, flint knapping, and woolly mammoth hunting before tracing post-glacial woodland life through Cheddar Man at Gough’s Cave, Star Carr’s carved red-deer antler headdresses, and the c. 6500 BC flooding of Doggerland.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain how Palaeolithic hunter-gatherers survived the Ice Age using cave shelters and chipped flint handaxes.
  • Infer how warming forests changed Mesolithic life using clues from Cheddar Man and Star Carr.
  • Describe what Doggerland was and explain why Britain became an island around 6500 BC.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Between the icy Palaeolithic and the warmer Mesolithic, Stone Age life in Britain changed hugely. In the freezing Old Stone Age, early humans left footprints at Happisburgh, sheltered inside caves like Creswell Crags, chipped flint handaxes, and hunted giant woolly mammoths. Once the ice melted around 10,000 BC, green Mesolithic forests spread across Britain, where seasonal hunter-gatherers like Cheddar Man and Star Carr families hunted red deer, gathered hazelnuts, and fished lakes. Finally, rising seas drowned Doggerland around 6500 BC, turning Britain into an island!”
Enquiry 2: The First Farmers & Skara Brae (c. 4000–2500 BC) Textbook p.5 • Workbook pp.5–6
Key Enquiry: How did farming and stone villages change Stone Age life?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This second Key Stage 2 enquiry investigates the Neolithic revolution in Britain (c. 4000–2500 BC). Primary pupils contrast mobile Mesolithic hunter-gatherers with settled Neolithic farmers who cleared woodland using ground-and-polished stone axes, raised sheep, pigs, and cattle, harvested wheat and barley with flint sickles, and coiled clay pottery. Pupils then explore Skara Brae in Orkney (uncovered by the 1850 storm) and communal ancestor burials inside West Kennet Long Barrow.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain how farming, polished stone axes, and livestock allowed Neolithic families to settle in permanent villages.
  • Use archaeological clues from Skara Brae in Orkney to describe inside a Neolithic home.
  • Explain why Neolithic communities built giant stone-chambered tombs like West Kennet Long Barrow.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Historians view the switch to Neolithic farming around 4000 BC as a giant turning point because people began reshaping Britain’s landscape instead of just following wild herds. Using polished stone axes and flint sickles, farmers cleared forests to raise sheep and cattle and harvest wheat. Permanent food supplies let communities build snug stone homes with beds and dressers like Skara Brae, craft clay pots, and unite to raise monumental ancestor tombs like West Kennet Long Barrow!”

Enquiry 3: Secrets of Stonehenge (c. 3000–2200 BC) Textbook p.6 • Workbook pp.7–8
Key Enquiry: How and why did Stone Age people build giant Stonehenge?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This third Key Stage 2 enquiry explores Britain’s most iconic prehistoric monument across its main building stages (c. 3000–2200 BC). Primary pupils trace Stage 1’s chalk henge ditch dug with red-deer antler picks and cremation burials, Stage 2’s 140-mile transport of Welsh Preseli bluestones and 25-tonne sarsen trilithons joined with stone mortise-and-tenon pegs, the builders’ feasting village at Durrington Walls, and the Heel Stone’s alignment with Midsummer sunrise and Midwinter sunset.
Learning Objectives:
  • Sequence how Stonehenge grew from a circular chalk earth henge (c. 3000 BC) into a giant stone circle (c. 2500 BC).
  • Explain how Stonehenge’s builders locked giant trilithons together and lived at nearby Durrington Walls.
  • Evaluate the main archaeological ideas for why Stonehenge was built, including solar alignments.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Because Neolithic builders left no written records, archaeologists use clues to suggest three main purposes for Stonehenge. First, over one hundred cremation burials inside the 3000 BC chalk henge prove it began as a sacred cemetery honouring ancestors. Second, aligning the Heel Stone and tallest trilithons with Midsummer sunrise and Midwinter sunset made it a giant solar calendar marking the farming year. Third, Welsh bluestones and huge pork feasts at Durrington Walls show it united distant communities for worship and healing!”
Enquiry 4: Enter the Bronze Age & Beaker People (c. 2500–1200 BC) Textbook p.7 • Workbook pp.9–10
Key Enquiry: How did Beaker newcomers and bronze metal change life in Britain?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This Key Stage 2 enquiry explores the technological and social shift from the New Stone Age into the Early Bronze Age (c. 2500–1200 BC). Primary pupils investigate how Beaker migrants arrived from mainland Europe around 2400 BC with bell-shaped pottery and metalworking knowledge, how miners extracted copper at Great Orme (North Wales) and tin in Cornwall, and how smiths smelted ore and alloyed nine parts copper with one part tin to cast recyclable bronze axes in two-piece moulds. Finally, pupils examine dazzling sheet-gold regalia (the Mold Gold Cape, lunulae, and Bush Barrow lozenge) alongside the Amesbury Archer — whose Alpine tooth-enamel signature proves long-distance European travel.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain who the Beaker people were and how miners gathered copper and tin to smelt metal in Britain.
  • Describe how smiths mixed copper and tin to cast sharp, recyclable bronze tools inside two-piece moulds.
  • Use grave evidence from Bush Barrow and the Amesbury Archer to explain how metal created wealthy chieftains and European links.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Bronze metalworking transformed both everyday work and leadership in Britain because better tools and long-distance trade fed each other. On farms, tough bronze axes cast in two-piece moulds cleared woodland much faster than brittle flint, helping families grow more barley and raise larger herds. Even more dramatically, because tin in Cornwall and copper at Great Orme were rare, leaders who controlled metal trade and skilled guests like the Alpine-born Amesbury Archer became powerful chieftains, buried alone under round barrows near Stonehenge wearing gold lunulae and the Bush Barrow breastplate.”

Enquiry 5: Must Farm — "Britain's Pompeii" (c. 1200–800 BC) Textbook p.8 • Workbook pp.11–12
Key Enquiry: What did the sunken village at Must Farm reveal about Bronze Age homes?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This Key Stage 2 enquiry takes primary pupils inside one of Europe's most remarkable archaeological discoveries: the Late Bronze Age stilt settlement at Must Farm in Cambridgeshire (c. 900 BC), alongside nearby Flag Fen and the seagoing Dover Bronze Age Boat. Pupils learn in crystal-clear everyday language why wood, cloth, and food survived for 3,000 years (falling from a sudden fire straight into thick, airless wet river mud so nothing rotted away). Through rich domestic artefacts — porridge bowls with spoons, plant-fibre textiles, willow eel traps, hafted bronze axes, Mediterranean beads, and Britain's oldest complete 1-metre wooden wheel — pupils overturn any stereotype of prehistoric people as clumsy or isolated.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain why Must Farm was built on wooden stilts over a river and how thick, airless wet mud preserved organic items after a fire.
  • Use everyday finds from Must Farm — charred porridge, plant-fibre cloth, eel traps, and Britain's oldest complete wheel — to describe Bronze Age family life.
  • Describe how Bronze Age shipwrights built sewn-plank oak vessels like the Dover Boat to trade across the English Channel.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Archaeologists call Must Farm "Britain's Pompeii" because, just as volcanic ash froze Roman Pompeii in a single day, a sudden fire around 900 BC dropped Must Farm's stilt houses straight into thick, airless river mud so nothing rotted away. Because wood, food, and fabric normally vanish on dry sites, Must Farm matters enormously: its hafted bronze axes, superfine plant-fibre cloth, willow eel traps, porridge spoons, Mediterranean beads, and Britain's oldest complete wooden wheel prove Bronze Age Britons were skilled engineers, weavers, farmers, and traders!”
Enquiry 6: Iron Age Celts & Giant Hillforts (c. 800 BC–AD 43) Textbook p.9 • Workbook pp.13–14
Key Enquiry: How did iron tools and giant hillforts shape Celtic Britain?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This capstone Key Stage 2 enquiry brings over 800,000 years of British prehistory to a vivid climax in the Iron Age (c. 800 BC–AD 43). Primary pupils explore why common reddish iron ore allowed every village blacksmith to forge tough ploughshares, saws, and swords without relying solely on distant tin routes. Pupils step inside smoky thatched wattle-and-daub roundhouses (rotary querns, upright looms), climb the giant chalk ramparts of Maiden Castle and the Uffington White Horse, and admire Celtic artistry (the Waterloo Horned Helmet, Snettisham Great Torc, and gold stater coins) — proving Britain on the eve of Emperor Claudius's AD 43 invasion was populous, artistic, and thriving.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain why widespread iron ore and blacksmith forging made iron tools more common and tougher than bronze.
  • Describe everyday Celtic life inside wattle-and-daub roundhouses and giant defended hillforts like Maiden Castle.
  • Synthesise changes across the Stone, Bronze, and Iron Ages using torcs, horned helmets, and gold stater coins up to AD 43.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Across more than 800,000 years from the Stone Age to the Iron Age, Britain changed from tiny bands of Ice Age hunter-gatherers tracking mammoths across Doggerland into bustling Iron Age tribal kingdoms! Starting to farm around 4000 BC let families settle in stone homes like Skara Brae and raise Stonehenge, while Bronze Age smiths cast recyclable axes, built Must Farm's wheeled carts, and paddled the Dover Boat. Finally, widespread iron ploughs after 800 BC fed thousands inside roundhouses and giant hillforts like Maiden Castle, minting gold coins right as Rome arrived in AD 43!”
