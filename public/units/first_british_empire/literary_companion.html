The Velvet Coach & The Red Silk Tent: Sir Thomas Roe (1616) and Clive’s Diwani (1765)

The History Revision Hub · Grounded in Sir Thomas Roe’s Journal (BL Add MS 6115) & Clive’s 1765 Dispatches
Historical Context: When English ships first reached India after 1600, the Mughal Empire produced roughly a quarter of the world’s goods and dwarfed Stuart England in wealth. Tracing the arc from Sir Thomas Roe’s humble gift at Emperor Jahangir’s court in 1616 to Robert Clive’s seizure of Bengal’s taxes in 1765 reveals how a London trading company turned itself into a territorial empire.
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Emperor Jahangir[juh-HAHN-geer]
Fourth Mughal Emperor (r. 1605–1627), whose dazzling court at Ajmer received English ambassador Sir Thomas Roe.
Firman[fur-MAHN]
An official Mughal imperial decree granting foreign merchants permission to trade at coastal ports like Surat.
Sepoys[SEE-poyz]
Indian infantry soldiers recruited, drilled, and paid by the British East India Company.
Diwani[dee-WAH-nee]
The 1765 right to collect all land taxes across Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa—turning the East India Company into Bengal’s ruler.

In January 1616, English ambassador Sir Thomas Roe bowed low inside the marble palace of Mughal Emperor Jahangir at Ajmer. Mughal India then produced nearly a quarter of the world’s textiles and riches. Jahangir sat glittering in ropes of pearls and rubies larger than walnuts. Hoping to impress the Emperor, Roe presented a wooden English coach lined with crimson velvet.

Jahangir smiled politely, rode once around the palace courtyard by torchlight, and handed the coach to his royal craftsmen. Within weeks, Indian builders produced a finer copy upholstered in gleaming silver brocade. Humbled by Mughal wealth and horsemen, Roe warned the East India Company in London: "If you will profit, seek it at sea, and in quiet trade." English merchants survived solely by the Emperor’s firman—his written permission.

One hundred and forty-nine years later, that world had turned upside down. As Mughal authority fractured after 1707, the Company drilled private armies of Indian sepoys armed with flintlock muskets. After bribing rival general Mir Jafar to win the Battle of Plassey in 1757, Robert Clive met young Emperor Shah Alam II inside a red silk tent at Allahabad in August 1765. Perched atop a covered dining table as a makeshift throne, Clive took the Diwani—the legal right to collect taxes from twenty million people in Bengal. Without shipping an ounce of English silver, humble spice traders had become corporate kings.

The Interesting Narrative of the Life of Olaudah Equiano: Below Decks (1789)

Olaudah Equiano (Chapter II, London, 1789 • Calibrated for Aloud Reading)
Historical Context: Olaudah Equiano (c. 1745–1797)—known for much of his life as Gustavus Vassa—survived enslavement, bought his own freedom for forty pounds in 1766, and helped lead the 'Sons of Africa' abolitionist campaign in London. Published in 1789 just as MPs debated the slave trade, his bestselling memoir shattered pro-slavery myths with unforgettable eyewitness truth.
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Olaudah Equiano[oh-LOW-duh ek-wee-AH-noh]
Formerly enslaved writer and London abolitionist whose 1789 autobiography exposed the horrors of the Middle Passage.
Middle Passage[MID-ul PASS-ij]
The brutal six-to-twelve-week Atlantic voyage carrying enslaved Africans in chains to plantations in the Americas.
Chattel Slavery[CHAT-ul SLAY-vuh-ree]
A legal system treating human beings and their children as permanent private property ('cargo') to be bought and sold.
Avarice[AV-uh-riss]
Extreme greed for money—the moral crime Equiano blamed for slave traders cramming captives below decks to die.

"The first object that met my eyes when I reached the coast was the sea, and a slave ship riding at anchor. Sight of that vessel filled me with astonishment, which quickly turned to terror when I was carried on board. Rough sailors immediately handled and tossed me up to check whether I was sound and strong. Looking around at their strange faces and a boiling copper pot on deck, I felt sure I had entered a world of bad spirits who meant to kill me.

Soon I was forced down beneath the wooden decks. There a dreadful stench struck my nostrils such as I had never known in my life. Overcome by the foul air and the weeping all around me, I grew so sick and low that I could not swallow a bite of food. Two crewmen tied my hands and flogged me severely because I refused to eat.

Down in the hold, the fierce tropical heat and the crushing crowd nearly suffocated us. Chained captives had scarcely room to turn, and heavy sweat poured from every body until the air became unfit to breathe. Deadly sickness soon swept through the ship, killing many poor souls who fell victim to the short-sighted greed—the avarice—of their purchasers. The shrieks of the women and the groans of the dying made the whole hold a scene of horror almost beyond belief."

Sound of the Abeng: Queen Nanny, Mountain Camouflage & The 1739 Maroon Peace Treaty

The History Revision Hub · Grounded in Colonial Office CO 137/23 & Bryan Edwards (1796)
Historical Context: Enslaved Africans in Jamaica never accepted sugar-plantation bondage passively. Escaping into the razor-sharp limestone Cockpits and mist-covered Blue Mountains, Windward leader Queen Nanny and Leeward commander Captain Cudjoe waged such brilliant guerrilla warfare between 1728 and 1739 that the British Empire had to beg for peace.
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Maroons[muh-ROONZ]
Self-liberated Africans and their descendants who escaped Caribbean plantations and defended free mountain towns.
Abeng[ah-BENG]
A curved cow-horn trumpet blown by Jamaican Maroon lookouts to send coded warnings across miles of mountain valleys.
Guerrilla Warfare[guh-RIL-uh]
Fast hit-and-run ambushes using steep terrain, stealth, and leaf camouflage to defeat a larger regular army.
Queen Nanny[NAN-ee]
Asante-born military strategist and spiritual leader of the Windward Maroons in Jamaica’s Blue Mountains.

High in the mist-wrapped Blue Mountains of eastern Jamaica, British redcoats marched single-file along a narrow stone ledge. Sweat soaked their heavy red wool coats and clanking musket belts. Suddenly, a low, haunting note echoed from peak to peak. Far above them, a Maroon scout blew the curved cow-horn called the abeng to warn of the marching column.

Before the soldiers spotted a single face, the green forest wall seemed to move. Dressed from head to foot in tied branches and wild leaves, Windward Maroon fighters struck fast and vanished into deep limestone gullies. Their leader was Queen Nanny, a bold Asante-born leader who built Nanny Town high above sheer cliffs. There free families grew yams, plantains, and pumpkins far beyond any planter’s whip. In western Jamaica’s jagged Cockpit Country, Captain Cudjoe’s fighters broke British supply lines the same way.

By March 1739, eleven years of hard mountain fighting had exhausted the British garrison. Nearly a century before Britain ended slavery in 1833, Governor Edward Trelawny sent officers into the hills to beg for peace. Signed treaties recognised Maroon freedom forever and granted fifteen hundred acres of free land. Yet peace carried a bitter price. To keep their hard-won liberty, Maroon chiefs had to promise to hand back future runaways from lowland sugar estates.

Tyranny & Tea Chests: Phillis Wheatley’s Verse (1772) and George Hewes at Boston Harbour

Phillis Wheatley (Poems, 1773) & Shoemaker George Robert Twelves Hewes (1773 Eyewitness)
Historical Context: After the Seven Years’ War doubled Britain’s debt, Westminster taxed American newspapers (1765 Stamp Act) and granted the East India Company a colonial tea monopoly (1773). In rebellious Boston, enslaved poet Phillis Wheatley exposed the sharp irony of slaveholding colonists crying 'Tyranny!'—months before shoemaker George Hewes helped dump 342 tea chests into the harbour.
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Phillis Wheatley[FIL-iss WEET-lee]
Enslaved African-born Boston teenager who in 1773 became the first African American author to publish a book of poetry.
Tyrannic Sway[tih-RAN-ik SWAY]
Cruel, unchecked rule without consent—linked by Wheatley to her own kidnapping from West Africa.
Sons of Liberty[SUNZ uv LIB-er-tee]
Colonial protest networks of artisans, dockworkers, and merchants who organised boycotts and the 1773 Boston Tea Party.
Monopoly[muh-NOP-uh-lee]
Exclusive control of a trade—Parliament’s 1773 Tea Act let the East India Company undercut local colonial merchants.

In Boston in October 1772, white colonists cried out that London taxes without elected MPs turned freeborn Britons into "slaves." Watching from a Boston house where she herself was legally enslaved, nineteen-year-old poet Phillis Wheatley wrote to King George III’s minister, the Earl of Dartmouth.

Should you, my lord, while you peruse my song,
Wonder from whence my love of Freedom sprung?
I, young in life, by seeming cruel fate
Was snatch’d from Afric’s fancy’d happy seat.
What pangs excruciating must molest,
What sorrows labour in my parent’s breast?
And can I then but pray
Others may never feel tyrannic sway?

Fourteen months later, on the freezing night of 16 December 1773, Boston’s tax anger boiled over at Griffin’s Wharf. Poor shoemaker George Hewes slipped into a blacksmith’s shop, rubbed black coal dust over his face and hands, and gripped a small hatchet. Dressed as Mohawk warriors, he and the Sons of Liberty boarded three East India Company ships in dead silence. In three hours, they chopped open three hundred and forty-two chests of Company tea and heaved them into the harbour. Salt water turned brown with floating leaves from Bengal and China. Not one man was allowed to pocket a single ounce for himself.

Memoirs of the Life of Boston King: From Charleston Bondage to the Book of Negroes

Boston King (The Methodist Magazine, London, 1798 • Calibrated for Aloud Reading)
Historical Context: While Thomas Jefferson’s 1776 Declaration of Independence proclaimed that 'all men are created equal', Jefferson himself enslaved over six hundred people. Judging that real freedom lay behind British lines under Lord Dunmore’s 1775 and Clinton’s 1779 proclamations, over 20,000 Black Loyalists—including South Carolina carpenter Boston King and his wife Violet—risked their lives to escape.
Scan to Listen
(UK Narrator)
Step 1 • Pre-Teach Key Words Before Reading Sound out [pronunciation] helpers aloud
Black Loyalists[BLAK LOY-uh-lists]
Over 20,000 enslaved African Americans who escaped Patriot masters during the American Revolution to claim freedom with British forces.
Dunmore’s Proclamation[DUN-morz]
The 1775 British offer promising immediate freedom to enslaved people owned by American Rebels if they crossed into British lines.
Book of Negroes[BOOK uv NEE-grohz]
The official 1783 British military ledger registering 3,000 Black Loyalists evacuated as free people from New York to Nova Scotia.
Nova Scotia[NOH-vuh SKOH-shuh]
The cold Atlantic province in Canada where Boston and Violet King settled as free citizens before later sailing to Sierra Leone.

"In 1780, British troops captured Charleston in South Carolina. Word ran fast across the plantations that freedom awaited all enslaved people who reached the King’s camp. Choosing to risk death rather than stay in chains, I borrowed a horse at midnight and rode past the Rebel patrols. British soldiers welcomed me as a free man, though deadly smallpox soon swept through our tents.

Yet when peace was signed with the United States in 1783, dreadful terror gripped every Black Loyalist in New York. Rumours ran down the alleys that all former slaves would be handed back to their old American masters! Worse still, angry Southern planters walked right into New York seizing men and women off the pavements. For days my wife Violet and I could neither eat nor sleep for fear of returning to chains.

Mercifully, British General Sir Guy Carleton kept the Crown’s promise to everyone who had served a full year. Clerks wrote our names, ages, and scars into a great ledger called the Book of Negroes. Holding our signed certificates of freedom, Violet and I boarded the ship L’Abondance and sailed free to Nova Scotia. Years later, we crossed the Atlantic once more to help build a free Black settlement in Sierra Leone."

2. Why was the 1783 peace treaty a moment of terror rather than celebration for Black Loyalists waiting in New York?
3. Did Britain issue Dunmore’s Proclamation out of pure anti-slavery principle, or wartime calculation?

Parent, Carer & Tutor Conversation Guide & Answer Key The First British Empire
No Specialist History Knowledge Needed: Pupils do not need word-for-word answers—praise curiosity and listen for these core historical ideas during your 3-minute chat!
Enquiry 1 (p. 2): Roe & Clive in India
Q1: Mughal craftsmen dismantled and copied its design, quickly building a superior coach lined with silver brocade—showing India’s manufacturing skill.
Q2: Under Jahangir, the Mughal Empire was vastly richer and militarily stronger than Stuart England, so armed force would have ended in disaster.
Q3: The Company collected £2 million+ each year in taxes from Bengali farmers and used Indian taxpayers’ own money to buy Asian exports for London.
Enquiry 2 (p. 3): Equiano’s Voyage (1789)
Q1: Slave traders claimed captives were well cared for on ships; Equiano’s eloquent eyewitness book proved both the agony below decks and the humanity and brilliance of Black writers.
Q2: Merchants packed 454 people—and earlier over 600—into shelves just 2 feet 7 inches high to squeeze extra profit from every voyage, breeding suffocation and disease.
Q3: Under chattel slavery, captains treated enslaved people as financial 'cargo' whose survival until auction in the Caribbean determined the crew’s profits.
Enquiry 3 (p. 4): Queen Nanny & Maroons
Q1: Heavy red wool uniforms made easy targets on narrow trails, while Maroon fighters knew every cliff, used head-to-foot leaf camouflage, and sent coded abeng horn alerts.
Q2: Ninety-four years before Parliament ended slavery in 1833, self-liberated Africans militarily forced the British Crown to recognize their permanent freedom and land.
Q3: To protect their free mountain towns from endless British invasion, Maroon leaders were pressured into agreeing not to shelter newly escaped plantation captives.
Enquiry 4 (p. 5): Wheatley & Boston Tea
Q1: Unlike white colonists protesting paper and tea taxes, she had literally been kidnapped as a child from West Africa, tearing her from her heartbroken parents.
Q2: Disguises hid their identities from British arrest, while strict discipline proved they were staging a principled tax protest—not a greedy dockside robbery.
Q3: It was the British East India Company—Parliament passed the 1773 Tea Act to bail out the very same Company that had conquered Mughal Bengal!
Enquiry 5 (p. 6): Boston King’s Escape
Q1: Patriot leaders like Washington and Jefferson kept slavery legal in the new United States, whereas British commanders offered freedom certificates to runaways from Rebel estates.
Q2: American slaveholders entered New York demanding their escaped human 'property' back; only General Carleton’s refusal and the Book of Negroes protected 3,000 evacuees.
Q3: Wartime calculation—Britain freed enslaved people escaping Rebel Patriots to cripple the colonial economy, while keeping slavery fully legal in loyal Jamaica until 1833.
