LESSON 1 • Similarity, Contrast and Early State Building

Rainforest Kingdom — Ogiso Rulers, Red-Earth Walls and Anglo-Saxon Contrast (c. AD 900–1180)

Key Enquiry: How did Edo communities build a vast rainforest kingdom by AD 900?
[1.1] c. AD 900: Edo Rainforest Towns and Ogiso Kings
“How did the Ogiso rule early Edo communities in southern Nigeria?”

Around AD 900, the Edo people built thriving forest towns in modern southern Nigeria. That homeland lay east of the modern country called Republic of Benin! Beneath giant mahogany trees, families cleared sunny plots to grow white yams, plantains, and spicy pepper.

Tall palms gave rich red palm oil for cooking and night lamps. Local blacksmiths smelted iron ore into strong farming hoes! Thirty-one early rulers called the Ogiso, meaning Kings of the Sky, united separate villages and settled disputes.

[1.2] c. AD 900–1180: Digging 16,000 Kilometres of Red-Clay Iya
“Why did Edo villagers dig giant red-clay Iya ditches by hand?”

Without wheeled carts or machines, Edo villagers dug a vast network of deep ditches and steep banks called Iya. Diggers chopped damp red laterite clay with iron hoes. Chains of helpers passed woven reed baskets up to the top!

Hot dry-season sun baked the piled red clay as hard as stone walls. Many banks rose higher than a two-storey house! Over centuries, linked Iya rings enclosed over five hundred settlements across sixteen thousand kilometres, four times longer than China’s Great Wall.

[1.3] c. AD 900: Rainforest Edo Contrasted With Anglo-Saxon Britain
“What made AD 900 Edo so different from Anglo-Saxon Britain?”

What was happening in Britain around AD 900 while the Ogiso ruled Edo? Facing fierce Viking raids, Anglo-Saxon villages farmed barley and sheep around smoky thatched halls. Rulers defended smaller walled towns called timber burhs.

Early medieval monks in Britain wrote Latin and Old English on sheepskin. Meanwhile, Edo passed laws and history down through royal storytellers, sacred carvings, and vast earthwork walls! Both societies valued skilled ironworkers and strong community defence.

LESSON 2 • Cause, Consequence and Urban Planning

From Ogiso to Oba — Ewuare the Great and Golden Benin City (c. AD 1180–1500)

Key Enquiry: How did Oba Ewuare the Great turn Benin City into a golden capital?
[2.1] c. AD 1180: Sacred Ife, Prince Oranmiyan and Oba Eweka I
“How did Prince Oranmiyan of Ife link to Oba Eweka?”

Around AD 1180, senior Uzama elders banished the last cruel Ogiso ruler from the throne. Seeking calm and fair rule, those respected kingmakers asked sacred Ife to the north-west for wise help. Soon a young royal prince set out southwards for Benin.

Prince Oranmiyan arrived from Ife and married an Edo noblewoman from a leading family. Their Edo-born son grew up speaking Edo and was crowned Oba Eweka I! He founded the sacred royal Oba line that still reigns in Benin City more than eight hundred years later.

[2.2] c. AD 1440–1473: Oba Ewuare’s Nine Gates and Palm-Oil Lamps
“Why did Oba Ewuare build nine gates and palm-oil street lamps?”

Around AD 1440, warrior-king Oba Ewuare the Great rebuilt burned Benin City and named the capital Edo. Thousands of workers dug a massive inner moat around the town. Guards checked visitors and collected tolls at nine fortified gates!

Inside the walls, Oba Ewuare planned broad straight avenues and busy markets. Clever underground channels carried storm rain away cleanly. At night, tall iron palm-oil lamps lit the streets centuries before London had regular street lights!

[2.3] c. AD 1450–1500: Palace Courtyards, Coral Crowns and Craft Guilds
“What royal guilds and coral treasures filled Edo’s grand palace?”

At the heart of Edo rose a vast walled palace of sunny courtyards and smooth red-clay galleries. High atop pointed wooden roof turrets perched giant brass palace birds. Their wide metal wings watched proudly over the whole capital!

Ewuare wore heavy red coral beads so weighty that two attendants supported his arms! He also started the joyful Igue thanksgiving festival. Around the palace he organised forty craft guilds of weavers, drummers, woodcarvers, ivory sculptors, and brass-casters.

LESSON 3 • Technology, Craftsmanship and Visual Evidence

Lost-Wax Masterpieces — Igun Street Brass-Casters, Ivory and Coral (c. AD 1400–1700)

Key Enquiry: How did Igun Street artists cast brass plaques to record Benin’s history?
[3.1] c. AD 1400–1600: Golden Brass and the Royal Igun Street Guild
“Why are most famous Benin Bronzes really made of golden brass?”

Visitors today admire thousands of royal sculptures known worldwide as the Benin Bronzes. Yet modern tests show most are actually gleaming brass! Mixing copper and zinc created a bright golden metal that shone like sunlight inside palace courtyards.

By strict royal law, only the sacred Oba could own brass sculptures or gift them to chiefs. Master metalworkers lived together on Igun Street in Benin. Inside their royal guild, parents passed secret casting skills down to young apprentices.

[3.2] c. AD 1400–1700: Three Steps of Lost-Wax Brass Casting
“How did Igun sculptors pour liquid brass using melted beeswax moulds?”

West African smiths mastered lost-wax casting centuries before European ships arrived! First, an Igun sculptor shaped a rough clay core and covered it with soft yellow wax. Next, bone tools carved every tiny bead into the beeswax model.

Workers then packed fine wet clay round the wax and heated the dried mould until melted beeswax drained out! Charcoal furnaces pumped by goat-skin bellows melted brass inside a clay crucible. After pouring liquid brass in, casters cracked the cool clay open!

[3.3] c. AD 1500–1700: Palace Pillar Plaques and Carved Ivory Tusks
“What stories did carved tusks and palace plaques keep for Benin?”

Look closely at the corners of rectangular plaques from Benin and you can spot round nail holes! Over nine hundred plaques were nailed onto tall wooden palace pillars. Artists cast the Oba largest in the middle as a shining visual history archive.

On royal altars stood two-metre carved ivory tusks made by the Igbesanmwan ivory guild. Tiny river-leaf patterns honoured water-god Olokun! Together with court singers, plaques and tusks recorded battles, rituals, and visitors without paper books.

LESSON 4 • Beliefs, Female Leadership and Everyday Life

Sacred Court Rituals — The Oba, Queen Mother Idia and Everyday Edo Life (c. AD 1500–1700)

Key Enquiry: What was life like inside the Oba’s court and Edo rainforest villages?
[4.1] c. AD 1500–1700: Leopard of the House and Sea-God Olokun
“Why were spotted leopards and mudfish sacred symbols of the Oba?”

Edo people honoured supreme creator god Osanobua alongside Olokun, generous god of rivers, deep oceans, white chalk, and wealth. Citizens revered their sacred Oba as the living spiritual bridge between human land and the unseen spirit world!

Just like the fearless forest king, singers praised the ruler as Leopard of the House and kept tame leopards at court. Royal plaques also carved coiled mudfish! Because mudfish swim in rivers and wriggle across damp mud, they linked dry land to Olokun.

[4.2] c. AD 1504–1550: Oba Esigie and Wise Queen Mother Idia
“How did Oba Esigie honour his wise mother Idia at court?”

Around AD 1504, young Oba Esigie faced dangerous rival armies marching south from Idah. His wise, fearless mother Idia rallied royal troops and archers for battle! Her sharp political advice helped Benin win a famous victory.

In deep gratitude, Esigie created the high royal title of Iyoba, meaning Queen Mother. He built Idia her own grand Uselu palace with chiefs just outside the walls! At sacred ceremonies he wore a pale carved ivory mask of her calm face on his hip.

[4.3] c. AD 1500–1700: Everyday Edo Farming, Weaving and Igue Feasts
“What food and cloth did Edo village farmers make each year?”

Most Edo families lived in neat rainforest villages built from smooth red clay around open courtyards. Men cleared sunlit forest plots to grow white yams and sweet plantains. Women tended vegetable gardens, cooked spicy palm-oil stews, and wove fine cotton cloth!

Children helped fetch fresh stream water, sweep courtyards clean, and hear evening folktales under starlight. Brass heads, carved tusks, and square bells stood on ancestral altars. Every winter, families gathered in Benin City to dance at the joyful Igue festival!

LESSON 5 • Global Connections and Historical Interpretations

Global Traders — Brass Manillas, Cowrie Shells and European Visitors (c. AD 1485–1850)

Key Enquiry: Why were European visitors so amazed by Benin’s trade and wide streets?
[5.1] AD 1485–1553: Portuguese Ships, Gwato Port and Lisbon Envoys
“How did Benin rule trade when Portuguese ships arrived in 1485?”

Long before ocean ships appeared, Benin traded north across savanna trails for copper, beads, and salt. Then in AD 1485, Portuguese explorer João Afonso de Aveiro sailed up the river to Gwato port and visited the Oba’s royal court!

Benin met Europe as a proud, independent equal. Foreign captains could not buy a single basket without the Oba’s licence and fixed prices! Soon Benin sent royal ambassadors by ship to Lisbon, and in 1553 English sailors met an Oba speaking fluent Portuguese.

[5.2] c. AD 1485–1700: Pepper, Brass Manillas and Cowrie-Shell Money
“Why did Benin swap spicy pepper and cloth for brass bracelets?”

European merchants were eager to buy fiery Benin pepper, striped cotton cloth, and leopard pelts. Royal carvers also made fine ivory saltcellars for foreign buyers! Early in the 1500s, the Oba strictly banned selling male Edo citizens to foreign slave ships.

In return, ships unloaded glossy white cowrie shells for everyday market coins alongside heavy chests of horseshoe-shaped brass manillas. Hot furnaces on Igun Street melted thousands of imported manillas down! That fresh metal sparked a golden boom in palace plaques across Benin.

[5.3] AD 1668: Olfert Dapper Praises Thirty Wide Clean Streets
“What amazed Olfert Dapper about Benin’s wide clean streets in 1668?”

By the 1600s, Dutch and English traders joined Portuguese ships along the West African coast. Staying in Holland in 1668, Dutch writer Olfert Dapper gathered real eyewitness merchant diaries into a famous book praising great Benin City!

Dapper reported thirty straight streets around one hundred and twenty feet wide! Its walled palace covered as much ground as the Dutch town of Haarlem. Families scrubbed polished clay walls like mirrors while Stuart London still had muddy lanes.

LESSON 6 • Consequence, Ethics and Living Heritage

1897 British Invasion, Looted Bronzes and Living Edo Heritage Today (1897–Present)

Key Enquiry: Why did Britain invade Benin in 1897, and should looted bronzes return?
[6.1] 1888–February 1897: Palm-Oil Clash and the British Invasion
“Why did British troops invade Oba Ovonramwen’s kingdom in 1897?”

By the 1890s, the expanding British Empire wanted full control over southern Nigeria’s rich palm oil and rubber for Victorian factories. Oba Ovonramwen defended Benin’s royal customs tolls and trade independence firmly.

In January 1897, Ovonramwen asked visitors to wait during sacred Igue rituals. An uninvited British group marched on anyway and was ambushed by chiefs without his order! Britain struck back in February 1897 with twelve hundred troops in a fierce Punitive Expedition.

[6.2] 1897–1900s: Four Thousand Looted Bronzes Sold Around the World
“What happened to Benin’s sacred palace plaques after February 1897?”

In February 1897, invading soldiers captured Benin City and sent Oba Ovonramwen into sad exile in Calabar. Three days later, a fierce fire swept the palace roofs! Entering palace storehouses, troops stripped plaques from pillars and tusks from altars.

More than four thousand artworks of brass, ivory, and coral were packed into crates for London to pay war costs. Auctions scattered them across over one hundred and sixty world museums! Their lost-wax skill stunned experts who had doubted African history.

[6.3] 1914–Present: Modern Restitution and Living Edo Culture Today
“How do restitution returns and Igun casters keep Benin’s story alive?”

Benin’s royal throne was restored under Oba Eweka II in 1914, reviving palace guilds. Since 2021, Cambridge, London’s Horniman Museum, America, and Germany have begun formal restitution! Sacred cockerels, royal heads, and plaques are returning home to Nigeria after a century abroad.

Best of all, living Edo culture thrives every single day in modern Benin City! Crowned in bright red coral, Oba Ewuare II leads the joyful annual Igue festival. Nearby on Igun Street, hereditary families still pour glowing brass for the whole world to admire!

KEY STAGE 2 ILLUSTRATED GLOSSARY SYLLABLE HELPERS & DEFINITIONS

Historian's Word Bank (24 Essential Terms)

Clap the italic syllables out loud together to master new historical words with confidence!

Edo E-do
The people, language, and rainforest heartland of the historic Kingdom of Benin in modern southern Nigeria.
Ogiso O-gi-so
Early kings and queens who ruled Benin from around AD 900 to 1180; their royal title meant "Kings of the Sky".
Iya I-ya
Deep hand-dug ditches and steep sun-baked red-clay ramparts stretching 16,000 kilometres around Edo settlements.
Laterite Lat-er-ite
Iron-rich red tropical clay soil in West Africa that bakes hard as brick under the hot dry-season sun.
Oba O-ba
The sacred king of Benin, honoured by Edo citizens since around AD 1180 as ruler of the land and link to royal ancestors.
Ife I-fe
Sacred Yoruba city north-west of Benin, famed for lifelike brass royal heads and Prince Oranmiyan.
Dynasty Dy-nas-ty
An unbroken line of rulers from one royal family—such as the Oba dynasty founded by Oba Eweka I.
Coral Beads Cor-al Beads
Polished red sea-coral beads worn in heavy crowns, collars, and tunics introduced by Oba Ewuare the Great.
Benin Bronzes Be-nin Bron-zes
Famous royal plaques, heads, and figures from Benin City—mostly cast from bright golden brass alongside bronze.
Brass Brass
A gleaming golden-yellow metal alloy combining copper and zinc, prized by the Oba for palace plaques and heads.
Igun Street I-gun Street
The royal guild quarter in Benin City where master brass-casting families have lived and poured metal for centuries.
Lost-Wax Casting Lost-Wax Cast-ing
Carving a beeswax figure, coating it in clay, melting out the wax, and pouring molten brass into the hollow space.
Iyoba I-yo-ba
Royal Edo title meaning "Queen Mother", created around 1515 by Oba Esigie to honour his wise mother Idia.
Olokun O-lo-kun
Revered Edo god of great waters, rivers, ocean trade, and prosperity, honoured alongside creator god Osanobua.
Mudfish Mud-fish
A West African fish able to swim in rivers and breathe wriggling across damp land—symbolising the Oba’s dual power.
Igue Festival I-gue Fes-ti-val
Joyful annual royal Benin thanksgiving ceremony thanking ancestors and blessing the Oba and kingdom for the new year.
Gwato Port Gwa-to Port
Benin’s busy river harbour (Ughoton) where foreign ships anchored and waited for the Oba’s trade permission.
Ambassador Am-bas-sa-dor
A trusted royal diplomat sent abroad to represent one king at another ruler’s court—such as Benin’s envoy to Lisbon.
Manillas Ma-nil-las
Heavy horseshoe-shaped bracelets of brass and copper brought by European ships and melted down on Igun Street.
Cowrie Shells Cow-rie Shells
Small glossy white sea shells brought by ocean trade and used across Benin’s markets as everyday money.
Oba Ovonramwen O-ba O-von-ram-wen
Oba of Benin (r. 1888–1914) who defended Benin’s trade independence before British forces invaded and exiled him in 1897.
Punitive Expedition Pu-ni-tive Ex-pe-di-tion
The February 1897 British military invasion of 1,200 troops that captured Benin City, burned the palace, and looted its art.
Looting Loot-ing
Seizing sacred treasures, artworks, or property by military force during war—as befell over 4,000 Benin works in 1897.
Restitution Res-ti-tu-tion
Rightfully returning looted cultural artworks from foreign museums back to their homeland and living community.
