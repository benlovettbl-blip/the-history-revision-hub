WALK-IN LESSON PLANS • ENQUIRIES 1 & 2 NON-SPECIALIST PRIMARY TEACHER GUIDE
Enquiry 1: Rainforest Kingdom — Ogiso Rulers, Red-Earth Walls and Anglo-Saxon Contrast (c. AD 900–1180) TB p.4 • WB pp.3–4
Key Enquiry: How did Edo communities build a vast rainforest kingdom by AD 900?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This opening Key Stage 2 enquiry locates the historic Kingdom of Benin accurately in Edo State in modern southern Nigeria (carefully distinguishing it from the modern country called Republic of Benin further west). Pupils explore how rainforest resources (yams, palm oil, ivory, mahogany, and iron) supported early towns ruled by the Ogiso ("Kings of the Sky"), how villagers dug 16,000 kilometres of linked red-clay Iya moats and ramparts, and how AD 900 Benin contrasted with Anglo-Saxon Britain.
Learning Objectives:
  • Locate Edo (Benin City) in modern southern Nigeria and explain how rainforest resources supported early towns.
  • Explain how early Ogiso rulers ("Kings of the Sky") and village teams built 16,000 km of red-clay Iya earthworks.
  • Contrast early rainforest Benin around AD 900 with life in Anglo-Saxon Britain.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Around AD 900, both early Benin in West Africa and Anglo-Saxon Britain built defences, yet they differed sharply because of climate, crops, and scale. In southern Nigeria’s rainforest, Edo farmers grew yams and palm oil under Ogiso ("Kings of the Sky") rulers and dug 16,000 kilometres of giant red-clay Iya walls—far larger than the smaller timber burhs built across Anglo-Saxon Britain!”
Enquiry 2: From Ogiso to Oba — Ewuare the Great and Golden Benin City (c. AD 1180–1500) TB p.5 • WB pp.5–6
Key Enquiry: How did Oba Ewuare the Great turn Benin City into a golden capital?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This second enquiry traces how Benin transitioned around AD 1180 from the banished last Ogiso to the sacred Oba dynasty linked to Yoruba Ife (Prince Oranmiyan and his Edo-born son Oba Eweka I), before examining Oba Ewuare the Great’s (r. c. 1440–1473) sweeping transformation of Benin City: an immense inner moat with nine fortified toll gates, wide straight avenues, rainwater drains, palm-oil street lighting, red coral regalia, and specialist palace guilds.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain how Edo elders invited Prince Oranmiyan from sacred Ife around AD 1180, founding the royal dynasty under Oba Eweka I.
  • Describe how Oba Ewuare the Great rebuilt Benin City around 1440 with nine gates, straight avenues, and palm-oil lamps.
  • Identify how red coral regalia, brass palace birds, and specialist craft guilds enriched Oba Ewuare’s royal court.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “After AD 1440, Oba Ewuare the Great transformed Benin City into one of the world’s best-planned capitals by combining strong defences, clean streets, and dazzling royal art. He dug a deep inner moat with nine fortified gates, laid out broad straight avenues with drains and glowing palm-oil lamps, introduced sacred red coral beads, and organised forty specialist craft guilds!”

Enquiry 3: Lost-Wax Masterpieces — Igun Street Brass-Casters, Ivory and Coral (c. AD 1400–1700) TB p.6 • WB pp.7–8
Key Enquiry: How did Igun Street artists cast brass plaques to record Benin’s history?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This third enquiry explores the scientific and artistic genius of Benin’s royal guilds (c. AD 1400–1700). Pupils discover why most "Benin Bronzes" are actually golden brass (copper and zinc), trace the step-by-step lost-wax (cire perdue) casting process perfected on Igun Street centuries before European ships arrived, and investigate how over 900 brass pillar plaques alongside carved elephant tusks worked as Benin’s visual palace history archive.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain why most "Benin Bronzes" are actually cast brass and who was allowed to own them.
  • Sequence every step of "lost-wax" (cire perdue) brass casting on Igun Street.
  • Show how 900+ brass pillar plaques and carved elephant tusks preserved Benin’s history without paper books.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Even without printed paper books, the Kingdom of Benin preserved rich historical records for centuries by combining metal, ivory, and spoken memory. Igun Street casters nailed over 900 detailed brass plaques onto wooden palace pillars, ivory-carvers sculpted two-metre carved ivory tusks for royal altars, and trained court singers recited every Oba’s deeds aloud!”
Enquiry 4: Sacred Court Rituals — The Oba, Queen Mother Idia and Everyday Edo Life (c. AD 1500–1700) TB p.7 • WB pp.9–10
Key Enquiry: What was life like inside the Oba’s court and Edo rainforest villages?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This fourth enquiry pairs sacred royal ritual with everyday Edo village life during Benin’s sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Golden Age. Pupils explore why spotted leopards and amphibious mudfish symbolised the Oba’s link between dry land and sea-god Olokun, how Oba Esigie (r. c. 1504–1550) created the powerful office of Iyoba (Queen Mother) for his wise mother Idia, and how ordinary families farmed yams and plantains, pressed palm oil, wove cotton cloth, and celebrated the annual Igue festival.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain why spotted leopards and river mudfish were chosen as sacred symbols of the Oba and god Olokun.
  • Evaluate the leadership of Queen Mother (Iyoba) Idia during the reign of her son Oba Esigie.
  • Describe everyday family life, farming, cotton weaving, and the annual Igue festival across Edo villages.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “During Benin’s Golden Age, sacred palace rituals and everyday village families depended closely on one another because villages fed the kingdom while the court kept peace and order. While the Oba and Iyoba Idia led ceremonies beside brass ancestral altars, village families in red-clay compounds grew white yams, pressed palm oil, and wove fine cotton cloth—uniting joyfully every winter at the Igue festival!”

Enquiry 5: Global Traders — Brass Manillas, Cowrie Shells and European Visitors (c. AD 1485–1850) TB p.8 • WB pp.11–12
Key Enquiry: Why were European visitors so amazed by Benin’s trade and wide streets?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This fifth enquiry investigates Benin’s global trade and diplomatic standing between AD 1485 and 1850. Pupils learn how Benin met Portuguese explorer João Afonso de Aveiro (1485) and later Dutch and English merchants strictly as an independent equal at Gwato river port, how trading Benin pepper, cotton cloth, and ivory for thousands of brass manillas and cowrie shells fuelled a boom in Igun Street plaques, and how Dutch scholar Olfert Dapper’s 1668 book praised Benin City’s thirty wide, clean streets.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain how the Oba controlled European trade on Benin’s own terms after Portuguese ships arrived in 1485.
  • Trace how swapping Benin pepper, cloth, and ivory for brass manillas and cowrie shells boosted Benin’s economy and art.
  • Evaluate Olfert Dapper’s famous 1668 description of Benin City’s wide clean avenues as historical evidence.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Published in Amsterdam in 1668, Olfert Dapper’s book amazed European readers because Stuart London still had narrow, muddy lanes, whereas Benin City boasted thirty straight streets 120 feet wide, mirror-smooth polished clay walls, and a palace as big as the Dutch town of Haarlem! Careful historians value Dapper’s Dutch merchant eyewitness diaries highly while cross-checking engravings drawn in Amsterdam against real Benin plaques.”
Enquiry 6: 1897 British Invasion, Looted Bronzes and Living Edo Heritage Today (1897–Present) TB p.9 • WB pp.13–14
Key Enquiry: Why did Britain invade Benin in 1897, and should looted bronzes return?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This synoptic finale examines why late-Victorian British trade ambitions over southern Nigeria’s palm oil and rubber collided with Oba Ovonramwen Nogbaisi (r. 1888–1914), sparking the January 1897 ambush and Britain’s February 1897 Punitive Expedition. Pupils investigate how over 4,000 royal brass plaques, heads, and carved tusks were seized and sold to over 160 museums worldwide, why those works shattered Victorian prejudices about African art, and how modern restitution returns (from Cambridge, London’s Horniman Museum, and Germany) meet vibrant Edo royal life and Igun Street casting today.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain why British Empire trade rivalry over palm oil and rubber led to the February 1897 invasion of Benin.
  • Describe what happened to Oba Ovonramwen, Benin’s palace, and over 4,000 sacred bronzes after February 1897.
  • Weigh arguments around modern museum restitution and celebrate living Edo royal culture in Nigeria today.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Historians and museum curators debating the restitution of the 1897 Benin Bronzes weigh powerful points because over four thousand artworks were seized by force from Benin’s sacred royal archive. Since 2021, Cambridge, London’s Horniman Museum, and Germany have begun rightful restitution to Nigeria, where Oba Ewuare II and working Igun Street casters keep Edo culture alive—while Nigerian-agreed loans can still share Benin’s genius worldwide!”
