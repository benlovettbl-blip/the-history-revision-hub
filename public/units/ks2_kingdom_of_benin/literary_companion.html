Sixteen Thousand Kilometres of Red Clay: Digging the Great Earthworks of Edo

The History Revision Hub Narrative Archive
Historical Context: Long before bulldozers or steel diggers existed, rainforest communities in southern Nigeria dug one of the largest earthwork networks on Earth! Step into the red-clay ditches around early Edo around AD 950.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Edo[EH-doh]
The people, language, and rainforest heartland of the Kingdom of Benin in southern Nigeria.
Ogiso[oh-GEE-soh]
Early rulers of Benin before the Obas, whose royal title meant 'Kings of the Sky'.
Iya[EE-yah]
Deep ditches and steep red-earth banks dug around Edo villages and Benin City to mark boundaries and keep people safe.
Laterite[LAT-er-yt]
Rich, iron-red tropical clay soil in West Africa that bakes rock-hard under the hot dry-season sun.

Morning mist hung low beneath giant silk-cotton trees in southern Nigeria around AD 950. Down in a deep trench stood dozens of Edo villagers with iron hoes and woven reed baskets. Each digger scooped heavy red laterite clay from the ditch floor. Up a chain of strong hands went every full basket!

At the top, builders tipped the damp earth onto a steep rising bank. Together they were shaping an Iya earthwork to guard their farmland and homes. Early kings called the Ogiso, or Kings of the Sky, ruled these growing forest towns.

Work stopped only when heavy tropical rains turned the red clay slick. Once dry sun returned, baked banks stood firm as stone walls! Steep ditches slowed rival raiders and kept wild forest animals away from yam fields.

Over centuries, linked village rings stretched sixteen thousand kilometres through the green forest. While Anglo-Saxon Britain built small timber forts, Edo communities shaped a wonder of the medieval world.

Royal Edo Praise-Chants and Proverbs of Oba Ewuare the Great (c. 1440)

Traditional Edo Court Oral Heritage (Recorded in Public-Domain Edo Chronicles)
Historical Context: Around 1440, warrior-king Oba Ewuare the Great rebuilt Benin City with broad straight streets, nine guarded gates, and gleaming red-clay palace courtyards! Read aloud how royal Edo singers praised their sacred king.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Oba[OH-bah]
The sacred king of Benin, honoured by Edo people as ruler of the land and living link to royal ancestors.
Ewuare the Great[eh-WAH-reh]
Powerful fifteenth-century Oba (r. c. 1440–1473) who rebuilt Edo as a grand walled capital and started the Igue festival.
Coral Beads[KOR-ul BEEDZ]
Polished red sea-coral beads worn in heavy crowns, collars, and tunics as symbols of the Oba's royal rank.
Leopard of the House[LEP-erd uv thuh HOWS]
Royal Edo praise-title comparing the wise, fearless Oba inside his palace to the noble leopard ruling the forest.

Around 1440, Oba Ewuare the Great rebuilt his capital with wide straight roads. Nine strong wooden gates guarded the deep inner moat! Tall palm-oil lamps glowed outside neat courtyard homes at dusk. At palace feasts, royal drummers beat deep rhythms on tall drums. Court singers chanted proud praise-lines for their sacred Oba.

"Hail the Leopard of the House, calm and bold in might!
Red coral crowns his brow and shines like morning light.
Forest trees bow very low when sudden thunder rolls.
Nine strong gates stand firm around the city Ewuare holds!"

Wise elders also shared old Edo proverbs about teamwork and good rule.

"One hand alone can never lift a heavy roof on high.
Broad streets stay neat when every house sweeps dry!
Great Ewuare guides his land with wisdom sharp and deep.
Tall brass birds on palace roofs watch Edo safely sleep!"

Spoken songs kept royal deeds alive across many proud generations.

3 Which real features of Oba Ewuare's rebuilt city of 1440 appear inside these verses?

Beeswax, Red Clay and Liquid Fire: Casting a Royal Plaque on Igun Street

The History Revision Hub Narrative Archive
Historical Context: Museums often call Benin's shining sculptures 'Benin Bronzes', yet most were cast from golden-yellow brass using soft beeswax and fiery furnaces! Watch master casters at work on Igun Street around 1550.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Igun Street[ee-GOON STREET]
The famous royal quarter in Benin City where the hereditary guild of master brass-casters has worked for centuries.
Lost-Wax Casting[LOST WAKS KAH-sting]
Sculpting a figure in beeswax, coating it in clay, melting the wax out, and pouring molten brass into the hollow space.
Brass[BRAHS]
A bright golden metal alloy of copper and zinc used for most royal Benin plaques and heads.
Crucible[KROO-sih-bul]
A thick heat-proof clay pot used inside a roaring charcoal furnace to melt solid brass into glowing liquid.

Sparks danced toward the thatch roof of a courtyard workshop on Igun Street around 1550. Only the Oba's royal guild was allowed to cast sacred brass for the palace! For weeks, a master sculptor had shaped warm yellow beeswax over a dry clay core. With a bone knife he carved every tiny coral bead and leopard spot.

Next, young helpers packed fine wet mud and red clay smoothly around the wax warrior. Once the mould dried hard in the sun, they set it near the fire. Melted wax trickled out through a narrow spout! Inside the baked clay shell, an exact hollow space waited.

"Pump the goat-skin bellows steady!" the master called.

Charcoal roared white-hot around a heavy clay crucible. With iron tongs, casters tilted glowing liquid brass into the mould. Days later, they cracked the cool clay open. After careful filing, a bright plaque shone ready for a tall palace pillar!

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 Why is this clever West African metalworking method called 'lost-wax' casting?
2 What happened to the outer clay mould at the very end, and what did that mean about every single Benin plaque?
3 Who had permission to commission brass plaques and royal heads from the guild on Igun Street?

Enquiry 4 Eyewitness Voice Sixteenth-Century Court Eyewitness

Kneeling Before the Sacred Oba: Richard Eden's Account of Captain Windham in Benin (1553)

Richard Eden (Recording Captain Thomas Windham's 1553 Voyage to Benin, Public Domain)
Historical Context: In 1553—the very year young King Edward VI died in Tudor England—English sailors led by Captain Thomas Windham sailed up the Benin River to buy pepper! Read what amazed them most inside the Oba's grand palace hall.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Iyoba[ee-YOH-bah]
Title of the Queen Mother of Benin, first created by Oba Esigie for his wise mother Idia with her own palace at Uselu.
Ivory[EYE-vuh-ree]
Smooth creamy-white elephant tusk carved by royal Benin guild artists into sacred masks, horns, and altar tusks.
Igue Festival[ee-GWAY FES-tih-vul]
Joyful annual royal Benin thanksgiving ceremony renewing the sacred strength of the Oba and blessing the whole kingdom.
Portuguese[por-chuh-GEEZ]
People and language of Portugal—the first Europeans to trade with Benin from 1485, spoken fluently by Benin's mid-1500s Oba!

In 1553, our English ship anchored in the warm Benin River to buy pepper. Guides led Captain Windham and our crew into the grand walled palace of Benin City. Inside a wide pillared court sat the sacred Oba in bright red coral robes!

Even the highest Benin chiefs knelt low before their king. None dared look straight into his face until he spoke! Imagine our shock when the king greeted us in clear Portuguese. He had learned to read and speak that tongue as a young boy!

At court shone the proud memory of Iyoba Idia, Benin's wise first Queen Mother. Her brave counsel helped her son win great wars. On his hip hung a pale carved ivory mask of her calm face, crowned with tiny sea traders.

Within days, royal stores filled eighty chests with spicy Benin pepper for our ship. Few courts in Tudor Europe matched such calm order.

2 How did even the highest Benin chiefs show sacred respect when entering the Oba's palace courtyard?
3 Who was Iyoba Idia, and why was a carved ivory mask of her face worn at sacred court ceremonies?

Description of Africa: The Wide Clean Streets of Great Benin (1668)

Olfert Dapper (Published in Amsterdam, 1668; English Edition 1670, Public Domain)
Historical Context: In 1668—just two years after the Great Fire burned narrow, crowded timber streets in Stuart London—Dutch writer Olfert Dapper published eyewitness reports of Benin City! Read why European readers gasped in admiration.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Olfert Dapper[OL-fert DAP-er]
Dutch writer whose famous 1668 book 'Description of Africa' gathered merchant reports praising Benin City's size and cleanliness.
Manillas[muh-NIL-uz]
Heavy horseshoe-shaped bracelets of brass and copper brought on European ships to trade for Benin pepper, cloth, and ivory.
Cowrie Shells[KOW-ree SHELZ]
Small glossy white sea shells used across West Africa as everyday money in bustling food and cloth markets.
Haarlem[HAR-lem]
A wealthy Dutch city near Amsterdam that Dapper compared with Benin City so European readers could picture its grand avenues.

In 1668, Dutch writer Olfert Dapper shared lively reports from West African trade voyages. Ships swapped brass manillas and white cowrie shells for Benin cloth and spicy pepper! Dapper compared royal Benin City proudly with rich Dutch towns like Haarlem.

"The city of Benin has thirty very straight, broad streets. Each main road measures about one hundred and twenty feet wide! Many neat side lanes branch off them, almost as broad.

The King's palace takes up as much ground as the whole town of Haarlem. A fine tall wall runs right round it. Inside stand grand courtyards with long covered walkways as big as a royal market hall!

Families wash and rub their smooth red-clay walls until they shine like a bright mirror. Houses stand in fine order, for every home sweeps its own street clean."

Back then in 1668, Stuart London still had cramped, muddy lanes with no wide avenues!

3 Why did Dapper compare the Oba's palace to the Dutch town of Haarlem?

Enquiry 6 Eyewitness Voice 1897 Eyewitness & Modern Heritage

Palace Stores Full of Bronzes: Eyewitness to February 1897 and Modern Return

Dr Felix Roth & Commander Reginald Bacon (1897 Eyewitness Diaries, Public Domain)
Historical Context: In February 1897, twelve hundred British troops invaded Benin City, exiled Oba Ovonramwen, and stripped thousands of sacred brass plaques and carved tusks from the palace! Read what eyewitnesses saw—and how Edo heritage lives on today.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Oba Ovonramwen[oh-von-RAHM-wen]
Oba of Benin (r. 1888–1914) who defended Benin's palm-oil trade independence before British troops invaded and exiled him in 1897.
Punitive Expedition[PYOO-nih-tiv eks-puh-DISH-un]
The February 1897 British military invasion that captured Benin City, burned the palace, and seized royal treasures.
Looting[LOO-ting]
Taking sacred art, treasures, or property by military force during war—as happened to over 4,000 Benin Bronzes in 1897.
Restitution[res-tih-TYOO-shun]
Rightfully returning seized cultural treasures from foreign museums back to their homeland and people.

In February 1897, British troops marched into Benin City to grab rich palm-oil and rubber trade. Soldiers sent Oba Ovonramwen away from his people into sad exile. Soon a fierce fire swept across the tall palace roofs! Inside the royal storehouses, doctor Felix Roth gasped at Benin's sacred treasures.

"Under thick dust lay hundreds of cast bronze plaques of fine old skill! In the courts stood tall carved tusks above brass heads and royal leopards. Every single piece showed rare craft that amazed our eyes."

Troops packed more than four thousand plaques, tusks, and coral works into heavy crates for London! Museums across Europe and America bought the looted art to pay for the Punitive Expedition.

Yet Benin's proud story did not end in 1897! Today a crowned Oba still leads Edo life in Nigeria, while Igun Street casters pour bright brass. Museums from Cambridge to Berlin have begun restitution to Nigeria!
