WALK-IN LESSON PLANS • ENQUIRIES 1 & 2 NON-SPECIALIST PRIMARY TEACHER GUIDE
Enquiry 1: After the Romans Left — Why Did Angles, Saxons & Jutes Settle in Britain? (c. AD 410–600) Textbook p.4 • Workbook pp.3–4
Key Enquiry: Who were the Angles, Saxons, and Jutes, and why did they cross the North Sea to settle in Britain after AD 410?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This opening enquiry bridges directly from AD 410 in KS2 Romans to the post-Roman migration of Angles, Saxons, and Jutes across the North Sea (c. AD 410–600). Pupils distinguish push reasons (flooding coastal marshes in Jutland, Angeln, and Saxony) from pull reasons (fertile British farmland after Roman legions withdrew), step inside reconstructed timber halls and wattle-and-daub weaving workshops at West Stow, and trace the rise of the Heptarchy alongside surviving Old English place-names (-ham, -ton, -ford) and pagan weekday names (Tiw, Woden, Thunor, Frige).
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain the push and pull reasons why Angles, Saxons, and Jutes crossed the North Sea after AD 410.
  • Describe village craftwork, social roles (cyning and ceorls), and feasting inside a timber mead hall.
  • Identify the seven kingdoms of the Heptarchy and spot Old English place-names and weekday gods today.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “The Angles, Saxons, and Jutes were North Sea farming peoples from modern Denmark and northern Germany who transformed post-Roman Britain by creating villages, kingdoms, and the roots of our spoken language. Over time their settlements grew into the seven kingdoms of the Heptarchy — Northumbria, Mercia, East Anglia, Essex, Kent, Sussex, and Wessex — while "Englaland" ("land of the Angles") became England itself. Today, their Old English speech still echoes across our map in "-ham" (Birmingham), "-ton" (Brighton), and "-ford" (Oxford) towns, and in four weekdays named after Tiw, Woden, Thunor, and Frige!”
Enquiry 2: Ship Burials & Monasteries — What Do Sutton Hoo & Christian Monks Reveal? (c. AD 597–750) Textbook p.5 • Workbook pp.5–6
Key Enquiry: What does the Sutton Hoo ship burial reveal about Anglo-Saxon kings, and how did Christianity change Britain?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This enquiry develops archaeological inference and evidential thinking across c. AD 597–750. Pupils investigate Edith Pretty and Basil Brown’s 1939 excavation of Mound 1 at Sutton Hoo (likely King Raedwald of East Anglia, c. AD 625), inferring royal power, international trade (Sri Lankan garnets, Byzantine silver, Frankish coins), and religious transition from pagan grave goods alongside Christian spoons. They then trace the two missionary routes — Augustine from Rome to Canterbury (AD 597) and Aidan from Iona to Lindisfarne (AD 635) — culminating in Abbess Hilda at Whitby, illuminated vellum manuscripts, and Venerable Bede at Jarrow (AD 731).
Learning Objectives:
  • Infer what the Mound 1 ship burial at Sutton Hoo reveals about Anglo-Saxon kingship, craft skill, and global trade.
  • Compare the two missionary paths that spread Christianity across Anglo-Saxon kingdoms from AD 597 to AD 635.
  • Explain how monasteries led by figures like Abbess Hilda and Venerable Bede transformed literacy, art, and history-writing.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Historians need both buried archaeological treasures like Sutton Hoo and monastic books like Bede’s history because each source fills in the other’s blind spots! Written manuscripts like Venerable Bede’s AD 731 History of the English Church and People give us exact names, AD dates, and stories of missionaries like Augustine, Aidan, and Abbess Hilda. Yet Bede never mentioned the Mound 1 ship burial! Only Basil Brown’s careful 1939 excavation of iron rivets, the dragon-browed helmet, and Sri Lankan garnets proved just how dazzling pagan royal craftsmanship and 4,000-mile trade networks really were.”

Enquiry 3: Fury of the Northmen — Why Did Vikings Raid Lindisfarne & Conquer Kingdoms? (AD 793–878) Textbook p.6 • Workbook pp.7–8
Key Enquiry: Why did Viking raiders cross the sea from Scandinavia in AD 793, and how did the Great Heathen Army nearly conquer all England?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This enquiry examines the start of the Viking Age in Britain from the shocking 8 June AD 793 attack on Lindisfarne to the Great Heathen Army’s near-total conquest of the Anglo-Saxon Heptarchy by AD 877. Pupils analyse why wealthy coastal monasteries were soft targets, evaluate monastic bias in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle and Alcuin’s letters, dissect clinker-built longship engineering (overlapping oak strakes, tarred wool, square sail + oars, 1-metre shallow draught), and trace why Scandinavian land hunger and political ambition turned hit-and-run summer raids into permanent conquest at Repton and Jorvik (York).
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain why coastal monasteries like Holy Island (AD 793) were prime targets and why Christian writers reacted with horror.
  • Analyse how clinker-built longship technology enabled both open-ocean crossings and inland river strikes.
  • Explain why summer raiding changed in AD 865 into the Great Heathen Army’s conquest of Northumbria, East Anglia, and Mercia.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Vikings sailed west from Scandinavia for wealth and farmland, and between AD 793 and AD 877 their attacks transformed from quick summer raids into permanent conquest. Steep mountains in Norway and sandy soils in Denmark created a shortage of fertile farmland, while ambitious warlords craved silver treasure and trade routes. At first, in AD 793, small crews raided soft coastal monasteries like Lindisfarne in summer and sailed home. In AD 865, however, the massive Great Heathen Army under Ivar the Boneless, Halfdan, and Guthrum built winter camps like Repton, captured York as Jorvik, and conquered East Anglia and Mercia until only Wessex stood!”
Enquiry 4: Alfred the Great, Burhs & the Danelaw — How Did Wessex Survive? (AD 871–899) Textbook p.7 • Workbook pp.9–10
Key Enquiry: How did King Alfred save Wessex in AD 878, and why do historians call him 'Alfred the Great'?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This enquiry examines why King Alfred of Wessex (reigned AD 871–899) is the sole monarch in British history accorded the epithet "the Great." Pupils trace the dramatic crisis of AD 878 — from Guthrum's surprise Twelfth Night seizure of Chippenham and Alfred's guerrilla refuge in the Somerset Levels at Athelney, to the rallying of the West Saxon fyrd at Egbert's Stone and decisive shield-wall victory at Edington. Crucially, pupils evaluate historical significance beyond the battlefield: Alfred's pragmatic peacemaking (Guthrum's Christian baptism and the Watling Street treaty establishing the Danelaw), his systematic civil-defence engineering (33 fortified burhs placed within 20 miles of every farm, backed by 60-oar warships), and his cultural renaissance (Old English translations, the Alfred Jewel aestel, and the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle).
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain how King Alfred survived the midwinter crisis of AD 878 at Athelney and defeated Guthrum at the Battle of Edington.
  • Describe how the Treaty of Alfred and Guthrum divided England along Watling Street between Anglo-Saxon territory and the Danelaw.
  • Evaluate why historians call Alfred "the Great" by weighing his 33 fortified burhs, warships, Old English translations, and the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Historians call King Alfred of Wessex 'Alfred the Great' because he combined courageous leadership in crisis, wise peacemaking, clever town-planning, and a passion for education. In AD 878, when Northumbria, East Anglia, and Mercia had all fallen, Alfred refused to surrender at Athelney and defeated Guthrum at Edington. Rather than fighting endlessly, he baptised Guthrum and agreed the Danelaw border along Watling Street. Even more remarkably, he protected every family within 20 miles of 33 walled burhs, built taller warships, translated Latin books into Old English with pointers like the Alfred Jewel, and launched the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle — though historians remember Wessex scribes proudly praised their own king!”

Enquiry 5: Life in Jorvik & the Danelaw — Were the Vikings Only Fierce Raiders? (c. AD 876–1000) Textbook p.8 • Workbook pp.11–12
Key Enquiry: What does archaeology from Jorvik (York) reveal about everyday Viking settlers, traders, and craftworkers?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This enquiry teaches pupils how archaeological evidence challenges one-sided written stereotypes. Because early Viking raiders were non-literate pagans attacking literate Christian monasteries, surviving Latin and Old English chronicles naturally portrayed Norse arrivals solely as violent destroyers. Excavations by York Archaeological Trust at 16–22 Coppergate (1976–1981) transformed historical understanding: anaerobic waterlogged peat preserved timber workshops and 40,000 organic finds from Jorvik. Pupils investigate specialist craft production, long-distance trade networks stretching from Red Sea cowrie shells and Abbasid/Samanid dirhams (Baghdad and Samarkand) to North Atlantic knarr voyages (Iceland, Greenland, Vinland), Danelaw social structure (karls, thralls, and the legal rights of key-bearing Viking women), outdoor Thing law-assemblies, and Old Norse linguistic survival.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain how waterlogged archaeology at Coppergate in Jorvik (York) challenges the stereotype that Vikings were only violent raiders.
  • Trace the global reach of Viking merchants and explorers from Baghdad and Byzantium to Vinland in North America.
  • Describe everyday family life, women's legal rights, outdoor Thing assemblies, and Old Norse words in modern English.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “I strongly disagree that the Vikings were ONLY violent raiders, even though early attacks on monasteries like Lindisfarne in AD 793 were terrifying and keeping enslaved thralls on farms was cruel. Once families settled across the Danelaw, excavations beneath Coppergate in Jorvik revealed 40,000 clues from skilled wood-turners, comb-makers, and international merchants trading from Baghdad to Vinland. Viking women owned land and carried household keys, outdoor Thing assemblies settled laws fairly, and monuments like the Gosforth Cross blended Norse and Christian art. Even today, Danelaw towns ending in -by and everyday Old Norse words like sky, window, egg, knife, husband, and Thursday prove their lasting contribution!”
Enquiry 6: Aethelflaed, Athelstan, Cnut & 1066 — Who Won the Struggle for England? (AD 900–1066) Textbook p.9 • Workbook pp.13–14
Key Enquiry: How did Anglo-Saxon and Viking rulers forge one united Kingdom of England — and why did it all change in 1066?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This synoptic concluding enquiry resolves the overarching statutory KS2 theme: "the Viking and Anglo-Saxon struggle for the Kingdom of England to the time of Edward the Confessor" (extending cleanly through the twin autumn battles of 1066). Pupils trace three major turning points: (1) how Alfred's daughter Aethelflaed ("Lady of the Mercians") and nephew King Athelstan (York AD 927; Brunanburh AD 937) forged the first united Kingdom of England; (2) how renewed Danish raids, Aethelred's failed Danegeld payments, King Cnut's peaceful North Sea Empire (AD 1016–1035), and Edward the Confessor's reign blended Anglo-Saxon and Danish rule; and (3) how Edward's childless death in January 1066 sparked a three-way succession crisis between Harold Godwinson, Harald Hardrada (defeated at Stamford Bridge on 25 September, ending the Viking Age), and Duke William of Normandy (victorious at Hastings on 14 October 1066).
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain how Aethelflaed ("Lady of the Mercians") and King Athelstan united Anglo-Saxon and Viking lands into one Kingdom of England by AD 927–937.
  • Contrast King Aethelred's failed Danegeld silver payments with King Cnut's peaceful North Sea Empire and Edward the Confessor's reign.
  • Analyse why 1066 was a double turning point at Stamford Bridge (25 September) and Hastings (14 October).
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Looking across the whole story from AD 410 to 1066, Duke William's Normans won the military battle for the crown on 14 October 1066 at Hastings — helped enormously because King Harold had marched north nineteen days earlier to defeat Harald Hardrada at Stamford Bridge! Yet in everyday life, BOTH Anglo-Saxons and Vikings shaped the country William inherited. Anglo-Saxons gave "Angle-land" its name, Christian monasteries, Alfred's burh boroughs, Athelstan's united kingdom, and Westminster Abbey, while Viking settlers and King Cnut wove Danelaw farms, -by towns, and Old Norse words into English forever. The Normans won the throne in 1066, but Anglo-Saxons and Vikings built England itself!”
