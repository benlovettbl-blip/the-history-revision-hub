KEY ENQUIRY “Why did border tensions spiral towards another war by 1967?”
STAGE 1 The 1964 Cairo Conference & Founding of the PLO Jan 1964
“Why did Arab leaders meet in Cairo and establish the PLO in January 1964?”

In January 1964, President Nasser convened the Cairo Conference (the First Arab League Summit), bringing Arab monarchs and heads of state together to counter Israel’s National Water Carrier extracting water from the River Jordan. Nasser urged united Arab opposition while cautioning that Arab conventional armies remained unready for immediate open war against Israel. To channel Palestinian resistance under Arab supervision, the Cairo Conference backed a dedicated political body. In May 1964, a congress in Jordanian-ruled East Jerusalem led by Ahmad Shukeiri founded the [Key Organisation: PLO] (Palestine Liberation Organisation), adopting the Palestinian National Charter declaring Israel’s establishment "null and void" and giving displaced refugees a distinct national voice.

STAGE 2 The Dispute Over the River Jordan Waterways 1964–1966
“How did rival River Jordan water plans trigger cross-border military strikes?”

Fresh water was a critical strategic asset in the arid Middle East. In 1964, Israel completed its National Water Carrier, a major system of pipelines, canals, and pumping stations designed to pump fresh water from the Sea of Galilee to irrigate agricultural settlements and populate the southern Negev desert, dramatically strengthening the country’s agricultural output and economic security. Arab states condemned the project as an illegal diversion of shared international waters. In response, Syria and Jordan launched the Headwater Diversion Plan to redirect the River Jordan's northern tributaries away from Israeli territory. Between 1965 and 1966, Israeli tank artillery and air strikes bombed Syrian earth-moving construction equipment, destroying the diversion channels and making armed border skirmishes a routine occurrence.

Rising Tensions: Water & Border Raids — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 Syrian Support for Fatah Border Raids 1965–1966
“Why did Fatah launch raids into Israel, and how did Syria’s regime back them?”

Frustrated by conservative Arab diplomacy, younger Palestinian militants led by Yasser Arafat formed Fatah in Kuwait to wage independent revolutionary warfare. Fatah rejected reliance on Arab governments, arguing that direct guerrilla attacks inside Israel would ignite a wider Arab-Israeli conflict. In January 1965, Fatah carried out its first sabotage raid, attempting to dynamite an Israeli water canal pumping facility. In 1966, a radical military regime seized power in Damascus, offering Fatah advanced weapons, training facilities, and operational sanctuary. From heavily fortified artillery bunkers atop the Golan Heights, Syrian gunners routinely shelled Israeli tractors and farming kibbutzim in the Hula Valley below, prompting Israeli military leaders to warn that Syria faced devastating military retaliation if guerrilla attacks continued.

STAGE 4 The Israeli Raid on Samu 13 Nov 1966
“Why did Israel’s reprisal raid on Samu destabilise King Hussein in Jordan?”

In November 1966, an underground Fatah landmine killed three Israeli paratroopers patrolling near the southern border. On 13 November 1966, Israeli commanders launched a massive daylight reprisal strike against the village of As-Samu in the Jordanian-administered West Bank, deploying 4,000 soldiers supported by armored tanks and combat aircraft to punish the local population. The assault escalated into intense fighting with the Jordanian army, killing 15 Jordanian soldiers and flattening over 100 village houses. The raid deeply humiliated Jordan’s pro-Western monarch, King Hussein, who had secretly maintained backchannel dialogue with Israel. Widespread riots erupted across West Bank towns against the Hashemite monarchy, severely destabilizing the regime and forcing Hussein to harden his public rhetoric against Israel to survive politically.

STAGE 5 The Aerial Battle of 7 April 1967 7 April 1967
“How did the 7 April 1967 air battle humiliate Syria and escalate tension?”

On 7 April 1967, long-simmering border tensions reached a dangerous crisis when an Israeli armored tractor ploughed disputed agricultural fields in the demilitarized zone near the Syrian border. Syrian artillery atop the Golan Heights opened fire on Israeli farmers, prompting the Israeli Air Force to dispatch French-built Mirage jets to silence Syrian gun positions. Syrian Soviet-built MiG-21 fighters scrambled to intercept the Israeli aircraft, resulting in a dramatic aerial dogfight over the Sea of Galilee. Highly trained Israeli pilots shot down six Syrian MiGs without suffering a single loss, chasing retreating Syrian planes over Damascus. This public humiliation pushed Syrian leaders to demand that Egypt honor their mutual defence pact, accelerating the slide toward war.

KEY ENQUIRY “How did the May 1967 crisis end in a six-day Israeli victory?”
STAGE 1 Soviet Warning, UNEF Expelled & Straits of Tiran Closed 13–22 May 1967
“Why did false Soviet reports lead Nasser to expel UNEF and close Tiran?”

On 13 May 1967, the Soviet Union (USSR) passed a false intelligence warning to Cairo alleging that Israel had massed ten to twelve brigades on Syria’s border for an imminent invasion. Although UN observers confirmed no buildup existed, Nasser could not look weak as champion of Pan-Arabism: on 14–15 May he marched 100,000 Egyptian troops and 1,000 tanks into the Sinai Peninsula and demanded the withdrawal of United Nations Emergency Force (UNEF) peacekeepers on 16 May. When UN Secretary-General U Thant withdrew UNEF without Security Council debate, Egyptian forces occupied Sharm el-Sheikh overlooking the Gulf of Aqaba. On 22 May 1967, Nasser closed the Straits of Tiran to all Israeli shipping and vital oil tankers bound for Eilat—crossing the explicit red line Israel had warned since 1957 would constitute an act of war.

STAGE 2 The Jordan Defence Pact & National Unity Cabinet 30 May–4 June 1967
“Why did the Jordan Pact and failed US diplomacy convince Israel to strike?”

On 30 May 1967, Jordan’s King Hussein flew to Cairo and signed a mutual Joint Defence Pact with his former rival Nasser, placing Jordan’s armed forces under Egyptian command as Iraqi troops entered Jordan. Meanwhile, US President Lyndon B. Johnson warned Israel not to fire first, yet failed to assemble an international naval convoy to reopen the Straits of Tiran. Encircled by hostile armies on three fronts and convinced American diplomacy could not lift the naval blockade, Israeli civilians dug trenches in public city parks amid deep fear of destruction. On 1 June, Prime Minister Levi Eshkol formed a national unity cabinet, appointing war hero Moshe Dayan Minister of Defence alongside Chief of Staff Yitzhak Rabin to prepare a pre-emptive strike.

The Six-Day War, May–June 1967 — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 Operation Focus: Pre-Emptive Air Supremacy 5 June 1967
“How did Operation Focus win total air supremacy within three hours on 5 June?”

At 07:45 on 5 June 1967, the Israeli Air Force launched the pre-emptive air strike (Operation Focus, or Mivtza Moked) against Egyptian military airfields. Flying beneath radar at treetop height across the Mediterranean under strict radio silence, nearly 200 Israeli fighter jets struck just as Egyptian morning patrols landed and senior commanders sat stuck in Cairo traffic. Dropping specialised runway-cratering bombs first to trap enemy aircraft on the tarmac, Israeli waves strafed parked bombers and MiG fighters with cannon fire. Within three hours, the strike wiped out the Egyptian Air Force, destroying over 300 Egyptian aircraft on the ground—and crippling Jordanian and Syrian airbases that afternoon—to win total air supremacy across every front.

STAGE 4 Sinai Blitzkrieg & Capture of East Jerusalem 5–8 June 1967
“How did Israeli forces capture Gaza, East Jerusalem, and the West Bank?”

Shielded by total air supremacy between 5 and 8 June 1967, Israeli armoured divisions led by Generals Israel Tal, Avraham Yoffe, and Ariel Sharon smashed through Egyptian fortifications in Gaza and Abu Ageila. Retreating Egyptian columns caught in the open desert were devastated from the air at the Mitla Pass, allowing Israeli tanks to reach the Suez Canal and Sharm el-Sheikh by 8 June. Meanwhile on 5 June, misled by false Cairo radio broadcasts claiming Egyptian victories, King Hussein’s artillery shelled West Jerusalem despite Israeli appeals to stay out. Israeli brigades counter-attacked into the West Bank, and on 7 June 1967 paratroopers stormed Jerusalem’s Old City to capture East Jerusalem and the Western Wall, driving Jordanian forces back across the River Jordan.

STAGE 5 Storming the Golan Heights & Six-Day Victory 9–10 June 1967
“How did storming the Golan Heights complete Israel’s victory by 10 June?”

With Egypt and Jordan defeated, Defence Minister Moshe Dayan ordered a final northern offensive on 9 June 1967 against the fortified Syrian Golan Heights, from which Syrian artillery had shelled northern Israeli villages for years. Backed by relentless air strikes, Israeli infantry and armoured bulldozers fought uphill through concrete bunkers to capture Quneitra before a UN ceasefire took effect on 10 June 1967. In just six days, Israel shattered three Arab armies and tripled the territory under its control—occupying the Sinai Peninsula, Gaza Strip, West Bank, East Jerusalem, and Golan Heights. While giving Israel deep strategic buffers, victory placed over one million Palestinians under military occupation and humiliated Arab regimes, transforming the Middle East conflict.

KEY ENQUIRY “Why did Israel’s 1967 victory make lasting peace even harder?”
STAGE 1 The Capture of East Jerusalem June 1967
“How did annexing East Jerusalem and new refugees transform the conflict?”

Victory in June 1967 tripled the territory under Israeli control—occupying Sinai, Gaza, the West Bank, and the Golan Heights—while displacing 300,000 refugees into neighbouring Jordan and Syria. On 7 June 1967, Israeli paratroopers under Colonel Motta Gur stormed into Jerusalem’s Old City, allowing Jewish worshippers to pray freely at the Western Wall for the first time since 1948. Weeks later, the Knesset formally annexed East Jerusalem, expanding municipal boundaries and declaring a united Jerusalem Israel’s eternal capital while placing over one million Palestinians across the West Bank and Gaza under military rule. However, the United Nations condemned the annexation as illegal under international law, insisting Jerusalem’s status required direct bilateral negotiations.

STAGE 2 The Khartoum Summit & The "Three No’s" 1 Sept 1967
“Why did Arab leaders adopt the "Three Noes" at the Khartoum Summit in 1967?”

Stunned by their catastrophic territorial losses, eight Arab heads of state gathered in Sudan for the Khartoum Arab League Summit in August 1967. While moderates like Jordan's King Hussein sought diplomatic avenues to recover lost territory, hardline regimes in Syria and Algeria insisted on total military defiance and an economic oil embargo against Israel’s Western allies. On 1 September 1967, the summit issued the famous Khartoum Resolution, adopting the uncompromising Three Noes: "No peace with Israel, no recognition of Israel, no negotiations with Israel." This unyielding stance hardened Israeli public opinion, convincing political leaders that Arab states remained committed to Israel’s destruction and ending immediate hopes of trading captured land for permanent peace.

Occupied Territories & UN Resolution 242 — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 UN Resolution 242: "Land for Peace" 22 Nov 1967
“How did UN Resolution 242 define "Land for Peace", and why was it disputed?”

On 22 November 1967, the United Nations Security Council unanimously adopted Resolution 242, drafted by British Ambassador Lord Caradon. The resolution established the cornerstone principle of modern Middle Eastern diplomacy: "Land for Peace." It linked Israeli military withdrawal from captured lands to the right of every regional state to live in peace within secure and recognized boundaries. However, the resolution contained deliberate wording ambiguities: the English text called for withdrawal from "territories occupied" rather than all territories, allowing Israel to argue for defensible border adjustments. Furthermore, the resolution referred to Palestinian Arabs merely as a "refugee problem," ignoring their national rights to self-determination and prompting the PLO to reject the diplomatic framework completely.

STAGE 4 The Bar-Lev Line on the Suez Canal 1968–1970
“How did the blocked Suez Canal and Bar-Lev Line fuel the War of Attrition?”

Along the Suez Canal—which remained blocked to all international shipping by sunken vessels and naval mines from June 1967—Israeli Chief of Staff Haim Bar-Lev constructed a heavily fortified defensive barrier between 1968 and 1969. The Bar-Lev Line featured a 20-metre high artificial sand rampart backed by concrete bunkers, minefields, and observation posts along the east bank. This closed waterway became the fierce battleground of the War of Attrition (1969–1970), as Nasser launched sustained artillery bombardments and commando raids across the canal to wear down Israeli resolve and prevent the ceasefire line becoming permanent. Holding the line bred a false sense of Israeli invincibility that proved costly in October 1973.

STAGE 5 The Beginning of Israeli Settlements 1967–1977
“How did the Allon Plan and West Bank settlements obstruct a two-state deal?”

Following the 1967 conquest, Israel’s consolidation of control of the occupied territories began under Deputy Prime Minister Yigal Allon, whose Allon Plan proposed agricultural settlements along the Jordan Valley as military security buffers while returning Arab-populated central hilltops to Jordanian control. Labour initially authorised settlements strictly on perimeter security grounds to protect Israel’s narrow coastal heartland. However, religious-nationalist movements like Gush Emunim soon began establishing ideological Israeli settlements deep in the biblical heartland of the West Bank (Judea and Samaria). When Menachem Begin's Likud party took power in 1977, state subsidies expanded settlements dramatically, embedding hundreds of thousands of Jewish residents into occupied territories and creating severe physical obstacles to any viable two-state solution.

KEY ENQUIRY “Why did Palestinian groups turn to international violence, and did it work?”
STAGE 1 The Battle of Karameh March 1968–1969
“How did the Battle of Karameh boost Fatah and bring Arafat to lead the PLO?”

On 21 March 1968, Israeli armoured forces launched a major punitive cross-border raid across the River Jordan to destroy Yasser Arafat’s Fatah guerrilla stronghold at the border town of Karameh. Tipped off by intelligence, Palestinian fighters chose to stand their ground alongside Jordanian army artillery units in fierce close-quarters combat throughout the day. Although Israeli troops demolished the camp, the IDF suffered 28 soldiers killed and several disabled tanks. Celebrating the Battle of Karameh as a heroic moral triumph over Israeli armour, Yasser Arafat inspired thousands of new volunteers to join Fatah and was elected Chairman of the PLO in February 1969, placing armed guerrilla factions in charge of Palestinian politics.

STAGE 2 The PFLP Airplane Hijacks at Dawson’s Field 6 Sept 1970
“Why did the PFLP hijack four airliners to Dawson’s Field in September 1970?”

Radical Marxist factions challenged Arafat’s moderate dominance of the Palestinian national movement during the late 1960s. Led by Dr George Habash, the [Key Organisation: PFLP] (Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine) pioneered international airplane hijacks to publicise the Palestinian struggle globally and compel Western governments to release imprisoned guerrilla fighters from European and Israeli jails. On 6 September 1970, in the PFLP airplane hijacks of 1970, operatives including Leila Khaled targeted four Western passenger airliners simultaneously, forcing three aircraft to land at Dawson’s Field in Jordan. After evacuating over 300 civilian hostages, militants blew up the empty airliners before global television cameras, humiliating King Hussein and defying Jordanian royal control.

The PLO, Black September & Munich — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 Black September (Civil War in Jordan) Sept 1970–1971
“Why did King Hussein expel the PLO in Black September, and where did it move?”

By mid-1970, armed Palestinian militias operated as an autonomous state-within-a-state across Jordan, establishing armed checkpoints, collecting illegal taxes, and openly challenging royal authority in the capital of Amman. Outraged by the Dawson's Field spectacle and fearing for his throne, Jordan's King Hussein ordered his loyal Bedouin army to crush and disarm the Palestinian guerrilla organizations on 16 September 1970. The brutal civil war, known to Palestinians as Black September, claimed several thousand lives as Jordanian tank artillery shelled refugee camps across Amman. A Syrian armoured intervention was decisively repulsed, resulting in the complete expulsion of the PLO from Jordan (begun in 1970 and completed by mid-1971) as the PLO relocated its headquarters and fighters to southern Lebanon and Beirut.

STAGE 4 The Munich Olympics Massacre 5–6 Sept 1972
“What happened during the Black September attack at the 1972 Munich Olympics?”

On 5 September 1972, eight gunmen from the militant Palestinian Black September organisation scaled the perimeter fence of the Olympic Village at the Munich Olympics in West Germany. Storming the Israeli team quarters, they killed two Israelis who resisted and took nine Israeli athletes and coaches hostage, demanding the release of over 200 Palestinian prisoners held in Israel. When West German authorities refused Israeli commando assistance, a bungled police rescue attempt at Fürstenfeldbruck airfield ended in disaster as all nine hostages died—leaving 11 Israeli athletes and coaches murdered in total. Watched live on television by hundreds of millions worldwide, the massacre shocked global opinion and transformed international aviation and Olympic security.

STAGE 5 Israeli Retaliation (Operation Wrath of God) 1972–1973
“How did Israel retaliate, and how did Munich shape attitudes to Palestine?”

Outraged by the Munich massacre, Israeli Prime Minister Golda Meir ordered air strikes against PLO camps in Lebanon and Syria and authorised Mossad’s covert Operation Wrath of God (Mivtza Za'am Ha'El). Israeli assassination squads tracked and killed Black September planners across Europe and Beirut, though shooting an innocent waiter in Lillehammer, Norway in 1973 drew international criticism. Globally, Munich and international terrorism had a sharp dual effect on attitudes toward the Palestine issue. Western governments and public opinion fiercely condemned the killings and refused diplomatic contact with the PLO, yet worldwide television coverage forced world leaders to recognise that Palestinian statelessness could no longer be ignored as merely a refugee problem.

KEY ENQUIRY “How did war and oil in 1973 finally break the diplomatic deadlock?”
STAGE 1 The War of Attrition 1969–Aug 1970
“Why did Nasser wage the War of Attrition, and how did fighting end in 1970?”

In March 1969, Egyptian President Nasser launched the War of Attrition, a grinding military conflict fought along the Suez Canal that lasted until August 1970. Nasser sought to exploit Israel’s acute vulnerability to military casualties, preventing the 1967 ceasefire line from solidifying into an accepted permanent border and pressuring international superpowers to force an Israeli withdrawal from occupied Sinai. Israeli soldiers endured relentless heavy artillery shellfire inside reinforced concrete bunkers along the Bar-Lev Line, while Israeli jets launched deep retaliatory bombing raids into the Egyptian heartland. When the Soviet Union (USSR) deployed advanced surface-to-air missiles and combat pilots to defend Cairo, the acute danger of direct superpower confrontation prompted both nations to accept an American-brokered ceasefire in August 1970.

STAGE 2 Sadat Expels Soviet Advisers 1971–July 1972
“Why did Sadat expel Soviet advisers in 1972 and plan a limited war in Sinai?”

Following Nasser’s sudden death in September 1970, new Egyptian President Anwar Sadat offered in February 1971 to reopen the Suez Canal if Israel withdrew its forces from Sinai, but Prime Minister Golda Meir and Henry Kissinger dismissed his peace initiative. Convinced Israel would never negotiate while confident in its military superiority, Sadat resolved to wage a limited war to break the diplomatic freeze. Frustrated by Moscow’s cautious détente policy and refusal to supply offensive bombers, Sadat stunned the world in July 1972 by expelling 15,000 Soviet military advisers from Egypt. This bold move signalled Cairo’s independence to Washington while Sadat secretly forged a coordinated war plan with Syria armed with Soviet anti-tank and surface-to-air missiles.

The War of Attrition & Yom Kippur War — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 The Surprise Attack on Yom Kippur 6 Oct 1973
“How did Egypt and Syria achieve surprise on Yom Kippur and cross the canal?”

At 14:00 on 6 October 1973, Egypt and Syria launched a massive coordinated surprise offensive against Israeli positions. The attack fell on Yom Kippur, the holiest solemn fast in Judaism, and during the Muslim sacred month of Ramadan. With Israel largely demobilized for the religious holiday, Israeli military intelligence misread large-scale Arab troop buildups as routine annual training exercises. In a dramatic surprise assault, Egyptian troops crossed the Suez Canal under an intense 2,000-gun artillery barrage. Combat engineers used high-pressure water monitors to wash away the sand ramparts of the Bar-Lev Line within hours. Simultaneously, hundreds of Syrian tanks broke through Israeli defences on the Golan Heights, creating an immediate existential crisis and threatening the Israeli heartland.

STAGE 4 The US Emergency Airlift & Israeli Counter-Attack 14–22 Oct 1973
“How did superpower airlifts and Sharon’s counter-attack bring nuclear alert?”

In the opening days of fighting, Israel suffered heavy losses of tanks and aircraft to Soviet-supplied anti-tank and anti-aircraft missiles. Facing acute ammunition shortages, US President Richard Nixon ordered Operation Nickel Grass on 14 October, an emergency military airlift flying 22,000 tons of American tanks, shells, and equipment into Israel to counter Soviet supply flights to Egypt and Syria. On 15–16 October, General Ariel Sharon led an audacious counter-attack across to the west bank of the Suez Canal, encircling Egypt’s Third Army and advancing toward Cairo. When Soviet leaders threatened direct military intervention and Washington briefly raised a global nuclear alert, alarmed superpowers worked urgently together to impose an immediate ceasefire.

STAGE 5 The Arab Oil Embargo & Ceasefire Oct 1973–1974
“How did the OPEC oil embargo and Resolution 338 push rivals toward talks?”

On 17 October 1973, Arab oil ministers belonging to OPEC unleashed the powerful "oil weapon," cutting crude oil production and placing a total export embargo on the United States and Western countries supporting Israel. Global oil prices quadrupled within months, causing severe fuel shortages, economic recession, and double-digit inflation across Western economies, reshaping global energy geopolitics permanently. On 22 October 1973, the UN Security Council passed UN Resolution 338, demanding an immediate ceasefire and the start of direct negotiations based on Resolution 242. Although Israel ultimately secured a military victory on the battlefield, the heavy casualties destroyed the myth of Israeli invulnerability and restored Arab self-respect, creating the indispensable psychological climate for future diplomatic peace talks.
