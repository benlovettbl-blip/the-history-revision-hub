KEY ENQUIRY “Water, Faith, and Imperial Rulers: How did physical geography and artificial borders make conflict in the Middle East inevitable?”
STAGE 1 The McMahon-Hussein Correspondence 1915–1916
“Why did Arabs believe Britain promised them independent sovereignty in 1915?”

For over four centuries, the Middle East was ruled by the Turkish Ottoman Empire. When the Ottomans allied with Imperial Germany in 1914, British strategists faced an acute crisis: Turkish forces threatened the Suez Canal, Britain’s vital maritime route to India. Desperate to protect this waterway and tie down Turkish troops, Sir Henry McMahon, British High Commissioner in Cairo, opened secret correspondence with Sharif Hussein of Mecca. McMahon promised British support for an independent Arab state across the region in return for an armed rebellion against Turkish rule. Believing Palestine was included in this promised territory, Arab forces launched the Arab Revolt in 1916. However, McMahon inserted vague geographic exemptions to protect British interests, creating a bitter dispute over whether Britain had pledged Palestine to the Arabs.

STAGE 2 The Secret Sykes-Picot Agreement May 1916
“Why was the secret Sykes-Picot Agreement viewed as an imperial betrayal?”

While McMahon was negotiating with Arab leaders, Britain and France secretly planned to divide the Middle East between themselves. In May 1916, British diplomat Sir Mark Sykes and French representative François Georges-Picot signed the secret Sykes-Picot Agreement. Using straight lines drawn across desert maps, they carved Ottoman lands into imperial spheres of influence to secure oil reserves and trade routes. France took control of Syria and Lebanon, while Britain claimed Mesopotamia (Iraq) and Transjordan. Due to its religious significance to Christians, Muslims, and Jews, Palestine was designated an international zone under joint Allied administration. When Russian revolutionaries exposed the secret deal in late 1917, Arab leaders were outraged by what they regarded as a direct British betrayal of wartime promises.

Broken Promises & Imperial Borders: Why Was Conflict in the Middle East Inevitable? — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 The Balfour Declaration November 1917
“How did the Balfour Declaration create contradictory British commitments?”

By late 1917, Britain faced severe military strain on the Western Front and sought international Jewish support in the United States and Russia. Prime Minister David Lloyd George and Foreign Secretary Arthur Balfour courted the Zionist movement, which campaigned for a Jewish homeland in Palestine. On 2 November 1917, Balfour issued an official public statement to Lord Rothschild pledging British government backing. The Balfour Declaration announced British support for "the establishment in Palestine of a national home for the Jewish people." To reassure allies, it added that non-Jewish civil and religious rights must not be harmed. However, Arab Muslims and Christians made up over 90% of Palestine’s population. By promising a Jewish homeland in an Arab-majority land, Britain made conflicting commitments that entrenched decades of communal conflict.

STAGE 4 The British Mandate & Rising Immigration 1920–1936
“Why did rising Jewish immigration spark communal violence under the Mandate?”

In December 1917, British General Edmund Allenby's army captured Jerusalem from the retreating Ottomans. In 1922, the newly created League of Nations formally granted Britain administrative control over Mandatory Palestine. Britain was tasked with preparing the country for self-government while incorporating the Balfour Declaration’s pledge to facilitate Jewish immigration and land purchase through the Jewish Agency. Palestinian Arabs viewed expanding Jewish immigration as a direct threat to their national survival. As Zionist organizations purchased agricultural land, Arab tenant farmers were evicted from ancestral holdings. Tensions erupted into deadly communal riots in Jaffa and Jerusalem. During the 1930s, Jewish refugees fled rising Nazi persecution in Europe, swelling Palestine’s Jewish population to nearly 30% by 1936.

STAGE 5 The Arab Revolt & The 1939 White Paper 1936–1939
“Why did the 1939 White Paper turn Zionists against British rule?”

In 1936, rising anger erupted into the Great Arab Revolt, a widespread rebellion against British rule and Jewish immigration. Directed by the Arab Higher Committee under the Grand Mufti of Jerusalem, Haj Amin al-Husseini, Arabs held general strikes, attacked British military patrols, and repeatedly sabotaged the strategic IPC oil pipeline running to Haifa port. Britain responded with martial law, deploying 20,000 soldiers and executing rebel leaders. Facing imminent war against Nazi Germany, Britain needed to secure Arab oil and maintain peace across the Middle East. In May 1939, the British government published the 1939 White Paper, which strictly limited Jewish immigration to 75,000 over five years and promised independent statehood within ten years. Zionists were deeply angered, viewing the restrictions as a fatal betrayal of European Jews facing Nazi persecution.

KEY ENQUIRY “Why did the British Mandate collapse and how was the State of Israel created amidst war?”
STAGE 1 Holocaust Survivors & The 1, 500 Monthly Limit 1945–1946
“Why could Britain satisfy neither Jewish nor Arab demands after 1945?”

In 1945, over 250,000 Jewish Holocaust survivors remained stranded in European Displaced Persons (DP) camps, refusing to return to ruined communities and demanding passage to Palestine. Backed by the Jewish Agency, US President Harry S. Truman urged Britain to grant 100,000 immediate immigration certificates and establish an independent Jewish state. Conversely, Palestinian Arabs led by the Arab Higher Committee demanded an immediate halt to Jewish immigration and land purchases alongside majority-rule Arab independence. Caught between these irreconcilable demands, British Foreign Secretary Ernest Bevin enforced the 1939 White Paper quota of 1,500 migrants per month, fearing an Arab uprising would threaten British Middle Eastern oil supplies and strategic military bases.

STAGE 2 The Zionist Paramilitary Insurgency Oct 1945–1946
“How did the Haganah, Irgun, and Lehi disrupt British rule across Palestine?”

Frustrated by British immigration restrictions, Jewish underground militias united in October 1945 to form the United Resistance Movement (known in Hebrew as Tenuat HaMeri). This coalition brought together the mainstream socialist Haganah with two radical armed factions: Menachem Begin’s [Key Organisation: Irgun] and the militant Lehi (Stern Gang). Their goal was to make Palestine ungovernable for British forces through systematic sabotage. The insurgency launched coordinated armed assaults against British infrastructure, blowing up coastal radar stations, bridges, and railway lines used to intercept migrant vessels. Britain retaliated by deploying 100,000 soldiers, imposing strict curfews, and arresting thousands of Zionist leaders in Operation Agatha. Rather than ending resistance, British military repression alienated the Jewish population and convinced Zionist leaders that armed defiance was necessary.

The End of the British Mandate and the Creation of Israel — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 The King David Hotel Bombing 22 July 1946
“Why was the Irgun bombing of the King David Hotel a turning point for Britain?”

On 22 July 1946, the Irgun executed a devastating bomb attack on the south wing of the King David Hotel in Jerusalem. The hotel served as the central headquarters for both the British military command and the civil administration in Palestine. Irgun operatives disguised as Arab milk deliverymen smuggled milk cans packed with 350 kilograms of explosives into the basement restaurant. The massive explosion demolished all six floors of the headquarters, killing 91 people, including British officers, Arab civil servants, and Jewish administrative staff. The attack shocked the British public and drew fierce international condemnation. Shocked by the civilian death toll, David Ben-Gurion’s Haganah dissolved the United Resistance coalition, though the Irgun and Lehi continued aggressive guerrilla attacks.

STAGE 4 The Sergeants Affair & Public Backlash July 1947
“How did post-war debt and the Sergeants Affair fuel British demands to leave?”

In July 1947, the British executed three captured Irgun militants for armed attacks. In retaliation, the Irgun abducted two British intelligence sergeants, Clifford Martin and Mervyn Paice, holding them hostage in an underground bunker in Netanya. When British authorities proceeded with the executions of the Irgun fighters, Begin ordered both captured British soldiers to be hanged and their bodies booby-trapped in an eucalyptus grove. The Sergeants Affair provoked profound outrage across Britain, sparking anti-Jewish riots in London, Liverpool, and Manchester. British newspapers and parliamentarians openly demanded an immediate military withdrawal, arguing British soldiers should not die policing an intractable foreign conflict. Financially exhausted after World War Two, the British government concluded that maintaining the Mandate was militarily and politically unsustainable.

STAGE 5 The SS Exodus Crisis & Handover to the UN Feb–Nov 1947
“How did SS Exodus damage Britain, and what did UN Resolution 181 propose?”

In July 1947, Royal Navy destroyers intercepted the SS Exodus 1947, a clandestine Aliyah Bet steamship carrying 4,500 Holocaust survivors attempting to reach Palestine without visas. Determined to deter illegal migration, Foreign Secretary Ernest Bevin ordered the passengers forcibly returned to camps in British-occupied Germany, causing a global public relations disaster that destroyed British moral authority. Financially exhausted and unable to reconcile Jewish and Arab demands, Britain handed the Palestine problem to the [Key Organisation: United Nations] in February 1947, where UNSCOP investigated and recommended partition. On 29 November 1947, the General Assembly passed UN Resolution 181, voting to partition Palestine into separate Jewish and Arab states with international Jerusalem; the Jewish Agency accepted, while Arab leaders rejected it, sparking immediate civil war.

KEY ENQUIRY “How was the State of Israel established and defended during the 1948–49 War, and what were its lasting consequences?”
STAGE 1 The Declaration of the State of Israel & The Pan-Arab Invasion 14–15 May 1948
“Why did Ben-Gurion declare Israel on 14 May, and why did Arab armies invade?”

On 14 May 1948, hours before the British Mandate officially expired at midnight, David Ben-Gurion stood beneath Theodor Herzl’s portrait in the Tel Aviv Museum to proclaim the independent State of Israel. Asserting the Jewish people’s right to self-determination, Ben-Gurion won immediate diplomatic recognition from both US President Harry S. Truman and the Soviet Union. Statehood ignited immediate regional war. At dawn on 15 May 1948, regular military forces from five Arab League nations—Egypt, Transjordan, Syria, Lebanon, and Iraq—crossed into Palestine without a unified military command. Facing acute shortages of heavy artillery, tanks, and combat aircraft, outnumbered Israeli defenders mounted a desperate multi-front defence while Jordan’s British-trained Arab Legion captured East Jerusalem’s Old City.

STAGE 2 The First UN Truce & The Czech Arms Supply June–July 1948
“How did the first UN truce, Czech arms, and a unified IDF turn the war?”

Hard-pressed Israeli forces suffered severe ammunition shortages during the opening month of combat. In June 1948, the United Nations brokered the First Truce, an official four-week ceasefire negotiated by UN mediator Count Folke Bernadotte to halt fighting. Both sides agreed to pause hostilities, but Israel utilized the four-week interlude far more effectively than the fractured Arab coalition to reorganize and rearm. During the truce, Israel broke the international arms embargo by securing a massive clandestine Czech arms deal with Soviet approval, airlifting thousands of rifles, machine guns, and Avia S-199 fighter aircraft. Prime Minister Ben-Gurion simultaneously consolidated disparate militias into the unified IDF (Israel Defense Forces). When combat resumed in July, heavily reinforced Israeli divisions broke Egyptian lines and seized the military initiative across all operational fronts.

The Creation of Israel, the 1948–49 War & The Palestinian Refugee Crisis — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 The Palestinian Refugee Crisis (The Nakba) 1948–1949
“How did the 1948–49 war create the Palestinian refugee crisis and UNRWA?”

The 1948 war caused the catastrophic displacement of Palestinian Arab society, an event Palestinians commemorate as the Nakba ("The Catastrophe"). In April 1948, Zionist forces implemented Plan Dalet, a military operational strategy aimed at securing roads and Jewish enclaves by capturing hostile villages along vital transport corridors. Violent attacks—above all the 9 April 1948 Deir Yassin massacre, in which Irgun and Lehi fighters killed over 100 Palestinian villagers west of Jerusalem—spread intense civilian panic, causing whole communities to flee. By the war's conclusion, over 700,000 Palestinian Arabs had fled or been expelled from territories that fell under Israeli sovereignty, leaving over 400 villages depopulated. Most refugees were forced into squalid camps across Gaza, the West Bank, Lebanon, Syria, and Jordan. While the UN established UNRWA for humanitarian relief and passed Resolution 194 urging refugee return, Israel permanently barred repatriation to preserve a Jewish demographic majority.

STAGE 4 The 1949 Armistice Agreements (The Green Line) Feb–July 1949
“How did the Rhodes Armistices redraw borders while leaving tension unsolved?”

Between February and July 1949, acting UN mediator Dr Ralph Bunche convened separate bilateral negotiations between Israel and Egypt, Lebanon, Jordan, and Syria on the island of Rhodes. Bunche brokered four armistice agreements ending open military combat, though Arab states refused formal peace treaties, Egypt barred Israeli shipping from the Suez Canal, and cross-border fedayeen raids triggered fierce Israeli reprisals. Drawn with green grease-pencils on military maps, these ceasefire borders became known as the Green Line, leaving Israel holding 78% of Mandatory Palestine—a major expansion beyond the 55% allocated by UN Resolution 181. Meanwhile, Jordan annexed the West Bank and East Jerusalem and Egypt administered the Gaza Strip, leaving Palestinian Arabs stateless and regional hostility unresolved.

STAGE 5 The Israeli Law of Return & Demographic Surge 1950–1954
“How did the Law of Return (1950), conscription, and US aid consolidate Israel?”

On 5 July 1950, the Israeli Knesset passed the landmark Law of Return, declaring that every Jewish person worldwide held the legal right to settle in Israel and receive immediate citizenship. Prime Minister David Ben-Gurion paired mass immigration with compulsory IDF national service and trained reserve duty so the young state could populate vulnerable frontier borders and mobilise rapidly. State-run airlifts including Operation Magic Carpet from Yemen and Iraq helped double Israel’s Jewish population from 650,000 to over 1.4 million citizens by 1952, forcing strict food rationing and crowded canvas transit camps (ma’abarot). Crucial American financial backing—including a $100 million US Export-Import Bank loan and private US Jewish fundraising—funded housing and kept Israel’s economy solvent.

KEY ENQUIRY “Why did the nationalisation of the Suez Canal trigger a global crisis in 1956?”
STAGE 1 The Gaza Raid 28 Feb 1955
“How did Israel’s Gaza raid humiliate Nasser and escalate border hostility?”

In 1954, Colonel Gamal Abdel Nasser became leader of Egypt, emerging as the bold champion of Pan-Arab nationalism while allowing Palestinian fedayeen guerrillas to launch armed sabotage raids into southern Israel from Egyptian-held Gaza. On 28 February 1955, Israeli Prime Minister David Ben-Gurion and Defence Minister Moshe Dayan authorised a major cross-border reprisal raid into Gaza. Codenamed Operation Black Arrow, Israeli paratroopers stormed an Egyptian military garrison in Gaza, killing 38 Egyptian soldiers and policemen. The devastating strike exposed the military weakness of Egypt’s armed forces and deeply humiliated Nasser before his army officers, convincing him that Egypt could not deter Israel without acquiring modern jet fighters, tanks, and heavy artillery.

STAGE 2 The Czech Arms Deal Sept 1955
“Why did Nasser sign the Czech arms deal and blockade the Straits of Tiran?”

Desperate to rearm after the Gaza raid, Nasser sought weapons from Washington and London, but Western governments tied arms sales to joining anti-Soviet Cold War pacts. Determined to safeguard national independence, Nasser championed the Non-Aligned Movement at Bandung while tightening Egypt’s naval blockade of the Straits of Tiran against Israeli shipping bound for Eilat. In September 1955, Nasser stunned Western capitals by announcing the Czech arms deal, trading Egyptian cotton for advanced Soviet weaponry supplied via Czechoslovakia, including 200 MiG-15 jet fighters and 300 modern tanks. Breaking the Western arms monopoly gave the Soviet Union decisive influence in Cairo and alarmed Israeli commanders over Egypt’s growing offensive strength.

Increased Tension, Nasser, and the Suez Crisis — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 Dam Loans Cancelled & Suez Canal Nationalised July 1956
“Why did losing Aswan Dam loans lead Nasser to nationalise the Suez Canal?”

Nasser's central economic ambition was building the Aswan High Dam, a massive hydroelectric project designed to control Nile flooding and industrialize Egypt. However, when Nasser officially recognized Communist China in May 1956, US Secretary of State John Foster Dulles angrily cancelled a promised $56 million World Bank loan package to fund the dam, aiming to punish Nasser’s neutralist foreign policy. On 26 July 1956, Nasser responded with a stunning public act of anti-colonial defiance: addressing a massive crowd in Alexandria, he nationalised the British- and French-owned Universal Suez Canal Company. Nasser announced canal transit tolls would fund the dam himself. The nationalisation ignited fury in London and Paris, where leaders viewed Nasser as a dangerous dictator who had seized their strategic oil waterway.

STAGE 4 The Secret Sèvres Plot & Invasion Oct–Nov 1956
“How did Israel, Britain, and France collude in the secret Sèvres invasion?”

British Prime Minister Sir Anthony Eden and French leaders secretly colluded with Israeli Prime Minister David Ben-Gurion outside Paris. In late October 1956, they signed the covert Sèvres Agreement, engineering a pretext for military intervention: Israel would invade Egypt across Sinai, allowing Britain and France to issue an ultimatum to "separate the combatants" and reoccupy the Suez Canal. On 29 October 1956, Israeli paratroopers dropped near the Mitla Pass, sweeping rapidly across the Sinai Peninsula toward the canal. As secretly planned, Britain and France issued their joint ultimatum demanding both armies withdraw from canal banks. When Nasser predictably refused, Anglo-French forces bombed Egyptian airfields on 31 October and landed elite paratroopers at Port Said, seizing the northern canal zone.

STAGE 5 US Ultimatum & British Retreat Nov 1956–1958
“How did US pressure force retreat, deploy UNEF, and lead to the UAR (1958)?”

The Anglo-French invasion provoked global outrage and swift superpower condemnation. US President Dwight D. Eisenhower, furious that his allies had acted behind Washington’s back during the Soviet invasion of Hungary, threatened to block vital IMF loans and collapse the British pound while Moscow issued stern warnings, forcing a humiliating ceasefire on 6 November 1956 that shattered British imperial prestige. Under sustained American pressure, Israel withdrew from Sinai in early 1957 as the United Nations Emergency Force (UNEF) deployed along the border and reopened the Straits of Tiran to Israeli ships. Keeping the canal made Nasser the undisputed hero of Pan-Arabism, enabling him to unite Egypt and Syria into the United Arab Republic (UAR) in February 1958.
