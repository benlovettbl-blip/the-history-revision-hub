Enquiry 1 Eyewitness Voice Roman Britain Eyewitness

Stolen at the Baths! Docimedis's Lead Curse to Sulis Minerva

Docimedis of Aquae Sulis (Roman Baths Curse Tablet, Tabellae Sulis 10, c. AD 150–300)
Historical Context: Without a modern police force in Roman Britain, what could an ordinary bather do when a sneak thief snatched their warm woollen cloak or gloves from the changing rooms at Bath? Read how angry citizens scratched secret messages onto lead for the goddess Sulis Minerva!
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Aquae Sulis[AK-way SOO-liss]
The Roman spa town of Bath in Somerset, built around steaming hot mineral springs sacred to the goddess Sulis Minerva.
Curse Tablet[KERS TAB-lit]
A thin sheet of Roman lead or pewter scratched with a plea asking a god or goddess to track down and punish a thief.
Stylus[STY-lus]
A pointed iron or bronze Roman writing pen used to scratch sharp Latin letters onto wax boards or soft lead sheets.
Magistrate[MAJ-ih-strayt]
A senior Roman town judge who heard serious court cases inside the stone basilica hall when a suspect was caught.

Steam curled off the green pools at Aquae Sulis as I stepped from the warm bath. I shivered in shock! My thick wool cloak and two warm gloves had vanished from the stone bench. No police officer walked Roman streets to hunt an unseen thief. A town magistrate in the court hall could only judge a suspect if I caught them myself.

So I bought a thin grey sheet of soft lead and gripped a sharp iron stylus. Pressing hard, I scratched my plea to the goddess of the spring:

"Goddess Sulis Minerva! Docimedis has lost two gloves and a warm cloak. Is the thief man or woman, free citizen or slave? Let them lose sleep and health until they bring my goods back to your temple!"

I folded the lead sheet tight so human eyes could not read a word. Walking to the hot sacred pool, I dropped my curse into the dark water.

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 Why couldn't Docimedis simply ask a Roman magistrate to investigate the missing cloak and gloves right away?
2 Why did Roman bathers fold their lead curse tablets tightly before dropping them into the steaming spring at Bath?
3 What does listing 'man or woman, free citizen or slave' tell us about who visited the Roman baths and who could be suspected?

Ten Neighbours and One Cow-Horn: Raising the Anglo-Saxon Hue and Cry

The History Revision Hub Narrative Archive
Historical Context: Imagine living in a thatched Anglo-Saxon village in AD 895 with zero police cars, zero radios, and thick oak woods all around! Discover how every neighbour over twelve had to drop their plough and sprint whenever the cow-horn sounded!
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Tithing[TY-thing]
A group of ten Anglo-Saxon neighbours aged twelve and over who promised to keep one another honest and bring lawbreakers to court.
Hue and Cry[HYOO und KRY]
A loud alarm shouted or blown on a horn forcing every villager in earshot to stop work and chase a runaway thief immediately.
Wergild[WER-gild]
Literally 'person-price' in Old English—silver coins paid by a wrongdoer's family to an injured victim's kin to stop blood feuds.
Hundred Court[HUN-dred KORT]
An open-air monthly Anglo-Saxon court where local elders and witnesses settled village disputes and set silver fines.

Frost crunched under Oslac's boots in Wiltshire around AD 895. Checking his thatched sheep pen at dawn, he gasped aloud! The wood gate hung wide open, and two plump ewes had gone. Fresh prints in the mud led straight to the oak woods.

Oslac could not call a police station, for none existed in England. Instead, he raised his curved cow-horn and blew three loud blasts. Then he yelled across the fields: "Out! Out! Thief in the woods!"

That cry raised the Hue and Cry. By Saxon law, all ten men in Oslac's tithing had to drop their spades at once and run! Any neighbour who hid lazily indoors faced a steep fine.

Soon twelve fast neighbours trapped the shaking thief by the brook. Rather than start a blood feud with swords, they led him to the open-air Hundred Court. There village elders made his kin give back both sheep and pay silver wergild coins!

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 What happened to an Anglo-Saxon villager who heard the 'Hue and Cry' horn but stayed indoors instead of helping chase the thief?
2 How did grouping everyone aged twelve and over into a ten-person 'tithing' stop lots of crimes before they even started?
3 Why was paying silver 'wergild' compensation wiser for a small village than letting angry families take revenge with swords?

Enquiry 3 Classic Literature & Verse Medieval English Ballad

A Gest of Robyn Hode (Outlaws in the King's Royal Forest)

Traditional Medieval English Ballad (First Printed c. 1495, Public Domain)
Historical Context: After 1066, Norman kings fenced off huge woodlands under harsh Forest Laws—making it a terrible crime for hungry villagers to hunt wild deer! Read aloud from England's oldest Robin Hood ballad to hear why ordinary folk cheered a greenwood outlaw!
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Forest Laws[FOR-ist LAWZ]
Harsh Norman laws brought in by William the Conqueror forbidding villagers from hunting royal deer or chopping wood without pay.
Outlaw[OWT-law]
A fugitive placed completely 'outside the law' after fleeing court—anyone could capture them, so many hid in deep forests.
Sheriff[SHER-if]
From 'shire-reeve'—the king's chief county officer who collected taxes, hunted outlaws, and guarded royal castles.
Poaching[POH-ching]
Secretly hunting deer, rabbits, or fish on land claimed by the king or wealthy lords—seen by many villagers as a 'social crime'.

After 1066, Norman kings claimed wide English woods for royal sport. Taking one wild deer became poaching, punished by grim fines! Men who fled court were named outlaws and hid among tall green oaks. By winter fires, folk sang of a bold archer who tricked the greedy Sheriff:

"Listen close, good neighbours all, of freeborn country blood! I shall tell you of a brave archer, his name was Robin Hood. Robin stood in green Barnsdale, and leaned against a tree. Beside him stood tall Little John, a loyal friend was he."

Robin gave Little John one firm rule before they walked the woods:

"Never harm an honest farmer, who ploughs the muddy ground! Never rob a kindly yeoman, walking where green trees grow round. Greedy lords and unfair judges, bind their purses tight! And watch the Sheriff of Nottingham, by morning and by night!"

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 In the medieval ballad, which hardworking country people did Robin Hood strictly forbid Little John from harming?
2 Why did ordinary medieval villagers resent William the Conqueror's 'Forest Laws' so deeply?
3 Even if Robin Hood himself was a legend, what real historical feelings do medieval Robin Hood ballads reveal to historians?

Enquiry 4 True Historical Story True Historical Micro-Story

Thirty-Six Barrels in the Dark: Catching Guy Fawkes on 5 November 1605

The History Revision Hub Narrative Archive
Historical Context: High treason—plotting to kill the monarch or blow up Parliament—was the most terrifying crime in Tudor and Stuart Britain! Creep down into the pitch-black cellar beneath the House of Lords just before midnight on 4–5 November 1605!
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
High Treason[HY TREE-zun]
The most serious crime in the kingdom: plotting to overthrow or kill the king or queen or destroy Parliament.
Dark Lantern[DARK LAN-tern]
An iron candle lantern with a sliding metal shutter that hid its flame instantly in dark cellars—like Guy Fawkes's 1605 lantern.
Monteagle Letter[mont-EE-gul LET-er]
A mysterious unsigned note delivered in October 1605 warning Lord Monteagle to stay away from Parliament before a 'terrible blow'.
Deterrence[dih-TER-uns]
Making punishments deliberately frightening in public so other people are too scared to copy the crime.

Ten days before King James I opened Parliament, a stranger handed a secret note to Lord Monteagle. "Stay away!" the unsigned Monteagle Letter warned. "Parliament shall receive a terrible blow, yet they shall not see who hurts them!"

Clever King James guessed at once what "a terrible blow" meant. He ordered guards to check for gunpowder! Near midnight on 4 November 1605, Sir Thomas Knyvet led armed men into a dark cellar right below the House of Lords.

By a pile of wood stood a tall man in riding boots calling himself "John Johnson". His real name was Guy Fawkes! In his hand shone an iron dark lantern with a sliding cover. In his coat lay a watch and slow fuses.

Guards pulled the firewood aside and gasped! Thirty-six barrels of black powder sat waiting to blow the hall sky-high. Caught in high treason, the plotters faced harsh public deterrence.

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 Which clue inside the anonymous Monteagle Letter helped King James I guess that plotters planned an explosion?
2 Why did Guy Fawkes carry a special 'dark lantern' with a sliding iron cover instead of an open flaming torch?
3 Why did Tudor and Stuart rulers punish 'high treason' more fiercely and publicly than any other crime?

Enquiry 5 Eyewitness Voice Prison Reformer Eyewitness

Straw, Cold Stone and Hope: Elizabeth Fry Enters Newgate Prison (1813)

Elizabeth Fry & Anna Buxton (Adapted from Newgate Prison Journals, 1813–1817, Public Domain)
Historical Context: Under the harsh 18th-century 'Bloody Code', damp prisons like London's Newgate crammed hundreds of awaiting-trial suspects, debtors, and children together onto filthy straw! Step inside Newgate with Quaker reformer Elizabeth Fry in 1813!
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Newgate Prison[NYOO-gayt PRIZ-un]
London's grim, overcrowded stone prison where men, women, and children awaited trial, hanging, or convict ships to Australia.
Rehabilitation[ree-huh-bil-ih-TAY-shun]
Helping prisoners reform through clean rooms, reading lessons, and paid work skills so they can live honestly after release.
Quakers[KWAY-kerz]
Members of the Christian Religious Society of Friends—like Elizabeth Fry—who believed every human being deserved kindness and dignity.
Transportation[trans-por-TAY-shun]
Shipping over 160,000 British convicts in chains across the ocean to work in Australian penal colonies between 1787 and 1868.

Guards told me to leave my watch outside before opening the heavy gate at Newgate Prison in winter 1813. Nearly three hundred women and small children crowded into four damp stone rooms!

Women facing transportation ships to Australia slept next to mothers who had not yet seen a judge. Most had no beds and no warm blankets against the icy stone floor. Little babies shivered in thin rags.

My fellow Quakers and I did not shout at them. We stitched warm wool clothes for the children and spoke to each woman with kind respect. Back in 1817, I asked the mothers a gentle question. "Even behind these dark bars, will you help us start a school for your children?"

Tears of hope shone on tired faces as every mother agreed! Soon women learned to read and sew neat quilts to sell. Kindness and real skills proved rehabilitation worked.

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 What shocked Elizabeth Fry most about how prisoners were mixed together inside Newgate's women's ward in 1813?
2 Why did Elizabeth Fry ask the women's permission to start a prison school and sewing work instead of simply barking orders?
3 What is the big difference between 'deterrence' under the Bloody Code and 'rehabilitation' taught by Elizabeth Fry?

Enquiry 6 Classic Literature & Verse Victorian Detective Classic

A Study in Scarlet (Sherlock Holmes Inspects Tiny Clues, 1887)

Sir Arthur Conan Doyle (Published 1887, Public Domain)
Historical Context: Before 1900, real police detectives were only just learning to guard crime scenes and study footprints, dust, and fingerprints! Read Sir Arthur Conan Doyle's famous 1887 story to watch Sherlock Holmes amaze Dr Watson with a magnifying glass and tape measure!
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Forensics[fuh-REN-ziks]
Using careful scientific tests—such as fingerprints, microscopes, and DNA—to solve crimes fairly with physical proof.
Deduction[dih-DUK-shun]
Working out what happened step by step from tiny physical clues rather than guessing or relying on rumours.
Fingerprints[FING-ger-prints]
The unique loops, arches, and whorls on each person's fingertips—first used by Scotland Yard's Fingerprint Bureau in 1901.
DNA Profiling[dee-en-AY PROH-fy-ling]
The 1984 Leicester breakthrough by Sir Alec Jeffreys reading unique genetic code from tiny drops of blood, hair, or saliva.

In 1887, doctor Arthur Conan Doyle thrilled readers with a sharp new detective named Sherlock Holmes. Holmes trusted real clues and forensics far above guesswork! Watch Dr Watson describe Holmes checking a dusty London room.

"As he spoke, he whipped a tape measure and a round glass lens from his coat pocket. With those two tools he stepped softly round the room. Sometimes he stopped still or knelt low. Once he even lay flat upon the dusty boards!

He grew so busy with his search that he forgot we stood there. Under his breath he gave little cries and whistles of joy.

For twenty minutes he worked on with the utmost care. He measured faint boot marks unseen to my eyes. Then he checked every tiny scratch along the walls!"

Fourteen years later, Scotland Yard opened its real Fingerprint Bureau!

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 Which two simple scientific tools did Sherlock Holmes pull from his pocket to examine the dusty room?
2 How is Sherlock Holmes's careful search for unseen boot marks the exact opposite of Anglo-Saxon 'Trial by Ordeal' or 1640s witch trials?
3 Which two real British scientific breakthroughs in 1901 and 1984 turned Sherlock Holmes's dream of scientific proof into everyday policing?

GUIDE KEY
Crime and Punishment Through Time · Support for Families & Tutors

Parent, Carer & Tutor Conversation Guide

Model Talking Points
No Specialist History Knowledge Needed: Children never need word-for-word answers—praise curiosity and listen for these core ideas during your 3-minute chat!
Enquiry 1: Bath Curse Tablet p. 2
Q1: Roman Britain had no detective police force—victims had to catch a suspect themselves before bringing them to a magistrate's court!
Q2: They folded the thin lead sheets so mortal eyes could not read the secret message meant only for the goddess Sulis Minerva!
Q3: It shows the busy baths held men, women, free citizens, and enslaved workers side by side—and Docimedis had no clue who took his things!
Enquiry 2: Sound the Hue and Cry! p. 3
Q1: Their whole tithing or household faced a strict silver fine because chasing thieves was every neighbour's legal duty!
Q2: If one member broke the law and ran away, the other nine neighbours had to bring them to court or pay the fine themselves!
Q3: Revenge killings started endless blood feuds between neighbours, whereas agreed silver compensation restored peace safely.
Enquiry 3: Robin Hood & Greenwood Law p. 4
Q1: Hardworking farmers ploughing the ground and ordinary country yeomen walking through the greenwood!
Q2: Woods where families had gathered firewood and hunted food for generations were suddenly locked up for royal deer hunting under cruel punishments.
Q3: They prove how strongly ordinary medieval people hated unfair taxes, corrupt sheriffs, and harsh Forest Laws!
Enquiry 4: The Midnight Lantern p. 5
Q1: The warning that Parliament would 'receive a terrible blow... yet they shall not see who hurts them' pointed straight to hidden gunpowder!
Q2: A sliding shutter hid the light from guards AND kept naked sparks safely away from thirty-six barrels of explosive gunpowder!
Q3: With no national police or standing army, monarchs used terrifying public deterrence to scare anyone else away from rebellion.
Enquiry 5: Inside Newgate Prison p. 6
Q1: Convicted prisoners awaiting transportation slept crammed into four freezing rooms alongside unconvicted women and little children!
Q2: Treating prisoners with dignity and letting them agree to their own fair rules gave them pride, hope, and real self-respect.
Q3: Deterrence tries to terrify people with harsh punishments, whereas rehabilitation teaches reading and job skills so prisoners can rebuild honest lives.
Enquiry 6: Sherlock's Magnifying Lens p. 7
Q1: A tape measure and a round magnifying lens!
Q2: Ordeals and witch trials relied on superstition and painful tests, whereas forensic detectives measure real physical clues left at the scene!
Q3: Scotland Yard's Fingerprint Bureau in 1901 and Sir Alec Jeffreys's discovery of DNA profiling at Leicester in 1984!
Bonus 6-Story Quick Recall Check (Ask Aloud After Reading!) 18 Interactive Quiz Qs via Page QR Codes
E1: Why did Roman bathers at Aquae Sulis scratch messages onto lead sheets for Sulis Minerva instead of calling the police? → Roman Britain had no police detectives to hunt unknown thieves, so victims asked the goddess Sulis Minerva to punish the thief until their goods were returned.
E2: How did Anglo-Saxon 'tithings', the 'Hue and Cry', and 'wergild' keep villages peaceful without a police force? → Ten-person tithings watched neighbours' behaviour, everyone had to chase thieves during the Hue and Cry, and silver wergild fines stopped revenge feuds.
E3: Why did Norman Forest Laws make legends like 'Robin Hood' so popular among medieval English villagers? → Norman Forest Laws banned villagers from hunting deer or gathering wood under harsh penalties, so songs about an outlaw outwitting greedy sheriffs thrilled ordinary folk.
E4: How was the 1605 Gunpowder Plot uncovered, and what physical items were found on Guy Fawkes beneath Westminster? → The anonymous Monteagle Letter alerted King James I; guards searching the cellar found 36 gunpowder barrels plus Fawkes's dark lantern, watch, and fuses.
E5: What terrible conditions did Elizabeth Fry find inside Newgate Prison in 1813, and how did she prove 'rehabilitation' worked? → Nearly 300 women and children slept on freezing stone without beds; Fry opened a prison school, provided clean clothes, and taught paid sewing.
E6: How did Sherlock Holmes's magnifying-glass clues point forward to real British forensic breakthroughs in 1901 and 1984? → Holmes championed measuring tiny physical clues; soon Scotland Yard opened its Fingerprint Bureau (1901) and Sir Alec Jeffreys discovered DNA profiling (1984).
