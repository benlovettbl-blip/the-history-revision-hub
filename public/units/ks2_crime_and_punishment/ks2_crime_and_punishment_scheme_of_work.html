WALK-IN LESSON PLANS • ENQUIRIES 1 & 2 NON-SPECIALIST PRIMARY TEACHER GUIDE
Enquiry 1: Roman Law, Twelve Tables and Bath Curse Tablets (AD 43–410) TB p.4 • WB pp.3–4
Key Enquiry: How did the Romans catch thieves and judge crimes without police?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This opening Key Stage 2 enquiry introduces Britain’s earliest written legal system under Roman rule (AD 43–410). Pupils investigate how written laws rooted in Rome’s Twelve Tables were judged by town magistrates inside stone basilicas, why the absence of a detective police force meant ordinary victims had to catch suspects themselves, how punishments differed sharply between wealthy Roman citizens and enslaved people, and why bathers at Aquae Sulis (Bath) threw over 130 scratched lead curse tablets to goddess Sulis Minerva.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain how Rome introduced Britain’s first written laws and stone basilica courts after AD 43.
  • Contrast how Roman courts punished wealthy citizens compared with poor or enslaved people.
  • Use Bath’s scratched lead curse tablets to explain what happened when thieves escaped unseen.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Between AD 43 and AD 410, Roman towns judged crimes using written rules, yet catching thieves relied on ordinary people because legionaries guarded forts and night-watch vigiles mainly watched for fires. Victims had to catch suspects themselves and bring them to a magistrate in the stone basilica, where Roman citizens paid fines while enslaved people faced harsh penalties—and whenever thieves escaped unseen at Bath, victims dropped lead curses to Sulis Minerva!”
Enquiry 2: Anglo-Saxon Tithings, Wergild and Trial by Ordeal (AD 410–1066) TB p.5 • WB pp.5–6
Key Enquiry: Were Anglo-Saxon laws fair community rules or painful superstitions?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This second enquiry explores how Anglo-Saxon kingdoms (AD 410–1066) maintained peace across small rural villages without police stations or prisons. Pupils investigate collective responsibility through ten-person tithings and compulsory Hue and Cry pursuits, examine how royal law codes from King Aethelberht to King Alfred the Great used silver wergild compensation to prevent deadly family blood feuds, and evaluate why deadlocked Hundred Courts turned to priests for Trials by Hot Iron or Cold Water.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain how ten-person tithings and the Hue and Cry policed Anglo-Saxon villages without paid officers.
  • Describe how King Alfred’s written law code and silver wergild payments stopped violent blood feuds.
  • Evaluate why Hundred Courts used Trial by Ordeal when human witnesses could not agree.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Historians can see both clever community peacekeeping and painful superstition in Anglo-Saxon justice because everyday disputes were settled sensibly, while deadlocked cases relied on luck. Ten-person tithings, the Hue and Cry, and silver wergild payments cleverly stopped violent blood feuds without prisons. However, wergild valued rich thanes above ceorl farmers, and when witnesses disagreed, Trial by Ordeal such as Trial by Hot Iron judged infection rather than real clues!”

Enquiry 3: Norman Forest Laws, Sanctuary and Royal Courts (1066–1485) TB p.6 • WB pp.7–8
Key Enquiry: How did Norman kings and medieval royal judges change English justice after 1066?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This third enquiry traces continuity and change across the Norman Conquest and High Middle Ages (1066–1485). Pupils examine why William the Conqueror kept Anglo-Saxon tithings and the Hue and Cry while introducing deeply resented Forest Laws and Trial by Combat, how King Henry II’s travelling assize judges established one shared English Common Law with twelve-person juries, and why 1215 (Magna Carta plus Pope Innocent III banning Trial by Ordeal) alongside Church sanctuary and village stocks/pillories reshaped English justice.
Learning Objectives:
  • Identify what Normans kept after 1066 and explain why villagers hated William I’s new Forest Laws.
  • Explain how King Henry II, 1215 Magna Carta, and the Pope’s 1215 ban made twelve-person juries central.
  • Compare medieval Church sanctuary with public shaming in village stocks and town pillories.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Between 1066 and 1485, medieval England laid the foundations of modern courtroom trials because royal courts replaced superstitious ordeals with evidence and juries. Although William I angered peasants with harsh Forest Laws, King Henry II sent travelling judges across England to build one shared Common Law heard by twelve-person juries. Then in 1215, King John sealed Magna Carta promising lawful trials and Pope Innocent III banned priests from Trial by Ordeal—making trial by jury England’s lasting standard!”
Enquiry 4: Tudor Vagrants, High Treason and Witchcraft Trials (1485–1700) TB p.7 • WB pp.9–10
Key Enquiry: Why did Tudor and Stuart rulers fear vagrants, plotters, and accused witches so fiercely?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This fourth enquiry investigates why early modern laws (1485–1700) punished wandering vagrants, religious plotters, and accused witches so severely—and why witchcraft trials later vanished. Pupils explore economic causes of Tudor poverty alongside the 1601 Elizabethan Poor Law ("deserving poor" vs whipped "sturdy beggars"), examine High Treason deterrence through Guy Fawkes’s 1605 Gunpowder Plot, and trace why Matthew Hopkins’s 1645–1647 East Anglian witch-hunt thrived during Civil War chaos before peace and 1660s scientific thinking ended witchcraft executions.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain why Tudor towns whipped "sturdy beggars" while helping local elderly people under the 1601 Poor Law.
  • Use the 1605 Gunpowder Plot to explain why monarchs punished High Treason with harsh public deterrence.
  • Explain why witchcraft accusations peaked in 1645–1647 and why scientific thinking ended witch trials.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Between 1485 and 1700, fear shaped harsh Tudor and Stuart laws across England because rulers had only unpaid parish constables to keep control during poverty, religious division, and Civil War. Towns whipped wandering sturdy beggars to deter unrest, King James I used fierce deterrence after Guy Fawkes’s 1605 High Treason gunpowder plot, and wartime panic fuelled Matthew Hopkins’s witch trials until scientific proof triumphed!”

Enquiry 5: Smugglers, Highwaymen and The Bloody Code (1700–1820) TB p.8 • WB pp.11–12
Key Enquiry: Why did Britain pass over 200 death-penalty laws—and why did juries stop using them?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This fifth enquiry explores Georgian crime and punishment (1700–1820), asking why Parliament expanded capital offences from roughly 50 to over 220 ("the Bloody Code")—and why harsh deterrence backfired. Pupils investigate economic and technological causes of coastal smuggling (high customs duties on tea, brandy, and silk) and highway robbery (unlit turnpikes, flintlock pistols, and cash-carrying travellers), see why sympathetic juries refused to hang petty thieves, and trace the shift toward Thames prison hulks, Australian transportation (1787–1868), and Elizabeth Fry’s humane rehabilitation inside Newgate Prison.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain why coastal smuggling and stagecoach highway robbery grew rapidly in the 1700s—and why highwaymen later disappeared.
  • Explain why Georgian MPs passed over 220 death-penalty laws ("the Bloody Code") and why juries stopped enforcing them.
  • Describe convict transportation to Australia (1787–1868) and Elizabeth Fry’s reforms inside Newgate Prison.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Georgian MPs created the "Bloody Code" hoping terror would stop crime cheaply, yet the plan backfired because ordinary twelve-person juries refused to hang people for tiny offences like stealing five shillings! Jurors deliberately undervalued stolen goods or acquitted thieves, pushing Britain toward Australian transportation and inspiring Quaker reformer Elizabeth Fry to prove humane rehabilitation worked inside Newgate Prison.”
Enquiry 6: Peel’s 1829 Bobbies, Victorian Prisons and Forensics (1829–Present) TB p.9 • WB pp.13–14
Key Enquiry: How have uniformed police, prison reform, and forensic science transformed justice since 1829?
Subject Knowledge Primer: This synoptic final enquiry connects 1829 to the present day while reviewing 2,000 years of change and continuity. Pupils investigate how Home Secretary Sir Robert Peel slashed over 180 Bloody Code hangings and founded London’s unarmed 1829 Metropolitan Police ("Bobbies" / "Peelers") to prevent crime by public consent, debate Victorian prisons like Pentonville (1842 Separate System and hard-labour treadwheels) versus modern rehabilitation and ending capital punishment (1965), and trace how 1901 fingerprints, 999 calls, CCTV, cyber policing, and Professor Sir Alec Jeffreys’s 1984 Leicester DNA profiling revolutionised evidence.
Learning Objectives:
  • Explain why Sir Robert Peel dressed his 1829 Metropolitan Police in blue coats without guns to police by consent.
  • Compare harsh Victorian Pentonville cells and treadwheels with 20th-century abolition of hanging and rehabilitation.
  • Trace how 1901 fingerprints and Sir Alec Jeffreys’s 1984 DNA profiling transformed crime detection across 2,000 years.
Model Big Question Answer (WAGOLL): “Across 2,000 years from Roman Britain to today, the biggest transformation in Crime and Punishment has been shifting from unpaid neighbour chases, superstitious ordeals, and harsh public executions to professional prevention, rehabilitation, and forensic science! While medieval twelve-person juries and Magna Carta still protect fair trials, Peel’s 1829 Bobbies, Scotland Yard’s 1901 Fingerprint Bureau, and Sir Alec Jeffreys’s 1984 Leicester DNA profiling now solve crimes and clear innocent people using real scientific proof.”
