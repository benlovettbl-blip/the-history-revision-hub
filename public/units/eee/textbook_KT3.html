KEY ENQUIRY “How did educational expansion and popular theatrical culture reflect both social change and strict Tudor hierarchy?”
STAGE 1 Attitudes to Education & Literacy 1558–1580s
“How did Tudor hierarchy and gender shape basic literacy and schooling?”

Tudor education reflected humanist learning and Protestant belief in reading the English Bible, yet widespread illiteracy persisted and schooling stayed divided by class and gender. Overall literacy rose to roughly 30% of men and 10% of women by 1603. Most readers belonged to gentry, merchant, and yeoman families rather than farm labourers, whose children worked from age six. Middling-sort boys learned basic reading from a wooden hornbook alongside writing and sums at elementary petty schools in a teacher’s home. Some girls attended dame schools for reading and sewing. Above all, Tudor schooling upheld the Great Chain of Being, teaching obedience to God, monarch, and social superiors.

STAGE 2 Grammar Schools 1558–1580s
“How did endowed grammar schools educate gentry and merchant sons?”

Education expanded fastest in fee-paying town grammar schools: forty-two were founded in the 1560s and thirty more in the 1570s by wealthy Protestant benefactors and merchant guilds. Bright boys aged ten to fourteen from gentry, professional, merchant, and yeoman homes (plus scholarship boys) studied long days from 6 a.m. to 5 p.m. Grammar schools taught a strict classical curriculum centred on Latin and Greek authors like Cicero, Virgil, and Seneca alongside rhetoric, scripture, archery, and corporal punishment. Meanwhile, merchants’ and craftsmen’s sons often moved from petty school into an apprenticeship to learn bookkeeping, arithmetic, and skilled trades.

Education and Leisure in Elizabethan England — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 Universities & Noble Education 1558–1580s
“How did noble home tutors differ from Oxford, Cambridge, and Law Inns?”

Children of the nobility studied at home under private tutors, just as humanist scholar Roger Ascham had tutored young Princess Elizabeth. Noble boys learned Latin, Greek, French, history, fencing, and riding. Noble girls studied languages, music, dancing, needlework, and running great households, though women remained barred from universities. At fourteen or fifteen, gentlemen’s sons went up to Oxford and Cambridge. There, Protestant colleges like Emmanuel (1584) and Jesus (1571) expanded to train loyal, educated Anglican clergy and royal officials. Many gentry sons also studied common law in London at the Inns of Court.

STAGE 4 Sports and Pastimes by Social Class 1558–1580s
“How did Elizabethan sports and pastimes reflect sharp social class divides?”

Elizabethan leisure pastimes were sharply divided by social rank, landownership, and gender. Only nobles and landed gentry owned walled deer parks and game rights for hunting and hawking. Wealthy gentlemen from the Inns of Court also fenced, played indoor real tennis, danced galliards, played the lute, and read classical poetry. Working farmers, labourers, and apprentices played rough parish folk football — rowdy village-wide matches with few rules and frequent broken bones — and wrestled on holy days. Yet blood sports drew every class together: crowds from Privy Councillors to apprentices paid pennies in Southwark to bet on bear-baiting, bull-baiting, and cock-fighting.

STAGE 5 Development of the Theatre 1576–1588
“Why did purpose-built London playhouses thrive despite Puritan anger?”

Secular drama thrived after the 1572 Vagabonds Act required touring actors to hold a nobleman’s licence, creating troupes like Leicester’s Men and the Queen’s Men (1583). In 1576, actor James Burbage built England’s first permanent purpose-built playhouse, ‘The Theatre’, in Shoreditch outside City of London rule, followed by the Curtain (1577) and the Rose (1587). Plays drew up to 2,000 spectators across all classes. Poorer groundlings paid one penny to stand in the open yard around the stage, while wealthy gentry bought covered gallery seats. Conversely, Puritans and London aldermen attacked theatres for spreading bubonic plague, drawing pickpockets, and encouraging idleness.

KEY ENQUIRY “Why did poverty and vagabondage surge, and why did Tudor policy shift from harsh punishment toward parish work relief?”
STAGE 1 Population Growth & Rising Prices 1558–1580s
“How did rapid population growth and bad harvests fuel food inflation?”

Poverty and vagrancy surged across Elizabeth’s reign largely through rapid population growth. England’s population climbed by roughly 35% between 1551 and 1601, rising from 3 million to 4 million (over 4.2 million people by 1603). Towns like London and Norwich grew fastest while farm output lagged behind, driving up food prices and rents. Country hardship worsened whenever wet summers brought bad harvests (especially 1573–77), causing grain shortages and price inflation lingering from earlier Tudor coin debasement. With surplus workers chasing scarce jobs and the 1563 Statute of Artificers capping top pay, real wages fell and landless families went hungry.

STAGE 2 Changes in Farming: Enclosure & Sheep 1558–1580s
“Why did enclosure and profitable sheep farming cause rural eviction?”

Countryside farming changes pushed thousands more labourers off the land. Because raw wool and cloth made up over 80% of exports, gentry landlords increasingly carried out enclosure. They hedged open village strip-fields and common grazing land into private pastures for commercial sheep farming instead of grain. Growing corn employed whole parishes all year, whereas tending enclosed sheep pasture needed barely one tenth the workers. Landlords also raised entry fines sharply (rack-renting). Evicted cottagers and jobless farmhands drifted toward towns — hit further whenever Spanish embargoes halted Antwerp cloth sales — swelling crowds of homeless families.

The Problem of the Poor and Vagrancy — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 Changing Attitudes to the Poor 1560s–1570s
“Why did authorities distinguish ‘impotent poor’ from ‘sturdy beggars’?”

Elizabethan authorities split the poor into two groups. The impotent poor (deserving poor — young orphans, widows, the elderly, sick, and disabled unable to work) received parish relief funded by a compulsory poor rate. By contrast, sturdy beggars (idle or undeserving able-bodied adults) were suspected of choosing laziness over honest work. Fear of wandering vagrants was fuelled by Kent magistrate Thomas Harman’s popular 1567 book A Caveat for Common Cursitors. Harman exposed tricks like ‘counterfeit cranks’ chewing soap to fake fits and ‘Tom o’ Bedlams’ faking madness. Without a police force, gentry JPs feared vagrant gangs spread plague, crime, and rebellion.

STAGE 4 The 1572 Vagabonds Act 1572
“How did the 1572 Vagabonds Act combine whipping with compulsory rates?”

After the 1569 Northern Revolt, Parliament passed the 1572 Vagabonds Act combining local poor taxes with harsh physical punishment. Justices of the Peace had to keep a parish register of deserving poor and appoint Overseers of the Poor. They also collected a weekly compulsory poor rate from rich householders — jailing anyone who refused. At the same time, able-bodied vagrants aged over fourteen caught begging without a licence faced fierce punishment. First offenders were whipped through town until bloody and bored through the ear (burned through the ear) with a hot iron. A second arrest within sixty days meant hanging as a felon.

STAGE 5 The 1576 Act for Relief of the Poor 1576
“How did the 1576 Poor Relief Act pioneer raw wool work and Bridewells?”

By 1576, MPs recognised an important change in attitudes: cloth-trade slumps meant many able-bodied poor genuinely could not find work. The 1576 Poor Relief Act ordered town and parish officials to buy stocks of raw wool, flax, iron, and hemp so unemployed locals could spin yarn and earn wages. Able-bodied vagrants who still refused work were sent to a House of Correction known as Bridewells (named after London’s Bridewell Palace), where strict hard labour punished idleness. Together, the 1572 and 1576 Acts accepted national duty for unemployment and shaped the Elizabethan 1601 Poor Law.

KEY ENQUIRY “How did navigational science, galleon engineering, and Drake’s circumnavigation turn England into an oceanic sea power?”
STAGE 1 New Navigational Technology 1558–1575
“How did astrolabes, quadrants, and Mercator maps transform navigation?”

Elizabethan ocean voyages relied on major advances in navigation and mapmaking. Sailors found their north-south latitude far from land using heavy brass astrolabes and steadier marine quadrants (or John Davis’s back-staff). Measuring the midday sun or Polaris alongside Spanish manuals like The Art of Navigation (1561) kept ships on course. Ocean chart-making took a leap forward in 1569 when Flemish mapmaker Gerardus Mercator published his world map projection placing latitude and longitude on a flat compass grid. Using printed charts, magnetic compasses, and knotted log and line ropes, English captains crossed the Atlantic far more safely.

STAGE 2 Improvements in Ship Design 1558–1577
“How did race-built galleons, lateen sails, and culverins improve ships?”

English royal shipyards also transformed warship design. Replacing slow, top-heavy medieval carracks, Naval Treasurer Sir John Hawkins built sleek race-built galleons from the 1570s able to cross the Atlantic and Pacific Ocean. Longer, lower hulls cut wind drag, rode heavy gales better, and carried larger food and water stores. Large square main sails gave speed downwind, while triangular lateen sails on the mizzenmast allowed sharp windward tacking. Full-length gun decks mounted long-range bronze culverins and demi-culverins, giving English explorers both ocean endurance and heavy broadside firepower.

Exploration and Voyages of Discovery — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 The Search for New Trade Routes 1550s–1570s
“Why did Antwerp’s collapse spur new trade companies and Arctic voyages?”

Economic need pushed English merchants to seek distant markets after Antwerp’s cloth trade collapsed and Spanish embargoes choked wool exports. London investors pooled risk in royal joint-stock companies. The Muscovy Company (1555) traded Russian furs and timber, the Eastland Company (1579) entered the Baltic, and the Levant Company (1581) imported Ottoman spices and silks. Others sought direct sea routes to Asia or sailed to Africa for gold, ivory, and enslaved captives (begun by Hawkins in 1562). Between 1576 and 1587, Martin Frobisher and John Davis braved Arctic icebergs near Baffin Island seeking a Northwest Passage above Canada.

STAGE 4 Drake’s Circumnavigation 1577–1580
“How did Drake navigate the Strait of Magellan and claim Nova Albion?”

In December 1577, Sir Francis Drake sailed from Plymouth Sound with five ships and 164 men — backed in secret by Elizabeth, Walsingham, and Leicester. He aimed to raid Spanish Pacific ports and avenge Spain’s 1568 ambush at San Juan de Ulúa. After fierce storms through the Strait of Magellan, only Drake’s flagship Golden Hind reached the Pacific in September 1578. Sailing north past Valparaíso and Callao, Drake seized the silver-laden Cacafuego before landing up the North American coast in June 1579. Welcomed by friendly Miwok people, he claimed Nova Albion (‘New England’, likely California) for Elizabeth, traded for cloves in the Moluccas, and rounded Africa home to Plymouth in September 1580.

STAGE 5 Significance of Drake’s Voyage 1580–1588
“Why was Drake’s £400,000 return so significant for English sea power?”

Guided by compass and log and line, Drake’s three-year circumnavigation (1577–80) was the first led throughout by an English captain. Returning £400,000 in Spanish treasure gave investors a 4,700% profit (4,700% return), while Elizabeth’s Crown share cleared England’s entire national debt. Captured Pacific sea charts, spice treaties in the Moluccas, and the claim to Nova Albion boosted English maritime pride and inspired Raleigh’s colonial plans. Conversely, Elizabeth’s public Deptford knighting of Drake in April 1581 shattered Spain’s Pacific monopoly and hastened open Anglo-Spanish war.

KEY ENQUIRY “Why did Sir Walter Raleigh promote Virginia, and why did both the 1585 and 1587 Roanoke settlements fail?”
STAGE 1 Raleigh’s Patent & Planning 1584
“How did Raleigh’s royal patent and Hakluyt justify colonising Virginia?”

After his half-brother Humphrey Gilbert drowned, courtier Sir Walter Raleigh received a six-year royal patent from Elizabeth in March 1584 to settle North American lands. Barred by the protective Queen from sailing himself, Raleigh raised £40,000 from investors and named the territory Virginia (where Virginia Dare was later born) for the Virgin Queen. In April 1584, Raleigh sent Philip Amadas and Arthur Barlowe to Roanoke Island, bringing back glowing reports and two Algonquian men, Manteo and Wanchese. Meanwhile, clergyman Richard Hakluyt wrote Discourse on Western Planting (1584). He urged Elizabeth to back Virginia to raid Spain, supply ship timber, and buy English cloth.

STAGE 2 Recruiting Colonists & Leaders APRIL 1585
“Why did recruiting 107 soldiers and gentlemen weaken the 1585 voyage?”

For the April 1585 voyage — two years before the 1587 ‘Lost Colony’ — Raleigh recruited 107 colonists (all men) aboard seven ships. His fiery cousin Sir Richard Grenville commanded at sea, while Ralph Lane governed ashore alongside scientist Thomas Harriot and artist John White, who had learned Algonquian from Manteo and Wanchese. From the outset, poor recruitment and bitter clashes between Grenville and Lane weakened the colony. Roughly half the 107 settlers were soldiers and proud gentlemen expecting quick gold rather than skilled farmers, carpenters, masons, and winter fishermen able to feed an Atlantic settlement.

Raleigh and the Colonisation of Virginia — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 The First Roanoke Colony & Disaster JUNE 1585–1586
“How did the wreck of the Tiger ruin seed corn and force food reliance?”

Leaving Plymouth on 9 April 1585 — already too late to plant spring crops — Ralph Lane’s fleet hit disaster on 29 June 1585. Flagship Tiger struck a sandbar at Ocracoke Inlet, and flooding seawater ruined most of the seed corn, salt, dried peas, and gunpowder. After Grenville sailed home for supplies in August, Lane’s 107 men at Roanoke grew dependent on local Algonquians. They needed Native maize and woven reed Secotan fish traps that Englishmen could neither build nor mend, while European diseases sparked deaths and mistrust inside Native villages.

STAGE 4 Conflict & Evacuation JUNE 1586
“Why did killing Chief Wingina force Lane’s colonists to flee with Drake?”

Relations with the Secotan people collapsed over winter 1585–86. In July 1585, Grenville had already burned Aquascogoc village over a missing silver cup. By spring 1586, short of food during a severe drought, Secotan ruler Chief Wingina (who took the name Pemisapan) halted maize trade and organised local resistance. Fearing an attack, Governor Ralph Lane staged a pre-emptive 1 June 1586 ambush at Dasamonquepeuc, luring Wingina to a parley and killing Chief Wingina. Algonquian tribes cut off all food at once; facing starvation, the colonists fled Roanoke on 18 June 1586 aboard Sir Francis Drake’s passing fleet.

STAGE 5 The ‘Lost Colony’ & Significance 1587–1590
“Why did the 1588 Spanish Armada leave John White’s Roanoke settlers stranded?”

Learning from 1585’s mistakes, Raleigh sent artist-governor John White back in May 1587 with 118 men, women, and children promised 500 acres each around Chesapeake Bay in Virginia. Instead, Portuguese pilot Simon Fernandez landed them back on Roanoke Island, where Manteo became Lord of Roanoke and Virginia Dare was born. After hostile Algonquians killed colonist George Howe, settlers persuaded White to sail home for urgent supplies in August 1587. Tragically, the 1588 Spanish Armada caused Elizabeth to keep every English ship at home; when White returned in August 1590, Roanoke stood deserted with ‘CROATOAN’ carved on a post.
