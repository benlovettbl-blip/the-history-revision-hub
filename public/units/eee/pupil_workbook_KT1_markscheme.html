Grade 9 Model Answer (2 / 2 Marks):

One feature of the French threat in 1558 was the strategic encirclement of England through France’s traditional Auld Alliance with Scotland. Battle-hardened French garrison troops were stationed along the Scottish border under Regent Mary of Guise while England had just lost Calais in January 1558, exposing the realm to a two-front Catholic invasion.

Explain why Elizabeth’s legitimacy, gender and marriage were a problem for her in 1558.
Stimulus bullets printed: • Henry VIII’s marriage to Anne Boleyn • The succession (+ mandatory own knowledge)
Edexcel Paper 2 (1HI0/B4) Q2 Explain Why Tariff (12 Marks — AO1: 6, AO2: 6): Level 1 (1–3m): Simple statements • Level 2 (4–6m): Descriptive or capped at 6m if confined to stimulus • Level 3 (7–9m): Explained causation with specific detail & own knowledge • Level 4 (10–12m): Sustained analytical explanation across 3 coherent reasons integrating accurate own knowledge beyond the two stimulus bullets.
Grade 9 Model Answer (12 / 12 Marks):

Elizabeth’s disputed legitimacy posed an acute dynastic threat in 1558 because devout Catholics at home and abroad rejected Henry VIII’s divorce from Catherine of Aragon and his 1533 marriage to Anne Boleyn. Under Roman Catholic canon law—and even Henry VIII’s own 1536 Act of Succession passed after Anne Boleyn’s execution—Elizabeth was viewed by strict Catholics as illegitimate and barred from inheriting the throne. Consequently, Catholic powers such as France promoted Elizabeth’s cousin Mary, Queen of Scots—married to the French Dauphin Francis—as England’s rightful Catholic monarch, encouraging Mary to quarter the English royal coat of arms alongside her own.

Second, sixteenth-century patriarchal attitudes made Elizabeth’s gender an immediate political problem. Contemporary society believed the Great Chain of Being placed men in authority over women, viewing female monarchs as emotionally weak, indecisive, and incapable of leading royal armies into battle. Recent painful memories of Mary I’s reign—marked by disastrous harvests, nearly 300 Protestant burnings, financial ruin, and the humiliating loss of Calais in January 1558—deepened elite prejudice that a woman ruling alone would invite domestic rebellion and foreign invasion.

Finally, the problem of marriage and the succession trapped Elizabeth in an almost impossible political dilemma. Because Elizabeth ascended at twenty-five with no Protestant heir, her death from illness such as smallpox threatened to plunge England into civil war, prompting the Privy Council and Parliament to press her relentlessly to marry. Yet marrying an English nobleman like Robert Dudley risked fierce court faction fighting, whereas marrying a foreign Catholic prince such as Philip II of Spain risked repeating Mary I’s unpopular Spanish marriage and surrendering English independence to foreign control.

Page 5 • Question 2: Explain Why [12 Marks] 12 Marks
Explain why Elizabeth’s religious settlement (1559) took the form it did in 1559.
Stimulus bullets printed: • Religious divisions in 1558 • The French threat (+ mandatory own knowledge)
Edexcel Paper 2 (1HI0/B4) Q2 Explain Why Tariff (12 Marks — AO1: 6, AO2: 6): Level 1 (1–3m): Simple statements • Level 2 (4–6m): Descriptive or capped at 6m if confined to stimulus • Level 3 (7–9m): Explained causation with specific detail & own knowledge • Level 4 (10–12m): Sustained analytical explanation across 3 coherent reasons integrating accurate own knowledge beyond the two stimulus bullets.
Grade 9 Model Answer (12 / 12 Marks):

Elizabeth’s 1559 religious settlement took the form of a cautious Protestant ‘middle way’ primarily to heal deep domestic religious divisions without provoking civil war. While London, East Anglia, and returning Marian exiles from Geneva demanded radical Protestant reform, the majority of rural parishes—especially powerful gentry and earls in the north and west—remained conservatively attached to traditional Catholic ceremonies, and twenty-six Marian bishops initially opposed change in the House of Lords. Consequently, Elizabeth blended Protestant theology with reassuring outward continuity: she took the diplomatic title ‘Supreme Governor’ rather than ‘Supreme Head’, combined the 1549 and 1552 communion wordings in the Book of Common Prayer, kept clergy vestments, and set the recusancy fine at a modest 12 pence (one shilling) per missed Sunday.

Second, dangerous foreign threats in 1558–59 forced Elizabeth to avoid an aggressively anti-Catholic settlement that might unite Catholic superpowers against England. Having just lost Calais, England faced French troops stationed in Scotland backing Mary, Queen of Scots’ rival Catholic claim, while Elizabeth desperately needed the diplomatic neutrality of Catholic King Philip II of Spain before the Treaty of Cateau-Cambrésis was signed in April 1559. Retaining traditional church ornaments, bishops, and moderate enforcement convinced Philip II and most European observers that England had not fallen to radical Calvinist extremism, averting an immediate Catholic crusade.

Finally, Elizabeth’s own personal religious convictions and fierce defence of the royal prerogative shaped the exact structure of the settlement. Raised as a moderate Protestant, Elizabeth genuinely favoured dignified ceremony, choral music, and episcopal hierarchy—where bishops answered directly to the monarch—over radical Puritan congregational equality. Establishing a state church governed through bishops, the Oath of Supremacy, and fifty-seven Royal Injunctions enabled Elizabeth and William Cecil to secure firm royal control over every pulpit in England while demanding outward conformity rather than persecuting private conscience.

‘The Catholic challenge was a greater threat to Elizabeth’s religious settlement than the Puritan challenge in the years 1559–69.’ How far do you agree? Explain your answer.
Stimulus bullets printed: • The role of the Catholic nobility • The Puritan challenge (+ mandatory own knowledge)
Edexcel Paper 2 (1HI0/B4) Q3/Q4 Evaluative Essay Tariff (16 Marks — AO1: 6, AO2: 10, ZERO SPaG): Level 1 (1–4m): Simple/generalised assertions • Level 2 (5–8m): Descriptive or one-sided explanation • Level 3 (9–12m): Balanced analysis for & against using own knowledge beyond the stimulus • Level 4 (13–16m): Sustained evaluative argument weighing factors against explicit criteria to reach a justified overall verdict.
Grade 9 Model Answer (16 / 16 Marks):

Between 1559 and 1569, the Catholic challenge posed a substantially greater threat to Elizabeth’s religious settlement than the Puritan challenge because Catholic opposition combined powerful northern aristocratic armies, a rival claimant to the throne, and dangerous foreign powers, whereas Puritans lacked foreign backing or any desire to depose the Queen.

On the one hand, the Catholic challenge carried mortal political and military danger, particularly in the late 1560s. Roughly one-third of the English nobility and gentry—concentrated in the north under ancient magnates like the Earls of Northumberland and Westmorland—remained loyal to Catholicism, sheltering Marian priests while lax local JPs ignored recusancy. Although Pope Pius IV issued no immediate excommunication in 1559–66 and many Catholics acted as outward ‘Church Papists’, international Catholic pressure escalated sharply after 1567 when Philip II sent the Duke of Alba with 10,000 veteran Spanish troops to crush Protestants in the nearby Netherlands and Elizabeth seized the £85,000 Genoese Loan in 1568. Once Catholic Mary, Queen of Scots fled to England in May 1568, northern Catholic earls gained a legitimate figurehead, culminating in armed insurrection in November 1569 where rebels tore up English Bibles and celebrated Latin Mass in Durham Cathedral.

On the other hand, Puritans mounted vocal clerical opposition inside the Church of England during the early 1560s. Returning Marian exiles influenced by John Calvin demanded the removal of ‘popish’ ornaments: Puritan bishops threatened to resign until Elizabeth removed the silver crucifix from her Royal Chapel, and reformist clergy came within a single vote of abolishing saint days and organs at the 1563 Convocation. Friction peaked in the 1566 Vestments Controversy when clergy refused to wear the white surplice; however, when Archbishop Matthew Parker issued the Book of Advertisements and sacked 37 non-conforming London preachers, most Puritans reluctantly conformed.

Overall, I strongly agree with the statement. While Puritans noisily challenged church ceremony, they remained fiercely loyal to a Protestant Queen because replacing Elizabeth meant Catholic rule. Conversely, the Catholic challenge possessed noble militias, papal sympathy, Alba’s nearby Spanish army, and Mary Stuart’s rival claim—making it a far graver threat to both the settlement and Elizabeth’s crown by 1569.

Describe one feature of relations between Elizabeth and Mary, Queen of Scots, in the years 1568–69.
Grade 9 Model Answer (2 / 2 Marks):

One feature of relations between Elizabeth and Mary in 1568–69 was Elizabeth’s decision to detain Mary under guarded house arrest while convening the York and Westminster Inquiry (October 1568–January 1569). By examining the disputed Casket Letters without passing a formal guilty or innocent verdict, Elizabeth avoided restoring Mary to Scotland while keeping her neutralized in English custody.

‘Mary, Queen of Scots’ claim to the English throne was the main problem in relations between Elizabeth and Mary in the years 1568–69.’ How far do you agree? Explain your answer.
Stimulus bullets printed: • Mary’s arrival in England (1568) • The Catholic nobility (+ mandatory own knowledge)
Edexcel Paper 2 (1HI0/B4) Q3/Q4 Evaluative Essay Tariff (16 Marks — AO1: 6, AO2: 10, ZERO SPaG): Level 1 (1–4m): Simple/generalised assertions • Level 2 (5–8m): Descriptive or one-sided explanation • Level 3 (9–12m): Balanced analysis for & against using own knowledge beyond the stimulus • Level 4 (13–16m): Sustained evaluative argument weighing factors against explicit criteria to reach a justified overall verdict.
Grade 9 Model Answer (16 / 16 Marks):

Mary, Queen of Scots’ legitimate Catholic claim to the English throne was indeed the underlying root problem in relations between the two queens in 1568–69, because without that hereditary claim neither her arrival in England nor English noble conspiracies would have endangered Elizabeth’s crown.

First, Mary’s dynastic bloodline made her uniquely dangerous. As the granddaughter of Henry VIII’s elder sister Margaret Tudor, Mary possessed an unimpeachable legitimate Tudor pedigree, whereas Roman Catholics regarded Elizabeth as the illegitimate daughter of Anne Boleyn. Unlike Elizabeth, Mary had also given birth to an heir (James VI) in 1566. Consequently, from the moment she quartered the English royal arms, Catholic Europe and English traditionalists viewed Mary not merely as a neighbouring monarch, but as the rightful Catholic alternative waiting to replace Elizabeth.

Second, however, Mary’s sudden arrival in England in May 1568 turned a distant dynastic claim into an immediate constitutional and diplomatic crisis. Having fled Lochleven Castle after the murder of her husband Lord Darnley at Kirk o’ Field and her scandalous marriage to the Earl of Bothwell, Mary crossed the Solway Firth demanding English troops to crush Scotland’s Protestant regency under the Earl of Moray. Every option damaged Elizabeth: helping Mary risked deposing a friendly Protestant Scottish government; handing her back risked executing an anointed sovereign; freeing her risked her seeking French military aid. Even the 1568–69 York Inquiry over the eight Casket Letters could only manage—not solve—the crisis by issuing an inconclusive verdict to justify indefinite detention under the Earl of Shrewsbury.

Third, the ambition of England’s nobility and court factions turned Mary’s captivity into active conspiracy in 1569. Protestant courtiers including Robert Dudley secretly proposed marrying Mary to England’s premier nobleman, the Duke of Norfolk, to settle the succession, while Catholic Earls of Northumberland and Westmorland went further—planning to wed Mary to Norfolk, overthrow Cecil, and restore Catholicism by force.

In conclusion, I largely agree with the statement. While Mary’s flight to England in May 1568 and the scheming of English nobles triggered the immediate crisis of 1568–69, both only mattered because Mary possessed a powerful, legitimate Catholic claim to Elizabeth’s throne; without that bloodline claim, she would have been a powerless exile rather than a mortal magnet for rebellion.

Pages 14–15 • Question 2: Explain Why [12 Marks] (Unscaffolded) 12 Marks (AO1: 6 + AO2: 6)
2  Explain why Elizabeth faced challenges to the religious settlement in the years 1559–69.
Stimulus bullets printed: • The Puritan challenge • Foreign powers (Candidates must also include own knowledge beyond the stimulus to progress above Level 2 / 6 marks.)
Level 4 (10–12 Marks): Three analytical causation paragraphs consistently directed at the conceptual focus of the question, supported by precise dates/terminology and own knowledge beyond the two stimulus bullets.
Grade 9 Model Answer (12 / 12 Marks):

First, Elizabeth faced vocal domestic challenges from radical Puritans who believed the 1559 settlement was an incomplete ‘half-reformed’ compromise that retained popish superstitions. Influenced by Calvinist Geneva during Mary I’s reign, returning Marian exiles objected fiercely to crucifixes, organs, saint days, and clerical vestments, viewing the white linen surplice as ‘rags of Rome’. This provoked the 1562 Crucifix Controversy in the Royal Chapel, a narrow one-vote battle in the 1563 Convocation, and open clerical defiance in 1566 when 37 London ministers refused Archbishop Parker’s Book of Advertisements and had to be sacked.

Second, deep-rooted Catholic loyalty among England’s northern nobility and rural gentry created persistent resistance to Protestant uniformity. Roughly one-third of peers and a majority of northern JPs remained sympathetic to Catholicism; thousands of ‘Church Papists’ conformed only outwardly on Sundays while sheltering former Marian priests to celebrate Latin Mass in manor houses. Once Pope Pius V instructed Catholics in 1566 to boycott Anglican worship—and especially after Catholic claimant Mary, Queen of Scots arrived in England in May 1568—northern earls like Northumberland and Westmorland rallied around restoring Catholicism, culminating in the 1569 Revolt of the Northern Earls.

Finally, shifting actions by Catholic foreign powers steadily intensified pressure on the settlement by the late 1560s. Although France was distracted by its Wars of Religion (1562) and Philip II initially preserved friendship to counter French influence, the Counter-Reformation sharpened international hostility: William Allen founded the Douai seminary in 1568 to train missionary priests, while Philip II dispatched the Duke of Alba with 10,000 ruthless Spanish troops to the Netherlands in 1567 right across the Channel. Combined with the 1568 Genoese Loan crisis, Alba’s nearby Catholic army emboldened English Catholic conspirators to believe foreign military help might back a rebellion against Elizabeth’s Church.
