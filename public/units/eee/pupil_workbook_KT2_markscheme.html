Explain why Mary, Queen of Scots was executed in 1587.
Stimulus bullets printed: • The Babington Plot • Walsingham’s spies (+ mandatory own knowledge)
Edexcel Paper 2 (1HI0/B4) Q2 Explain Why Tariff (12 Marks — AO1: 6, AO2: 6): Level 1 (1–3m): Simple statements • Level 2 (4–6m): Descriptive or capped at 6m if confined to stimulus • Level 3 (7–9m): Explained causation with specific detail & own knowledge • Level 4 (10–12m): Sustained analytical explanation across 3 coherent reasons integrating accurate own knowledge beyond the two stimulus bullets.
Grade 9 Model Answer (12 / 12 Marks):

The immediate trigger for Mary, Queen of Scots’ execution in February 1587 was her proven personal treason in the 1586 Babington Plot. Unlike the earlier Ridolfi (1571) and Throckmorton (1583) plots where Elizabeth could cling to doubts over Mary’s direct involvement, Catholic conspirator Anthony Babington planned for six gentlemen to assassinate Elizabeth while a foreign Catholic army invaded to free Mary. On 17 July 1586, Mary wrote back explicitly approving Elizabeth’s murder; under the 1584 Bond of Association and the 1585 Act for the Queen’s Safety, this signed endorsement of regicide made Mary legally liable to trial and execution at Fotheringhay Castle.

Second, Sir Francis Walsingham’s intelligence network and spies proved decisive in manufacturing irrefutable legal proof to overcome Elizabeth’s reluctance to execute an anointed monarch. Walsingham turned Catholic courier Gilbert Gifford into a double agent who intercepted every ciphered letter smuggled inside waterproof beer barrels at Chartley Manor, passing them to master codebreaker Thomas Phelippes. Once Phelippes decoded Mary’s fatal consent and forged a gallows postscript to expose Babington’s accomplices, Cecil and Parliament possessed unassailable evidence that forced Elizabeth to sign the death warrant.

Finally, the outbreak of open war with Spain after August 1585 made keeping Mary alive an intolerable national security risk. Since Pope Pius V’s 1570 bull Regnans in Excelsis had absolved Catholics from obedience to Elizabeth, Philip II was actively assembling an invasion Armada by 1586–87 to overthrow Protestant England. Privy Councillors warned Elizabeth that as long as Mary lived, foreign invaders and domestic Catholic plotters possessed a legitimate Catholic Queen waiting to take the throne; executing Mary in February 1587 permanently eliminated that domestic figurehead before Spanish warships could sail.

‘Commercial rivalry was the main reason for rivalry between England and Spain in the years 1569–85.’ How far do you agree? Explain your answer.

Edexcel Paper 2 (1HI0/B4) Q3/Q4 Evaluative Essay Tariff (16 Marks — AO1: 6, AO2: 10, ZERO SPaG): Level 1 (1–4m): Simple/generalised assertions • Level 2 (5–8m): Descriptive or one-sided explanation • Level 3 (9–12m): Balanced analysis for & against using own knowledge beyond the stimulus • Level 4 (13–16m): Sustained evaluative argument weighing factors against explicit criteria to reach a justified overall verdict.
Grade 9 Model Answer (16 / 16 Marks):

While commercial rivalry and Francis Drake’s audacious privateering provoked bitter Spanish anger throughout the 1570s, political and strategic rivalry over the Spanish Netherlands—intensified by religious hostility—was ultimately the decisive reason Anglo-Spanish relations collapsed into war by 1585.

Commercial rivalry certainly poisoned relations between 1569 and 1581. Following Alba’s trade embargo on Antwerp after the 1568 Genoese Loan seizure, English merchants led by John Hawkins and Francis Drake sought to break Philip II’s strict imperial trade monopoly over the New World. Backed secretly by Elizabeth and court investors, Drake ambushed Spanish silver mule trains at Nombre de Dios in 1572 and raided the Pacific coast during his 1577–80 circumnavigation, seizing £140,000 from the Cacafuego alone and returning roughly £400,000 in bullion to Plymouth. When Elizabeth publicly defied Spanish ambassador Mendoza by boarding the Golden Hind at Deptford to knight Drake in April 1581, Philip II viewed Elizabeth as a state sponsor of piracy.

However, religious rivalry steadily transformed commercial friction into an ideological conflict. Devoutly Catholic Philip II saw himself as the champion of the Counter-Reformation, especially after Pope Pius V excommunicated Elizabeth in 1570. Spain backed Catholic conspiracies to put Mary, Queen of Scots on the English throne—promising 10,000 troops in the 1571 Ridolfi Plot and 200,000 ducats in the 1583 Throckmorton Plot—while Elizabeth executed Jesuit missionaries and sheltered Protestant Dutch ‘Sea Beggars’.

Above all, geopolitical security in the Netherlands proved the decisive trigger that turned cold war into open conflict. England could never tolerate a hostile Spanish army controlling deep-water Channel ports just miles from London. While Elizabeth preferred covert subsidies to Dutch Protestant leader William of Orange during the 1570s, two events in 1584 shattered the balance of power: William of Orange was assassinated in July 1584, and Philip II signed the Catholic Treaty of Joinville with the French Guise family in December 1584. Faced with total Dutch collapse and Catholic encirclement, Elizabeth signed the Treaty of Nonsuch in August 1585, sending 7,400 English soldiers to fight Spain directly.

Overall, I disagree that commercial rivalry was the main reason. Privateering for silver inflamed pride on both sides, yet Philip II tolerated Drake’s raids for over a decade without invading; it was the strategic and religious crisis in the Netherlands in 1584–85 that compelled England and Spain to go to war.

‘The actions of Robert Dudley were the main reason for deteriorating relations with Spain in the years 1585–88.’ How far do you agree? Explain your answer.

Grade 9 Model Answer (16 / 16 Marks):

Robert Dudley’s provocative actions in the Netherlands certainly deepened Philip II’s determination to defeat England, yet they were part of a wider strategic escalation in which Elizabeth’s Treaty of Nonsuch (1585), Mary Stuart’s execution (1587), and Francis Drake’s raid on Cadiz (1587) played equally decisive roles in turning tension into full-scale invasion by 1588.

On the one hand, Dudley’s actions in 1585–87 directly challenged Philip II’s sovereignty over the Spanish Netherlands. In January 1586, without Elizabeth’s permission, Dudley accepted the supreme title of ‘Governor-General of the Netherlands’, implying England intended to depose Philip II permanently as ruler of the Dutch provinces rather than broker a compromise peace. Although Dudley’s campaign suffered from chronic underfunding and English defections by Sir William Stanley at Deventer and Rowland Yorke at Zutphen in 1587, his garrison successfully defended deep-water Channel ports such as Ostend and Flushing—convincing Philip II that he could never reconquer the Dutch rebels until England itself was neutralized.

On the other hand, Elizabeth’s foundational decision to sign the Treaty of Nonsuch in August 1585 had already crossed the diplomatic point of no return before Dudley even set foot in Flushing. Financing 7,400 English troops to fight Philip’s commander, the Duke of Parma, amounted to an open act of war, prompting Philip II immediately to seize English shipping in Spanish ports and begin planning the ‘Enterprise of England’ in late 1585.

Furthermore, events in 1587 escalated hostilities to irreversible naval warfare. In February 1587, Elizabeth executed Catholic Mary, Queen of Scots, prompting Pope Sixtus V to promise Philip II one million gold ducats once Spanish troops landed on English soil. Two months later, in April 1587, Elizabeth sent Francis Drake to raid Cadiz harbour, where he humiliated Spain by destroying 30 vessels and burning seasoned oak barrel staves along the Algarve coast—infuriating Philip and locking Spain onto its 1588 Armada launch.

In conclusion, I only partly agree. Dudley’s acceptance of the Governor-Generalship insulted Spanish sovereignty and blocked Parma from deep-water ports, but it was a consequence of Elizabeth’s Treaty of Nonsuch—and it was Mary’s execution alongside Drake’s devastating raid on Cadiz in 1587 that drove Anglo-Spanish relations to all-out invasion in 1588.

Page 11 • Question 2: Explain Why [12 Marks] 12 Marks
Explain why the English achieved victory over the Spanish Armada in 1588.
Stimulus bullets printed: • Spanish invasion plans • English ship design and tactics (+ mandatory own knowledge)
Edexcel Paper 2 (1HI0/B4) Q2 Explain Why Tariff (12 Marks — AO1: 6, AO2: 6): Level 1 (1–3m): Simple statements • Level 2 (4–6m): Descriptive or capped at 6m if confined to stimulus • Level 3 (7–9m): Explained causation with specific detail & own knowledge • Level 4 (10–12m): Sustained analytical explanation across 3 coherent reasons integrating accurate own knowledge beyond the two stimulus bullets.
Grade 9 Model Answer (12 / 12 Marks):

Fatal weaknesses in Philip II’s Spanish invasion plans laid the foundation for English victory before a single shot was fired in the Channel. Philip’s rigid strategy required the Duke of Medina Sidonia’s 130-ship fleet to rendezvous off Dunkirk with the Duke of Parma’s 27,000 soldiers, yet Spain possessed no deep-water harbour in the Netherlands: Dutch Justin of Nassau’s ‘Sea Beggars’ blockaded Parma’s flat-bottomed barges in the shallow canals while messages between Sidonia and Parma took up to a week to arrive. Worse still, inexperienced commander Medina Sidonia replaced veteran Admiral Santa Cruz, while Drake’s 1587 Cadiz raid had burned seasoned barrel staves, leaving Spanish food rotting in leaky green-wood casks and cannons supplied with mismatched iron shot.

Second, superior English ship design and aggressive naval tactics shattered the Armada once it anchored helplessly off Calais. John Hawkins’ fast, low-profile ‘race-built’ galleons outperformed high-castled Spanish vessels designed for grappling and boarding, while English gun crews mounted long-range culverins on compact four-wheel carriages to fire rapid, destructive broadsides. Crucially, on the night of 7 August 1588, Lord Howard and Francis Drake sent eight burning, pitch-soaked fireships into Calais Roads, panicking Spanish captains into cutting their anchor cables and breaking their protective crescent formation so English broadsides could batter isolated ships at close range at the Battle of Gravelines on 8 August.

Finally, shifting weather—celebrated in England as the ‘Protestant Wind’—turned tactical defeat at Gravelines into total strategic catastrophe. Strong south-westerly winds prevented Medina Sidonia from turning back to collect Parma’s army, driving the damaged Spanish fleet north around uncharted Scotland and western Ireland into ferocious late-summer Atlantic gales. Because Spanish captains had cut their anchors to escape the Calais fireships, dozens of crippled galleons were driven onto Scottish and Irish reefs, ensuring only 67 shattered vessels ever returned to Spain.

Pages 14–15 • Question 2: Explain Why [12 Marks] (Unscaffolded) 12 Marks (AO1: 6 + AO2: 6)
2  Explain why relations with Spain deteriorated in the years 1585–88.
Stimulus bullets printed: • English direct involvement in the Netherlands • Drake and the raid on Cadiz (Candidates must also include own knowledge beyond the stimulus to progress above Level 2 / 6 marks.)
Level 4 (10–12 Marks): Three analytical causation paragraphs consistently directed at the conceptual focus of the question, supported by precise dates/terminology and own knowledge beyond the two stimulus bullets.
Grade 9 Model Answer (12 / 12 Marks):

First, English direct military involvement in the Netherlands from August 1585 transformed years of covert rivalry into open undeclared war with Spain. Alarmed by William of Orange’s assassination and the Franco-Spanish Catholic Treaty of Joinville (1584), Elizabeth signed the Treaty of Nonsuch in August 1585, dispatching 7,400 English soldiers under Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester, to fight Philip II’s army under the Duke of Parma. Relations worsened dramatically in January 1586 when Dudley accepted the title ‘Governor-General of the Netherlands’, which Philip II viewed as an intolerable usurpation of his rightful Habsburg sovereignty, prompting Philip to seize English merchant ships and begin assembling an invasion fleet.

Second, Francis Drake’s aggressive naval raids between 1585 and 1587 repeatedly humiliated Spain and forced Philip II toward full-scale retaliation. After raiding Spanish colonial settlements at Santo Domingo and Cartagena in the Caribbean in 1585–86, Drake sailed 24 ships straight into Cadiz harbour in April 1587 (‘singeing the King of Spain’s beard’), destroying around 30 Spanish vessels and burning thousands of seasoned barrel staves along the Algarve coast before capturing the rich treasure carrack San Felipe. Although this delayed the Armada by twelve months, the public humiliation hardened Philip II’s determination to crush English naval piracy permanently.

Finally, the execution of Catholic Mary, Queen of Scots at Fotheringhay Castle in February 1587 eliminated any remaining hope of peaceful Catholic succession and turned Anglo-Spanish tension into a religious crusade. Before her death, Mary bequeathed her claim to the English throne not to her Protestant son James VI, but directly to King Philip II of Spain. Backed by Pope Sixtus V’s promise of one million gold ducats once Spanish soldiers landed in England, Philip II now possessed both dynastic and papal justification to launch the 130-ship Spanish Armada in May 1588.
