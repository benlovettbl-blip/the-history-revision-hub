1
(a) Describe one feature of Elizabethan grammar schools. (2)
Teacher Mark Scheme • Grade 9 Exemplar (2 / 2 Marks)

One feature was a classical curriculum provided for bright boys from gentry and merchant families. Pupils studied Latin, Greek, and ancient authors during strict ten-hour days starting at 6 a.m.

(b) Describe one feature of Elizabethan pastimes for the rich. (2)
Teacher Mark Scheme • Grade 9 Exemplar (2 / 2 Marks)

One feature was exclusive aristocratic sports unavailable to labourers. Wealthy nobles practised hawking with trained falcons, deer hunting inside private parks, and indoor real tennis.

2
Explain why the Babington Plot (1586) led to the execution of Mary, Queen of Scots. (12)
You may use the following in your answer:
  • Walsingham’s spies
  • The Act for the Preservation of the Queen’s Safety
You must also use information of your own.
Teacher Mark Scheme • Grade 9 Exemplar (12 / 12 Marks)

First, Sir Francis Walsingham’s intelligence network produced irrefutable written proof of Mary’s personal treason. Using double agent Gilbert Gifford, Walsingham monitored ciphered letters smuggled inside beer barrels at Chartley Hall between Mary and Catholic conspirator Anthony Babington. Cryptographer Thomas Phelippes decoded Mary’s July 1586 reply explicitly endorsing six gentlemen to assassinate Elizabeth, removing the plausible deniability that had shielded Mary during earlier plots.

Crucially, this intercepted evidence activated the 1585 Act for the Preservation of the Queen’s Safety. Passed by Parliament after the Bond of Association, this statute stipulated that if anyone plotted against Elizabeth’s life, a special commission would investigate and exclude any guilty claimant from the succession, rendering them liable to execution. Tried under this exact law in October 1586, Mary was convicted of high treason, leaving Elizabeth with dwindling legal grounds to delay.

Finally, the imminent danger of foreign Catholic invasion in 1586–87 forced Elizabeth and her Privy Council to eliminate Mary permanently as a living Catholic figurehead. England was already at war with Spain in the Netherlands following the 1585 Treaty of Nonsuch, and Babington’s plot relied on 60,000 foreign troops led by the Duke of Guise and backed by Philip II, who was actively assembling his Armada. Terrified of a Catholic rising, Privy Councillors secretly dispatched the signed warrant to execute Mary at Fotheringhay Castle in February 1587.

3
“The religious settlement of 1559 successfully solved the problem of religion in England.”
How far do you agree? Explain your answer. (16)
You may use the following in your answer:
  • Catholic recusancy
  • Puritan opposition
You must also use information of your own.
Teacher Mark Scheme • Grade 9 Exemplar (16 / 16 Marks)

In securing broad outward conformity and preventing religious civil war, the 1559 Religious Settlement achieved remarkable domestic stability. By naming Elizabeth ‘Supreme Governor’ under the Act of Supremacy and blending a Protestant English Book of Common Prayer with traditional clerical ornaments under the Act of Uniformity, her ‘Middle Way’ satisfied moderates. Around 8,000 of roughly 10,000 parish clergy swore the Oath of Supremacy, while mild initial shilling fines allowed most traditionalists to conform quietly as Church Papists.

However, persistent Catholic recusancy and Counter-Reformation plots prove the settlement did not permanently resolve Catholic opposition. Northern nobles rebelled to restore Latin Mass in 1569, and Pope Pius V’s 1570 Papal Bull turned obedience to Elizabeth into heresy. From 1574, Douai seminary priests and Jesuits such as Edmund Campion arrived secretly to sustain recusancy among wealthy gentry, forcing Parliament to raise recusancy fines to £20 a month in 1581 and treat Catholic priests as traitors during the Ridolfi, Throckmorton, and Babington plots.

Simultaneously, zealous Puritans challenged the settlement from within Protestantism because they viewed vestments and crucifixes as ‘popish’ remnants. Thirty-seven London ministers lost their livings during the 1566 Vestments Controversy for refusing Archbishop Parker’s Book of Advertisements, while Elizabeth had to suspend Archbishop Edmund Grindal in 1577 when he refused to ban Puritan ‘prophesyings’.

Overall, I largely agree with the statement. Although militant Catholic plotters and vocal Puritan clergy prevented complete religious consensus before 1588, the 1559 settlement won the loyal obedience of the overwhelming majority of ordinary English subjects and spared England the bloody religious wars tearing apart contemporary Europe.

4
“Elizabeth’s government was completely dependent on the Privy Council.”
How far do you agree? Explain your answer. (16)
You may use the following in your answer:
  • William Cecil
  • Parliament
You must also use information of your own.
Teacher Mark Scheme • Grade 9 Exemplar (16 / 16 Marks)

The Privy Council was undeniably the central engine of day-to-day Elizabethan government. Slimmed down to roughly nineteen trusted ministers meeting several times a week, it directed foreign diplomacy, finance, military defence, and enforcement of the Religious Settlement. Indispensable administrators such as Secretary of State William Cecil (Lord Burghley) drafted royal policy and managed parliamentary business, while Sir Francis Walsingham ran the espionage network that uncovered the Throckmorton and Babington plots to save Elizabeth’s life.

Nevertheless, Elizabeth’s government was never wholly dependent on the Privy Council because critical constitutional functions required Parliament. Although summoned only thirteen times across her reign, solely Parliament could enact binding statutes—such as the 1559 Acts of Supremacy and Uniformity or the 1572 Vagabonds Act—and grant extraordinary taxation subsidies needed to fund war against Spain. Even so, Elizabeth strictly guarded her Royal Prerogative, forbidding MPs from debating marriage, religion, or foreign policy.

Furthermore, lacking a standing army or paid police force, royal rule across the counties relied heavily on local unpaid officials and court patronage. Lords Lieutenant mustered county militias against invasion threats, while hundreds of gentry Justices of the Peace enforced the poor rate, fixed wages, and punished recusants locally. Above all stood Elizabeth herself, who deliberately balanced rival councillors like Cecil and Robert Dudley to retain ultimate royal control.

Overall, I partially agree with the statement. While the Privy Council under Cecil and Walsingham was Elizabeth’s indispensable executive instrument, effective Tudor government depended on an interlocking partnership combining Council administration, Parliamentary taxation and statute law, local JP enforcement, and Elizabeth’s personal authority.
