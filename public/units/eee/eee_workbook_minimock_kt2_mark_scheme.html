Page 14 • Question 1(a) & 1(b): Describe One Feature [4 Marks — 2 × 2m] Mark 1(a) out of 2 and 1(b) out of 2 Independently
Pearson Edexcel 1HI0/B4 Q1 Marking Rule: Award 1 mark for identifying a valid feature and 1 mark for specific supporting factual detail. Do not award 2 marks for two bare assertions without supporting detail.
1 (a)  Describe one feature of English direct involvement in the Netherlands.
Question 1(a) — Grade 9 Model Answer (2 / 2 Marks):

One feature of English direct involvement in the Netherlands was the August 1585 Treaty of Nonsuch. Under its terms, Elizabeth agreed to fund and dispatch 7,400 English soldiers under Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester, to protect Dutch Protestant rebels and Channel ports against Spain.

1 (b)  Describe one feature of the significance of Mary, Queen of Scots’ execution in 1587.
Question 1(b) — Grade 9 Model Answer (2 / 2 Marks):

One feature of the significance of Mary’s execution in February 1587 was that it removed the main Catholic figurehead for domestic plots inside England while provoking international fury from Philip II of Spain, who accelerated preparations for the 1588 Armada to claim the English crown himself.

Pages 14–15 • Question 2: Explain Why [12 Marks] (Unscaffolded) 12 Marks (AO1: 6 + AO2: 6)
2  Explain why relations with Spain deteriorated in the years 1585–88.
Stimulus bullets printed: • English direct involvement in the Netherlands • Drake and the raid on Cadiz (Candidates must also include own knowledge beyond the stimulus to progress above Level 2 / 6 marks.)
Level 4 (10–12 Marks): Three analytical causation paragraphs consistently directed at the conceptual focus of the question, supported by precise dates/terminology and own knowledge beyond the two stimulus bullets.
Grade 9 Model Answer (12 / 12 Marks):

First, English direct military involvement in the Netherlands from August 1585 transformed years of covert rivalry into open undeclared war with Spain. Alarmed by William of Orange’s assassination and the Franco-Spanish Catholic Treaty of Joinville (1584), Elizabeth signed the Treaty of Nonsuch in August 1585, dispatching 7,400 English soldiers under Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester, to fight Philip II’s army under the Duke of Parma. Relations worsened dramatically in January 1586 when Dudley accepted the title ‘Governor-General of the Netherlands’, which Philip II viewed as an intolerable usurpation of his rightful Habsburg sovereignty, prompting Philip to seize English merchant ships and begin assembling an invasion fleet.

Second, Francis Drake’s aggressive naval raids between 1585 and 1587 repeatedly humiliated Spain and forced Philip II toward full-scale retaliation. After raiding Spanish colonial settlements at Santo Domingo and Cartagena in the Caribbean in 1585–86, Drake sailed 24 ships straight into Cadiz harbour in April 1587 (‘singeing the King of Spain’s beard’), destroying around 30 Spanish vessels and burning thousands of seasoned barrel staves along the Algarve coast before capturing the rich treasure carrack San Felipe. Although this delayed the Armada by twelve months, the public humiliation hardened Philip II’s determination to crush English naval piracy permanently.

Finally, the execution of Catholic Mary, Queen of Scots at Fotheringhay Castle in February 1587 eliminated any remaining hope of peaceful Catholic succession and turned Anglo-Spanish tension into a religious crusade. Before her death, Mary bequeathed her claim to the English throne not to her Protestant son James VI, but directly to King Philip II of Spain. Backed by Pope Sixtus V’s promise of one million gold ducats once Spanish soldiers landed in England, Philip II now possessed both dynastic and papal justification to launch the 130-ship Spanish Armada in May 1588.
