Explain why relations between England and Spain declined in the years 1568–1585. (12)
You may use the following in your answer:
  • Religious differences
  • Francis Drake’s attacks on Spanish treasure
You must also use information of your own.
Teacher Mark Scheme • Grade 9 Exemplar (12 / 12 Marks)

Deepening religious hostility steadily poisoned Anglo-Spanish relations after 1568. Devoutly Catholic Philip II resented Elizabeth’s 1559 Protestant settlement, and once Pope Pius V excommunicated Elizabeth in 1570, Spain backed Catholic conspiracies to depose her in favour of Mary, Queen of Scots, including the 1571 Ridolfi Plot and 1583 Throckmorton Plot. In retaliation, Elizabeth executed seminary priests and expelled Spanish ambassador Mendoza in 1584, severing formal diplomatic ties.

This ideological mistrust escalated through commercial rivalry and Francis Drake’s privateering raids on Spanish treasure. Relations fractured in 1568 when Spanish ships ambushed John Hawkins and Drake at San Juan de Ulúa and Elizabeth seized Philip’s Genoese Loan gold sheltering in English ports. During his 1577–80 circumnavigation, Drake plundered £140,000 from the Cacafuego; when Elizabeth publicly knighted him aboard the Golden Hind in 1581, Philip saw open royal piracy.

Finally, Spanish military dominance in the Netherlands turned rivalry into undeclared war by 1585. Because Antwerp controlled English cloth exports and Dutch ports offered invasion launchpads across the Channel, Elizabeth secretly funded Dutch Protestant rebels and hired mercenary armies after the 1576 Spanish Fury. Following the 1584 assassination of rebel leader William of Orange and the Catholic Franco-Spanish Treaty of Joinville, Elizabeth signed the Treaty of Nonsuch in August 1585, dispatching Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester, with 7,400 English soldiers to fight Spanish rule.

“The Northern Earls’ Rebellion (1569) was a serious threat to Elizabeth.”
How far do you agree? Explain your answer. (16)
You may use the following in your answer:
  • Mary, Queen of Scots
  • Lack of foreign support
You must also use information of your own.
Teacher Mark Scheme • Grade 9 Exemplar (16 / 16 Marks)

On the surface, the 1569 Revolt of the Northern Earls posed a dangerous dynastic and religious challenge because it united discontented Catholic peers behind a living Catholic heir. Resenting their loss of court influence under William Cecil, the Earls of Northumberland and Westmorland raised 4,600 men, stormed Durham Cathedral in November 1569, tore up the English Bible, and celebrated Latin Mass. Crucially, they planned to free Mary, Queen of Scots and marry her to the Duke of Norfolk, threatening Elizabeth with an immediate legitimate successor.

However, the rebellion failed to endanger Elizabeth’s throne because it lacked decisive foreign support and strategic coordination. Philip II was reluctant to place pro-French Mary on the English throne, so the Duke of Alba sent zero Spanish troops from the Netherlands despite seizing Hartlepool as a landing port. Moreover, Pope Pius V did not issue his Papal Bull excommunicating Elizabeth until February 1570, after the rising had already collapsed, leaving English Catholics uncertain whether rebellion was lawful.

Furthermore, overwhelming domestic loyalty and swift royal action kept the revolt isolated north of Teesdale. Most English Catholics, including southern peers and Lancashire gentry, refused to join, while Cecil moved Mary south to Coventry beyond rebel reach and the Earl of Sussex mustered a royal army that forced the earls to flee into Scotland. Executing roughly 450 rebels and placing Henry Hastings over a strengthened Council of the North crushed northern feudal autonomy.

Overall, I largely disagree with the statement. Although Mary’s presence made the rising ideologically alarming and foreshadowed future papal plots, without Spanish military intervention or wider English support, the poorly led northern rebels never seriously threatened to topple Elizabeth’s government.

“The voyages of exploration in Elizabeth’s reign were primarily motivated by a desire for wealth.”
How far do you agree? Explain your answer. (16)
You may use the following in your answer:
  • Trade routes
  • Rivalry with Spain
You must also use information of your own.
Teacher Mark Scheme • Grade 9 Exemplar (16 / 16 Marks)

The desire for commercial profit and plunder was clearly a central driver of Elizabethan exploration. Once conflict in the Netherlands disrupted the Antwerp woollen cloth market, English merchants urgently sought alternative overseas trade routes, founding the Eastland and Levant Companies and funding Martin Frobisher’s search for a North-West Passage to Asian spices. Privateering offered even richer returns: courtiers and Elizabeth herself invested in Francis Drake’s 1577–80 voyage, which brought back roughly £400,000 in captured Spanish silver and cloves, yielding enormous dividends that cleared royal debt.

Nevertheless, geopolitical and religious rivalry with Catholic Spain increasingly shaped maritime ventures alongside financial gain. Raiding Philip II’s New World treasure fleets directly starved Spanish armies fighting Protestants in the Netherlands, blending private profit with national defence. Similarly, Sir Walter Raleigh’s 1585 expedition to colonise Virginia aimed not only to source raw materials like timber and wine without Spanish tariffs, but also to establish a secure North American naval base from which English ships could strike Spanish Caribbean convoys.

In addition, imperial ambition and scientific progress enabled and encouraged ocean voyages. Navigational advances—including Mercator’s 1569 world map, astrolabes, quadrants, and faster, weatherly galleons—gave sailors confidence to cross oceans. Explorers also sought Protestant expansion and royal prestige, shown when Drake claimed Nova Albion in California for Elizabeth in June 1579 to challenge Iberian territorial monopolies.

Overall, I largely agree with the statement. Although strategic hostility towards Spain and imperial prestige grew vital by the 1580s, Elizabethan voyages depended entirely on private joint-stock investors who risked ships only because new trade routes and Spanish silver promised substantial wealth.
