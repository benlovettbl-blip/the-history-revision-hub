Teacher Mark Scheme • Grade 9 Exemplar (2 / 2 Marks)

One feature was maintaining local law and order as unpaid gentry magistrates. JPs held Quarter Sessions every three months to try crimes and enforce statutes.

(b) Describe one feature of the poor rate in Elizabethan England. (2)
Teacher Mark Scheme • Grade 9 Exemplar (2 / 2 Marks)

One feature was compulsory parish taxation introduced nationally under the 1572 Vagabonds Act for the deserving poor. JPs assessed residents and jailed anyone refusing payment.

2
Explain why Drake’s circumnavigation of the globe (1577–1580) was significant. (12)
You may use the following in your answer:
  • Spanish treasure
  • Nova Albion
You must also use information of your own.
Teacher Mark Scheme • Grade 9 Exemplar (12 / 12 Marks)

First, Drake’s circumnavigation was financially significant because captured Spanish treasure enriched the Crown and wealthy investors. Raiding undefended Pacific ports and seizing the silver galleon Cacafuego in 1579, Drake returned to Plymouth in September 1580 with roughly £400,000 in bullion and spices. Investors earned returns of 4,700%, while Elizabeth’s personal share enabled her to clear England’s entire royal debt and fund future naval defences.

Second, the voyage expanded England’s imperial and commercial horizons across North America and Asia. Landing on the Californian coast in June 1579, Drake welcomed local Miwok people and claimed the territory as ‘Nova Albion’ in Elizabeth’s name, directly challenging papal recognition of exclusive Spanish New World rule and inspiring Sir Walter Raleigh’s 1580s plans to colonise Virginia. Furthermore, Drake negotiated a valuable spice-trading treaty with the Sultan of Ternate in the Moluccas, encouraging wealthy London merchants to establish joint-stock trading ventures such as the Levant Company.

Finally, the circumnavigation severely damaged Anglo-Spanish diplomacy while boosting English maritime prestige. As only the second captain in history to circle the globe, Drake proved English race-built galleons and navigators could master distant oceans. Outraged by Pacific raids, Philip II demanded Drake’s execution as a pirate; instead, Elizabeth publicly knighted Drake aboard the Golden Hind at Deptford in April 1581, signalling open defiance that accelerated war.

3
“English naval tactics were the main reason for the defeat of the Spanish Armada.”
How far do you agree? Explain your answer. (16)
You may use the following in your answer:
  • Fireships
  • The Duke of Medina Sidonia
You must also use information of your own.
Teacher Mark Scheme • Grade 9 Exemplar (16 / 16 Marks)

English naval tactics and superior ship design played a decisive role in breaking the Armada’s fighting formation. Commanded by Lord Howard, Francis Drake, and John Hawkins, English commanders avoided Spanish grappling and boarding by firing long-range culverin broadsides from faster, weatherly race-built galleons. Crucially, on the night of 6–7 August 1588, sending eight burning fireships into Calais roads panicked anchored Spanish captains into cutting their cables, shattering their defensive crescent and exposing scattered galleons to devastating close-range English gunfire at Gravelines.

However, English tactics succeeded largely because Spanish strategic planning and leadership were deeply flawed. Appointed by Philip II despite lacking naval experience, the Duke of Medina Sidonia had to execute a rigid rendezvous plan linking his 130 ships with the Duke of Parma’s 17,000 soldiers in the Netherlands without controlling a single deep-water port. Delayed communications, Dutch Sea Beggar blockades trapping Parma’s barges, mismatched cannonballs, and rotting supplies caused by Drake’s 1587 Raid on Cadiz crippled Spanish operational effectiveness before Gravelines began.

Furthermore, severe Atlantic storms turned tactical setback into catastrophic destruction. Unable to return south through the English Channel against prevailing winds and lacking cut anchors, the battered Armada was driven north around Scotland and Ireland, where gales wrecked dozens of unmapped Spanish vessels along rocky coasts, leaving barely half the fleet to limp home.

Overall, I largely agree with the statement. While Philip II’s flawed rendezvous plan weakened the expedition and storms destroyed most ships, English fireships at Calais and broadside gunnery at Gravelines were the decisive causes that prevented Parma’s army from crossing the Channel.

4
“The threat from Catholics at home was more serious than the threat from Spain in the years 1569–1588.”
How far do you agree? Explain your answer. (16)
You may use the following in your answer:
  • Jesuit priests
  • The Netherlands
You must also use information of your own.
Teacher Mark Scheme • Grade 9 Exemplar (16 / 16 Marks)

Domestic Catholic opposition posed an immediate personal danger to Elizabeth because conspirators sought her assassination to crown Mary, Queen of Scots. Following the 1569 Northern Earls’ Rebellion and the 1570 Papal Bull of Excommunication, missionary seminary priests from Douai and Jesuit priests such as Edmund Campion and Robert Parsons entered England from 1580 to sustain gentry recusancy. Simultaneously, the Ridolfi (1571), Throckmorton (1583), and Babington (1586) plots threatened regicide from within, forcing harsh £20 monthly recusancy fines and treason laws.

Nevertheless, domestic Catholic plots proved consistently weak on their own. Most ordinary English Catholics remained loyal to Elizabeth—even in 1569 and 1588—while Sir Francis Walsingham’s spy network and codebreakers repeatedly intercepted conspirators before any rising began. Crucially, every major plot after 1570 depended entirely on foreign Catholic troops and funding to stand any realistic chance of overthrowing the Tudor state.

By contrast, Spain represented Europe’s wealthiest military superpower, backed by New World silver and veteran armies stationed right across the Channel in the Netherlands under Alba and Parma. Once Philip II annexed Portugal in 1580 and signed the 1584 Treaty of Joinville with French Catholics, Spanish expansion threatened English trade and national survival directly, culminating in open war after the 1585 Treaty of Nonsuch and the 130-ship Spanish Armada of 1588.

Overall, I firmly disagree with the statement. Although homegrown plotters and Jesuit missionaries created persistent internal anxiety around Elizabeth’s personal safety, domestic English Catholics lacked arms and popular backing; only Philip II’s veteran Spanish armies in the Netherlands and the 1588 Armada possessed the military strength to conquer Protestant England.
