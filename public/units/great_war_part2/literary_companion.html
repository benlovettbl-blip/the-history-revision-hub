Enquiry 1 Classic Literature & Verse Frontline Anti-War Poem (1917 / 1920)

"Dulce et Decorum Est": Answering the Recruitment Lie (1917)

Wilfred Owen (written October 1917 · pub. 1920)
Historical Context: Second Lieutenant Wilfred Owen served in the Manchester Regiment and won the Military Cross before being killed just one week before the Armistice in November 1918. Outraged by civilian poet Jessie Pope’s jaunty verses urging teenagers to enlist, Owen wrote "Dulce et Decorum Est" in 1917 to shatter "the old Lie."
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Dulce et decorum est[DOOL-chay et day-KOR-um est]
Latin line from Roman poet Horace—"It is sweet and honourable to die for one’s country"—branded "the old Lie" by Owen.
Blood-shod[BLUD-shod]
Owen’s vivid compound word for exhausted soldiers limping barefoot with boots lost and feet wrapped in blood.
Floundering[FLOWN-dur-ing]
Thrashing and stumbling helplessly—describing a soldier choking inside green chlorine/phosgene gas without his mask.
Ardent[AR-dunt]
Burning with eager enthusiasm—describing naive teenage volunteers hungry "for some desperate glory" in 1914.

In 1914 and 1915, civilian writers like Jessie Pope urged teenagers to rush to the colours as if modern war were a lively rugby match. Haunted by watching a gas-poisoned soldier flung into a jolting wagon in France, frontline officer Wilfred Owen wrote this blistering reply in October 1917.

Bent double, like old beggars under sacks,
Knock-kneed, coughing like hags, we cursed through sludge,
Till on the haunting flares we turned our backs,
And towards our distant rest began to trudge.
Men marched asleep. Many had lost their boots,
But limped on, blood-shod. All went lame; all blind;
Drunk with fatigue; deaf even to the hoots
Of gas-shells dropping softly behind.
Gas! GAS! Quick, boys!—An ecstasy of fumbling,
Fitting the clumsy helmets just in time,
But someone still was yelling out and stumbling
And flound’ring like a man in fire or lime.
Dim through the misty panes and thick green light,
As under a green sea, I saw him drowning.
In all my dreams before my helpless sight,
He plunges at me, guttering, choking, drowning.
My friend, you would not tell with such high zest
To children ardent for some desperate glory,
The old Lie: Dulce et decorum est
Pro patria mori.

Killed in action just one week before the November 1918 Armistice, Owen left behind an unforgettable warning against romanticising industrial slaughter.

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 Look at Owen’s opening lines: how does he smash the cheerful idea of civilian writers like Jessie Pope who treated war like a lively rugby match?
2 How does the pace and mood of the poem suddenly change at "Gas! GAS! Quick, boys!"?
3 In the final verse, Owen calls the eager recruits "children" and brands the famous Latin motto "the old Lie." Why does he feel so strongly that writers at home should stop telling young volunteers that dying in war is sweet and honourable?

Enquiry 2 True Historical Story 1916–1918

7:30 a.m. on 1 July 1916: From Somme Disaster to the Creeping Barrage at Amiens

The History Revision Hub · Historical Story (1916–1918)
Historical Context: Few debates divide Great War historians more fiercely than General Sir Douglas Haig and the Somme. Following the war from the whistles of 1 July 1916 to the dawn breakthrough at Amiens in August 1918 shows both catastrophic human loss and rapid industrial learning.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
No Man’s Land[noh manz land]
The cratered, wire-tangled strip of ground between opposing British and German frontline trenches.
Chalk Dugouts[CHAWK DUG-owts]
Deep German shelters carved thirty feet into Somme chalk, shielding machine-gun crews during British shelling.
Creeping Barrage[KREE-ping buh-RAZH]
A timed curtain of artillery shells moving forward just ahead of advancing infantry to keep enemy gunners pinned down.
Combined Arms[kum-BYND armz]
Coordinating artillery, tanks, aircraft, and small infantry squads together—mastered at Amiens in August 1918.

At 7:28 a.m. on 1 July 1916, giant underground mines erupted beneath the white chalk ridges of the Somme. Two minutes later, officers’ brass whistles shrilled along eighteen miles of British trenches. Carrying heavy sixty-pound packs, volunteer Pals Battalions—clerks, dockworkers, and neighbours from Portsmouth, Leeds, and Accrington—climbed their ladders into bright summer sunshine. In the 8th East Surreys, twenty-one-year-old Captain Billie Nevill even kicked leather footballs into No Man’s Land to steady his nervous men.

General Sir Douglas Haig had trusted a seven-day, 1.5-million-shell bombardment to smash the German defences. Yet nearly a third of the shells were duds, and light shrapnel failed to slice thick German barbed wire. Thirty feet underground in reinforced chalk dugouts, German soldiers waited out the thunder. The instant British guns lifted at 7:30 a.m., German crews hauled heavy MG08 machine guns into daylight and swept every gap in the wire. By nightfall, 57,470 British troops were casualties—including 19,240 killed—making 1 July 1916 the bloodiest day in British military history.

Yet the Western Front was not frozen in stupidity forever. Over the next two years, British and Dominion armies overhauled every tactic. At 4:20 a.m. on 8 August 1918 outside Amiens, no week-long bombardment warned the enemy. Instead, sound-ranging pinpointed German batteries, aircraft buzzed overhead, hundreds of tanks crushed the wire, and infantry advanced yards behind a timed "creeping barrage"—shattering German lines at last.

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 Why did the massive seven-day British artillery bombardment fail to protect the Pals Battalions on 1 July 1916?
2 Why did twenty-one-year-old Captain Billie Nevill kick leather footballs into No Man’s Land at 7:30 a.m., and what happened the instant British guns lifted?
3 Compare the bloodiest day of the Somme (1 July 1916) with the dawn attack at Amiens two years later (8 August 1918). What had British and Dominion armies learned to do differently?

Enquiry 3 Eyewitness Voice Censored Indian Soldiers’ Letters (1915)

Letters from Flanders & Brighton’s Royal Pavilion Hospital (1915)

Havildar Abdul Rahman & Wounded Sepoys (India Office Censor Files, 1915)
Historical Context: Over 1.3 million Indian soldiers served in the First World War, rushing into Flanders trenches in autumn 1914 when Britain’s small regular army was near collapse. Because British military censors translated outgoing mail, hundreds of extraordinary letters from France and Brighton’s Royal Pavilion Hospital survive today.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Sepoy[SEE-poy]
An Indian infantry private serving in the British Indian Army.
Havildar[HAV-il-dar]
An Indian non-commissioned officer rank equivalent to a Sergeant.
Royal Pavilion[ROY-ul puh-VIL-yun]
The domed former royal palace in Brighton converted in 1914–1915 into a military hospital for wounded Indian soldiers.
Military Censor[MIL-ih-tuh-ree SEN-sur]
Army officials who opened, translated, and copied soldiers’ letters to check morale and block military secrets.

In autumn 1914, Indian regiments held flooded Flanders trenches before Kitchener’s volunteers were ready. Writing from France in May 1915, Punjabi sergeant Havildar Abdul Rahman of the 59th Rifles begged his family in India to stay away. British censors promptly seized his letter.

"For God’s sake don’t come, don’t come, don’t come to this war in Europe! Tell my brother Muhammad Yakub Khan for God’s sake not to enlist. Cannons, machine guns, rifles, and bombs are going day and night, just like the rains in the monsoon season. Those who have escaped so far are like the few grains left uncooked in a pot. In my company only ten men remain."

Thousands of wounded sepoys were carried across the Channel to Brighton, where the domed Royal Pavilion became an Indian military hospital with separate Hindu and Muslim kitchens. In 1915, a wounded Garhwali officer wrote home with both gratitude and sharp awareness of imperial inequality.

"Do not think this is war; it is the destruction of the world. Yet in this hospital at Brighton, the care given to the wounded is beyond praise. The King-Emperor himself visited our beds and spoke kindly to us. Even so, we recover behind high iron gates under guard. Our Indian officers may never command white soldiers, and when our wounds heal we return to an India that is still not free."

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 What two vivid everyday comparisons does Havildar Abdul Rahman use in May 1915 to describe trench warfare to his family in India?
2 Why did British censors seize Abdul Rahman’s letter—and what striking contrast did the wounded Garhwali officer notice at Brighton’s Royal Pavilion Hospital?
3 Read the Garhwali officer’s final line: "when our wounds heal we return to an India that is still not free." Why did fighting in Flanders make imperial inequality feel so painful to Indian soldiers?

Enquiry 4 Eyewitness Voice Eyewitness VAD Nursing Diary & Home Front

Chronicle of Youth: VAD Wards & Canary Girls in Total War (1915–1918)

Vera Brittain (Wartime Diary & Memoir, 1915–1918)
Historical Context: Twenty-one-year-old Vera Brittain left Oxford University in 1915 to nurse shattered soldiers as a Voluntary Aid Detachment (VAD) volunteer in London, Malta, and Étaples in France. Losing her fiancé Roland, her brother Edward, and two closest friends, she recorded how total war transformed women’s lives.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
VAD Nurse[vee-ay-DEE nurs]
A civilian Voluntary Aid Detachment volunteer—like Vera Brittain—who nursed wounded troops in wartime hospitals.
Étaples[ay-TAHPL]
The huge British military base and hospital camp on the French coast where Vera Brittain nursed gas casualties in 1917–1918.
Canary Girls[kuh-NAIR-ee gurlz]
Nearly one million female munitions workers whose skin and hair turned bright yellow from toxic TNT explosive powder.
DORA (1914)[DOR-uh]
The Defence of the Realm Act giving the wartime government sweeping control over factories, newspapers, and food rationing.

Leaving her quiet Oxford college in 1915, volunteer nurse Vera Brittain stepped straight into the surgical wards of London and Étaples in France.

"Day and night, ambulance trains unloaded an endless river of broken youth. Stretcher-bearers carried in eighteen-year-old boys with shattered legs, infected shrapnel wounds, and eyes bandaged blind from mustard gas. Their scorched lungs fought for every breath. Most of us were sheltered daughters who had never cleaned a wound before. Within a month, our hands were chapped raw with antiseptic as we dressed amputations and wrote last letters for dying boys.

"Back home across Britain, total war under the Defence of the Realm Act pulled nearly a million women into munitions factories. Inside shell-filling sheds from Chilwell to Portsmouth’s Priddy’s Hard, young working-class ’Canary Girls’ packed yellow TNT powder into high-explosive shells. Toxic dust dyed their skin bright canary yellow and rotted their teeth, while a single spark could blow a shed apart. Women drove city trams, worked railway signals, and ploughed fields to defeat the German U-boat blockade.

"When the Armistice bells finally rang out on 11 November 1918, I stood in a silent street unable to cheer. My fiancé and my brother both lay under wooden crosses abroad. Within months, thousands of factory women were dismissed so returning men could reclaim their jobs."

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 How did wartime VAD nursing change sheltered young women like Vera Brittain within a single month?
2 Why were female shell-filling workers nicknamed "Canary Girls," and what dangers did they face inside munitions sheds?
3 On 11 November 1918, Vera stood in a silent street unable to cheer, and within months thousands of factory women were dismissed so returning men could reclaim their jobs. After everything women did in wards, factories, trams, and fields, does that swift dismissal feel fair?

Enquiry 5 Eyewitness Voice Eyewitness Versailles Critique (1919)

Walking Out of Versailles: John Maynard Keynes’s Warning on the Peace (1919)

John Maynard Keynes ("The Economic Consequences of the Peace", 1919)
Historical Context: Thirty-six-year-old British Treasury economist John Maynard Keynes sat inside the 1919 Paris Peace Conference watching Allied leaders draft the Treaty of Versailles. Appalled by terms he believed would ruin Europe’s economy, Keynes resigned in June 1919 and published a prophetic bestseller before the year ended.
Listen Aloud
Scan Camera
UK Narrator
Step 1 · Key Words to Sound Out Before Reading Practise [pronunciation] helpers aloud together
Georges Clemenceau[ZHORZH klem-ahn-SOH]
Fierce French Prime Minister ("The Tiger") determined to weaken Germany permanently so France could never be invaded again.
Article 231[AR-tik-ul too-three-WUN]
The Versailles "War Guilt Clause" forcing Germany to accept legal blame for all Allied war losses and damage.
Reparations[rep-uh-RAY-shunz]
Heavy financial compensation (£6.6 billion set in 1921) demanded from defeated Germany to pay for war destruction.
Carthaginian Peace[kar-thuh-JIN-ee-un pees]
Keynes’s warning phrase for a crushing peace treaty designed to ruin a defeated rival rather than rebuild stability.

Inside Paris in the spring of 1919, British Treasury adviser John Maynard Keynes watched the "Big Three" argue over a ruined continent. French leader Georges Clemenceau sat in grey suede gloves with one fixed goal. He wanted Germany crippled forever so French towns could never be invaded again. British Prime Minister David Lloyd George feared angry voters at home who cried to "squeeze the German lemon until the pips squeak." Meanwhile, American President Woodrow Wilson preached noble Fourteen Points yet gave ground clause by clause behind closed doors.

In June 1919, appalled by the final treaty, Keynes quit the British team and walked out of Paris. Before the year ended, he published this stark warning.

"Germany was forced to sign Article 231—accepting sole guilt for the war—under the threat of invasion and a starvation sea blockade. At the same time, the treaty stripped Germany of her merchant ships, overseas trade, iron ore, and rich coalfields. Yet the victors still demanded billions of pounds in gold that a broken country now had no way to earn.

"Demanding the full cost of the war from Germany was one of the gravest acts of folly in modern history. If we aim on purpose to beggar Central Europe, vengeance, I dare predict, will not limp. Nothing will then hold back a final struggle of despair and hate, beside which even the horrors of the late war will fade."

Step 3 · Talk Together After Reading Talking Points & Answers: Page 8
1 Why did Clemenceau, Lloyd George, and Woodrow Wilson pull in three different directions in Paris in spring 1919?
2 What economic contradiction drove John Maynard Keynes to resign and walk out of Paris in June 1919?
3 Put yourself in both Clemenceau’s shoes and Keynes’s shoes in 1919. After a devastating war, should a peace treaty punish the defeated side harshly so it cannot attack again, or help Europe rebuild so "vengeance" does not grow?
