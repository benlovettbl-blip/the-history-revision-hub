Détente in the 1970s — SALT 1, the Helsinki Accords & SALT 2, 1970–79
Enquiry Question (Page 2): How genuinely did Détente ease superpower rivalry between 1970 and 1979?
Page 3 • Question 1 [4 Marks] Level 1: 1–2m | Level 2: 3–4m (AO1 + AO2)
Explain one consequence of the Helsinki Accords (1975). [4 marks]
Option A (4/4): Basket 3 & Dissident Monitoring:

One major consequence of the August 1975 Helsinki Accords was the growth of organised human-rights monitoring that publicly embarrassed Moscow. Because Basket 3 committed all 35 signatory states to uphold freedom of speech, religion, and movement, courageous activists formed Helsinki Watch inside the USSR and Charter 77 in Czechoslovakia, exposing Soviet arrests and straining relations under President Jimmy Carter.

Option B (4/4): Basket 1 & Recognition of Borders:

One significant consequence of the August 1975 Helsinki Accords was formal Western recognition of Moscow’s post-1945 territorial sphere in Eastern Europe. Under Basket 1, US President Gerald Ford, Leonid Brezhnev, and 33 other leaders declared post-war European frontiers inviolable and officially recognised Communist East Germany, temporarily easing long-standing Soviet security fears over a divided continent.

Page 3 • Question 2 • Analytical Narrative Account (12 mins) 8 Marks
Write a narrative account analysing the key events of détente in the 1970s. [8 marks]
Stimulus bullets printed: • SALT 1 (1972) • SALT 2 (1979)
Edexcel Paper 2 Q2 Narrative Tariff (8 Marks — AO1: 4, AO2: 4): Level 1 (1–2m): Simple statements • Level 2 (3–5m): Chronological description with implicit links (capped at 4/8 without own knowledge beyond stimulus) • Level 3 (6–8m): Organised beginning–middle–outcome sequence with explicit causal links & own knowledge beyond the two stimulus points.
Grade 9 Model Answer (8 / 8 Marks):

Drained by the $150 billion Vietnam War while Leonid Brezhnev needed Western grain and feared isolation after Richard Nixon visited China in February 1972, both superpowers turned toward détente. This directly produced the May 1972 Moscow Summit, where Nixon and Brezhnev signed SALT 1—restricting each nation to two ABM sites and freezing ICBM and SLBM launchers for five years, though leaving multi-warhead MIRVs uncapped.

Building on SALT 1, expanding American grain exports and follow-up summits culminated in the televised July 1975 Apollo–Soyuz orbital handshake and the August 1975 Helsinki Accords signed by Gerald Ford, Brezhnev, and 33 other leaders. While Basket 1 recognised post-war borders and East Germany, Basket 3’s human-rights pledges unexpectedly inspired Helsinki Watch and Charter 77 dissidents to expose Soviet repression.

Consequently, superpower trust frayed from 1977 when President Jimmy Carter championed dissidents like Andrei Sakharov while US Senate hawks condemned Soviet SS-20 missiles and interventions in Angola and Ethiopia. Although Carter and Brezhnev signed SALT 2 in Vienna in June 1979 to cap strategic launchers at 2,400 and limit MIRVs, Senate opposition stalled ratification until the December 1979 Soviet invasion of Afghanistan led Carter to withdraw SALT 2 in January 1980, ending détente.

Soviet Invasion of Afghanistan, Olympic Boycotts & Reagan’s ‘Second Cold War’, 1979–85
Enquiry Question (Page 4): Why did superpower relations plunge from Détente into a dangerous ‘Second Cold War’ between 1979 and 1985?
Page 5 • Question 1 [4 Marks] Level 1: 1–2m | Level 2: 3–4m (AO1 + AO2)
Explain one consequence of the Carter Doctrine (1980). [4 marks]
Option A (4/4): Militarised Gulf Containment:

One major consequence of the Carter Doctrine in January 1980 was the militarisation of American containment across the Persian Gulf, ending 1970s détente. Pledging US military force to block Soviet expansion beyond Afghanistan toward Middle Eastern oil supplies, President Jimmy Carter created a Rapid Deployment Force and raised defence spending by 5 per cent, reviving open superpower confrontation.

Option B (4/4): Sanctions & Covert Proxy Aid:

One immediate consequence of the January 1980 Carter Doctrine was sweeping economic and covert retaliation against the Soviet Union. Backing his warning over the Persian Gulf with action, Jimmy Carter withdrew SALT 2 from the Senate, banned US grain and computer exports to Moscow, and armed Afghan Mujahideen fighters through Pakistan under CIA Operation Cyclone.

Page 5 • Question 3 • Explain Importance (12 mins) 8 Marks
Explain the importance of the Strategic Defence Initiative (SDI) for US-Soviet relations. [8 marks]
Edexcel Paper 2 Q3 Importance Tariff (8 Marks — AO1: 4, AO2: 4): Level 1 (1–2m): Generalised assertions • Level 2 (3–5m): Descriptive account with limited link to importance • Level 3 (6–8m): Two distinct, factually supported analytical paragraphs directly explaining why X mattered for Y.
Grade 9 Model Answer (8 / 8 Marks):

First, Ronald Reagan’s March 1983 Strategic Defence Initiative (SDI) was strategically important for US-Soviet relations because it threatened to overturn Mutually Assured Destruction (MAD) and deepened distrust during the Second Cold War. By proposing orbiting space satellites armed with lasers to intercept Soviet ICBMs in flight before hitting America, SDI bypassed the spirit of the 1972 ABM Treaty and threatened to render Soviet nuclear missiles obsolete. Consequently, elderly Kremlin leaders Yuri Andropov and Konstantin Chernenko feared Washington was seeking a nuclear first-strike shield, intensifying superpower hostility between 1983 and 1985.

Second, SDI was decisively important because it exposed the severe economic and technological weakness of the USSR, shifting Cold War leverage toward Washington. Already drained by fighting Mujahideen guerrillas in Afghanistan and starved of Western technology since 1980, the stagnant Soviet command economy lagged far behind America in microchips and supercomputers. Realising that matching "Star Wars" would bankrupt the Soviet state ultimately compelled Mikhail Gorbachev from 1985 to seek summit diplomacy and deep arms reductions.

Gorbachev’s ‘New Thinking’ & The Superpower Summits, 1985–89
Enquiry Question (Page 6): How did Mikhail Gorbachev’s ‘New Thinking’ and superpower summit diplomacy transform US–Soviet relations between 1985 and 1989?
Page 7 • Question 1 [4 Marks] Level 1: 1–2m | Level 2: 3–4m (AO1 + AO2)
Explain one consequence of Gorbachev’s ‘new thinking’ for US-Soviet relations (1985–89). [4 marks]
Option A (4/4): Nuclear Disarmament (INF Treaty):

One major consequence of Gorbachev’s ‘new thinking’ was a historic nuclear disarmament breakthrough between Washington and Moscow. Needing to slash military spending consuming 25% of Soviet GDP to fund domestic perestroika, Gorbachev uncoupled talks from SDI and signed the December 1987 INF Treaty with Ronald Reagan, destroying over 2,600 intermediate-range missiles under mutual on-site inspections.

Option B (4/4): Ending Military Intervention Abroad:

One decisive consequence of Gorbachev’s ‘new thinking’ was the end of Soviet military interventions abroad, transforming American trust. By announcing unilateral cuts of 500,000 Red Army troops and abandoning the Brezhnev Doctrine at the UN in December 1988 before completing withdrawal from Afghanistan in February 1989, Gorbachev enabled President Bush to declare the Cold War over at Malta.

Page 7 • Question 2 • Analytical Narrative Account (12 mins) 8 Marks
Write a narrative account analysing the key events in how Reagan and Gorbachev reduced superpower tensions in the years 1985–89. [8 marks]
Stimulus bullets printed: • Gorbachev’s ‘new thinking’ • Reagan and the Strategic Defence Initiative (SDI)
Edexcel Paper 2 Q2 Narrative Tariff (8 Marks — AO1: 4, AO2: 4): Level 1 (1–2m): Simple statements • Level 2 (3–5m): Chronological description with implicit links (capped at 4/8 without own knowledge beyond stimulus) • Level 3 (6–8m): Organised beginning–middle–outcome sequence with explicit causal links & own knowledge beyond the two stimulus points.
Grade 9 Model Answer (8 / 8 Marks):

Inheriting economic stagnation, the $8 billion-a-year Afghan war, and military costs absorbing 25% of Soviet GDP in March 1985, Mikhail Gorbachev launched ‘new thinking’—perestroika and glasnost—requiring urgent arms reductions. This directly led to the November 1985 Geneva Summit, where Ronald Reagan and Gorbachev built warm fireside trust and signed a joint statement declaring that "a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought".

At the October 1986 Reykjavik Summit, Gorbachev proposed sweeping ballistic missile abolition provided Reagan confined SDI to the laboratory, causing talks to collapse when Reagan refused to scrap SDI. However, once Soviet scientists advised in February 1987 that SDI was unproven and cheaper to counter than match, Gorbachev uncoupled disarmament from SDI—producing the December 1987 Washington Summit and the INF Treaty eliminating over 2,600 missiles (500–5,500 km range) under on-site inspections.

Consequently, reconciliation accelerated across 1988–89. After Reagan walked Red Square at the May 1988 Moscow Summit and dropped his "Evil Empire" label, Gorbachev used his December 1988 UN speech to cut 500,000 troops and abandon the Brezhnev Doctrine, withdrew all Soviet forces from Afghanistan in February 1989, and joined President George H. W. Bush at Malta in December 1989 to declare the Cold War over.

The Collapse of Eastern Europe, Fall of the Berlin Wall & End of the USSR, 1989–91
Enquiry Question (Page 10): Why did abandoning the Brezhnev Doctrine topple the Berlin Wall in 1989 and bring about the collapse of the Soviet Union itself by December 1991?
Page 11 • Question 1 [4 Marks] Level 1: 1–2m | Level 2: 3–4m (AO1 + AO2)
Explain one consequence of the fall of the Berlin Wall (1989). [4 marks]
Option A (4/4): German Reunification (Oct 1990):

One major consequence of the fall of the Berlin Wall on 9 November 1989 was the rapid collapse of Communist East Germany and formal German reunification. Once checkpoints opened freely and Mikhail Gorbachev kept Soviet troops in their barracks, West German Chancellor Helmut Kohl secured Soviet consent for East and West Germany to unite inside NATO on 3 October 1990.

Option B (4/4): Eastern Bloc Domino Revolutions:

One decisive consequence of the fall of the Berlin Wall in November 1989 was a rapid domino collapse of remaining Communist dictatorships across Eastern Europe. Seeing Berlin’s concrete divide fall without Red Army intervention directly emboldened Czechoslovakia’s peaceful Velvet Revolution under Václav Havel and Romania’s December uprising that overthrew Nicolae Ceaușescu before the end of 1989.

Page 11 • Question 3 • Explain Importance (12 mins) 8 Marks
Explain the importance of Gorbachev’s ‘new thinking’ for the collapse of Soviet control of Eastern Europe. [8 marks]
Edexcel Paper 2 Q3 Importance Tariff (8 Marks — AO1: 4, AO2: 4): Level 1 (1–2m): Generalised assertions • Level 2 (3–5m): Descriptive account with limited link to importance • Level 3 (6–8m): Two distinct, factually supported analytical paragraphs directly explaining why X mattered for Y.
Grade 9 Model Answer (8 / 8 Marks):

First, Gorbachev’s ‘new thinking’ was decisively important for the collapse of Soviet control of Eastern Europe because abandoning the 1968 Brezhnev Doctrine removed the Red Army threat that had propped up unpopular Communist regimes since 1945. Confirmed in his December 1988 UN speech and dubbed the "Sinatra Doctrine" in October 1989, Soviet non-intervention meant satellite dictatorships could no longer rely on Moscow’s tanks. Consequently, Solidarity won Poland’s June 1989 elections under Tadeusz Mazowiecki, Hungary dismantled its Austrian border fence, and unarmed crowds toppled the Berlin Wall on 9 November 1989 alongside Czechoslovakia’s Velvet Revolution.

Second, ‘new thinking’ was crucial because Gorbachev’s commitment to glasnost and Western economic cooperation dismantled the military alliance binding the Eastern Bloc to Moscow. Encouraging political openness emboldened opposition movements across satellite states, while Gorbachev’s need for Western loans persuaded him to accept formal German reunification inside NATO on 3 October 1990. Once newly elected democracies in Poland, Hungary, and Czechoslovakia demanded Soviet troop withdrawals, the Warsaw Pact formally dissolved on 1 July 1991.

Explain one consequence of SALT 1 (1972). [4 marks]
Grade 9 Model Answer (4 / 4 Marks):

One consequence of SALT 1 in May 1972 was the first formal superpower cap on strategic nuclear launchers alongside major symbolic momentum for détente. By restricting both the USA and USSR to two Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) sites each and freezing ICBM and submarine launcher numbers for five years, Nixon and Brezhnev slowed the strategic arms race and paved the way for reciprocal Moscow–Washington summits (though MIRV warheads remained unrestricted).

Page 13 • Consequence Drill 2 [4 Marks] 4 Marks
Explain one consequence of the Olympic boycotts (1980 and 1984). [4 marks]
Grade 9 Model Answer (4 / 4 Marks):

One consequence of the 1980 Moscow and 1984 Los Angeles Olympic boycotts was the public politicisation of global sport that symbolised the deep freeze of the "Second Cold War". After the USA led over 60 nations in boycotting the 1980 Moscow Games over the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan—denying Moscow a global propaganda showcase—the USSR led 14 Eastern Bloc states in a retaliatory boycott of the 1984 Los Angeles Olympics, underscoring collapsed superpower diplomacy.

1  Explain two consequences of the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (1979). [8 marks]
Pearson Edexcel Paper 2 Q1 Marking Rule: Mark Consequence 1 (0–4 marks) and Consequence 2 (0–4 marks) separately. Level 1 (1–2m): Simple/general statement • Level 2 (3–4m): Specific factual detail + explained causal outcome.
Consequence 1 — Grade 9 Model Answer (4 / 4 Marks):

One consequence of the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979 was the immediate collapse of 1970s détente and severe American diplomatic and economic retaliation. Outraged by Soviet intervention to install Babrak Karmal, President Jimmy Carter withdrew SALT 2 from the US Senate, proclaimed the January 1980 Carter Doctrine pledging military force to defend the Persian Gulf, imposed a US grain embargo, and led over 60 countries in boycotting the 1980 Moscow Olympics.

Consequence 2 — Grade 9 Model Answer (4 / 4 Marks):

A second consequence was a costly decade-long guerrilla war ("the USSR’s Vietnam") that drained the stagnant Soviet economy and weakened Red Army morale. Backed by CIA funds and Stinger anti-aircraft missiles under Operation Cyclone, Islamic Mujahideen fighters inflicted ~15,000 Soviet deaths and cost Moscow billions of roubles a year—convincing Mikhail Gorbachev that the USSR had to withdraw by 1989 and cut Cold War arms spending.

Page 15 • Question 2 • Analytical Narrative Account [8 Marks] (Unscaffolded) 8 Marks (AO1: 4 + AO2: 4)
2  Write a narrative account analysing the key events in the collapse of Soviet control of Eastern Europe and the collapse of the Soviet Union in the years 1989–91. [8 marks]
Stimulus bullets printed: • the fall of the Berlin Wall (1989) • the end of the Warsaw Pact (1991)
Level 3 (6–8 Marks): Organised beginning–developments–outcome chronological sequence linked by explicit causal connectives, integrating accurate own knowledge beyond the two stimulus bullets.
Grade 9 Model Answer (8 / 8 Marks):

Once Mikhail Gorbachev’s "new thinking" repudiated the Brezhnev Doctrine in 1988–89—making clear under the "Sinatra Doctrine" that Soviet tanks would no longer prop up unpopular Eastern European regimes—peaceful revolutions swept the bloc. After Solidarity won Polish elections in June 1989 and Hungary opened its border with Austria, mass protests in Leipzig forced Erich Honecker out, culminating on 9 November 1989 when Günter Schabowski’s bungled broadcast led crowds to open the Berlin Wall.

The fall of the Berlin Wall triggered a rapid chain reaction that ended Soviet control across Eastern Europe. Communist regimes collapsed in Czechoslovakia’s Velvet Revolution and Romania before the end of 1989, East and West Germany formally reunified inside NATO in October 1990, and newly democratic governments demanded Red Army withdrawal—bringing about the formal end of the Warsaw Pact on 1 July 1991.

Watching Eastern Europe break free inspired independence movements inside the USSR itself, led by Lithuania in March 1990 as Glasnost and Perestroika weakened central authority. When eight Communist hardliners ("the Gang of Eight") staged an August 1991 coup in Moscow to block Gorbachev’s New Union Treaty, Russian President Boris Yeltsin rallied crowds to defeat them; Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus formed the Commonwealth of Independent States in December 1991, forcing Gorbachev’s resignation on 25 December 1991 and ending the Soviet Union.
