The Berlin Crisis, Summit Meetings & Construction of the Berlin Wall, 1958–63
Enquiry Question (Page 2): Why did Khrushchev replace his 1958 Berlin Ultimatum with a concrete wall in August 1961 — and how did it reshape superpower relations?
Page 3 • Question 1 [4 Marks] Level 1: 1–2m | Level 2: 3–4m (AO1 + AO2)
Explain one consequence of the summit meetings of 1959–61. [4 marks]
Option A (4/4): Vienna Deadlock & Construction of the Berlin Wall:

One consequence of the summit meetings of 1959–61 was the final breakdown of negotiations over Berlin that led directly to the construction of the Berlin Wall. When Khrushchev reissued his six-month ultimatum at the June 1961 Vienna Summit and President Kennedy responded by adding $3.2 billion to US defence spending, Khrushchev abandoned diplomacy and sealed East Berlin’s border on 13 August 1961.

Option B (4/4): Collapse of Camp David Goodwill at Paris:

One consequence of the 1959–61 summit meetings was the destruction of the diplomatic goodwill briefly achieved at Camp David in September 1959. After Soviet missiles shot down Francis Gary Powers’s U-2 spy plane on 1 May 1960 and Eisenhower refused to apologise, Khrushchev stormed out of the Paris Summit, ending any prospect of a negotiated Berlin settlement under Eisenhower.

Page 3 • Question 2 • Analytical Narrative Account (12 mins) 8 Marks
Write a narrative account analysing the key events in Berlin in the years 1958–61. [8 marks]
Stimulus bullets printed: • Khrushchev’s Berlin ultimatum (1958) • the construction of the Berlin Wall (1961)
Edexcel Paper 2 Q2 Narrative Tariff (8 Marks — AO1: 4, AO2: 4): Level 1 (1–2m): Simple statements • Level 2 (3–5m): Chronological description with implicit links (capped at 4/8 without own knowledge beyond stimulus) • Level 3 (6–8m): Organised beginning–middle–outcome sequence with explicit causal links & own knowledge beyond the two stimulus points.
Grade 9 Model Answer (8 / 8 Marks):

Between 1949 and 1958, nearly three million East Germans—including vital doctors, engineers, and teachers—fled low wages and Stasi repression under Walter Ulbricht through West Berlin’s open border. Determined to halt this crippling "brain drain", Nikita Khrushchev issued his Berlin ultimatum on 27 November 1958, demanding Western troops leave West Berlin within six months.

Because neither superpower risked nuclear war, the ultimatum triggered a series of summit meetings between 1959 and 1961. Although Khrushchev withdrew his deadline after meeting Eisenhower at Camp David (September 1959), Soviet downing of Gary Powers’s U-2 spy plane wrecked the May 1960 Paris Summit. At the June 1961 Vienna Summit, Khrushchev renewed his six-month ultimatum to test young President John F. Kennedy; however, Kennedy refused to yield, calling up 250,000 reservists and adding $3.2 billion to US defence spending, which sparked a surge of over 30,000 East German refugees in July 1961 alone.

Realising Kennedy would fight to defend West Berlin—yet would not invade East Berlin—Khrushchev backed Ulbricht’s request to seal the border. Before dawn on 13 August 1961, East German troops erected barbed wire and then a 3.6-metre concrete Berlin Wall, halting the refugee exodus and ending the ultimatum crisis despite October’s tense Checkpoint Charlie tank standoff.

Enquiry Question (Page 4): How did Cuba bring the USA and USSR to the brink of nuclear war in October 1962 — and why did the crisis usher in superpower arms control?
Page 5 • Question 1 [4 Marks] Level 1: 1–2m | Level 2: 3–4m (AO1 + AO2)
Explain one consequence of the Bay of Pigs incident (1961). [4 marks]
Option A (4/4): Cuba’s Military Alignment With the USSR:

One consequence of the failed Bay of Pigs incident in April 1961 was that Fidel Castro moved Cuba openly into the Soviet military camp for protection against a future US invasion. Following the defeat of 1,400 CIA-backed Brigade 2506 exiles, Castro declared himself a Marxist-Leninist and agreed in 1962 to let Khrushchev secretly station medium-range Soviet nuclear missiles on Cuban soil.

Option B (4/4): Humiliation of Kennedy & Soviet Confidence:

One consequence of the April 1961 Bay of Pigs fiasco was severe international humiliation for President Kennedy that emboldened Nikita Khrushchev. Exposing covert American aggression against a small neighbour while failing militarily within seventy-two hours, the defeat convinced Khrushchev that the 43-year-old president was weak and indecisive ahead of the June 1961 Vienna Summit and 1962 Cuban deployment.

Page 5 • Question 3 • Explain Importance (12 mins) 8 Marks
Explain the importance of the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962) for US-Soviet relations. [8 marks]
Edexcel Paper 2 Q3 Importance Tariff (8 Marks — AO1: 4, AO2: 4): Level 1 (1–2m): Generalised assertions • Level 2 (3–5m): Descriptive account with limited link to importance • Level 3 (6–8m): Two distinct, factually supported analytical paragraphs directly explaining why X mattered for Y.
Grade 9 Model Answer (8 / 8 Marks):

First, the Cuban Missile Crisis of October 1962 was vitally important for US-Soviet relations because standing on the brink of thermonuclear war shocked both superpowers into establishing direct crisis communication and curbing atmospheric testing. Realising how slow diplomatic messages during the Thirteen Days had nearly caused catastrophe, Washington and Moscow installed the direct White House–Kremlin teleprinter "hotline" in June 1963 to prevent future miscalculation, followed in August 1963 by the Limited Test Ban Treaty banning nuclear tests in the atmosphere, outer space, and underwater.

Second, the crisis ushered in a more stable era of negotiated arms control and Mutually Assured Destruction (MAD) across the mid-1960s, even as Moscow strove for nuclear parity. Determined to prevent runaway nuclear expansion, the superpowers signed the Outer Space Treaty (1967), outlawing nuclear weapons in orbit, and the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968), halting the spread of nuclear arms to new states—while perceived humiliation over withdrawing from Cuba contributed to the Soviet Politburo ousting Khrushchev in 1964.

Opposition in Czechoslovakia & Dubček’s Prague Spring, 1968
Enquiry Question (Page 6): Why did Alexander Dubček’s "Socialism with a human face" alarm Leonid Brezhnev and Warsaw Pact hardliners in 1968?
Page 7 • Question 1 [4 Marks] Level 1: 1–2m | Level 2: 3–4m (AO1 + AO2)
Explain one consequence of the Prague Spring (1968). [4 marks]
Option A (4/4): Warsaw Pact Alarm & August 1968 Invasion:

One consequence of Alexander Dubček’s Prague Spring reforms in April 1968 was fierce alarm among Warsaw Pact hardliners that culminated in military invasion. Fearing that abolishing press censorship under "Socialism with a human face" would spread anti-communist demands into East Germany and Poland, Leonid Brezhnev ordered 500,000 Warsaw Pact troops to invade Czechoslovakia on 20–21 August 1968.

Option B (4/4): Surge of Uncensored Political Debate in Prague:

One consequence of the Prague Spring was an immediate explosion of open political debate and civil society across Czechoslovakia. Once Dubček’s April 1968 Action Programme ended state press censorship and curbed StB secret-police repression, non-communist clubs formed and Ludvík Vaculík published the radical "Two Thousand Words" manifesto demanding faster democratic reform.

Page 7 • Question 2 • Analytical Narrative Account (12 mins) 8 Marks
Write a narrative account analysing the key events in Czechoslovakia in 1968. [8 marks]
Stimulus bullets printed: • the Prague Spring (1968) • the Brezhnev Doctrine (1968)
Edexcel Paper 2 Q2 Narrative Tariff (8 Marks — AO1: 4, AO2: 4): Level 1 (1–2m): Simple statements • Level 2 (3–5m): Chronological description with implicit links (capped at 4/8 without own knowledge beyond stimulus) • Level 3 (6–8m): Organised beginning–middle–outcome sequence with explicit causal links & own knowledge beyond the two stimulus points.
Grade 9 Model Answer (8 / 8 Marks):

Following economic stagnation and anger at StB secret-police repression under hardliner Antonín Novotný, Alexander Dubček became First Secretary in January 1968 and launched the Prague Spring. Through his April 1968 Action Programme ("Socialism with a human face"), Dubček abolished press censorship, permitted foreign travel, and planned market reforms while repeatedly assuring Moscow that Czechoslovakia would remain loyal to the Warsaw Pact.

However, uncensored criticism—including the June 1968 "Two Thousand Words" manifesto and Marshal Tito’s warm reception in Prague—convinced Leonid Brezhnev alongside East Germany’s Ulbricht and Poland’s Gomułka (the July Warsaw Letter) that reform risked toppling Communist rule across the bloc. Consequently, on the night of 20–21 August 1968, roughly 500,000 Warsaw Pact troops invaded Czechoslovakia; while citizens mounted peaceful passive resistance, Dubček was arrested, flown to Moscow, and forced to sign the Moscow Protocol reimposing censorship.

Because Czechoslovakia had never threatened to leave the Warsaw Pact like Hungary in 1956, Brezhnev justified the invasion in autumn 1968 through the Brezhnev Doctrine—declaring that every Communist state had a collective duty to intervene militarily if one-party Communist rule was threatened anywhere in the Eastern Bloc.

The Soviet Invasion of Czechoslovakia, Brezhnev Doctrine & International Reaction, 1968–70
Enquiry Question (Page 10): Why did the August 1968 invasion of Czechoslovakia tighten Moscow’s grip on Eastern Europe yet fracture the wider Communist world without triggering US military action?
Page 11 • Question 1 [4 Marks] Level 1: 1–2m | Level 2: 3–4m (AO1 + AO2)
Explain one consequence of the Brezhnev Doctrine (1968). [4 marks]
Option A (4/4): Freezing One-Party Control Across the Warsaw Pact:

One consequence of the Brezhnev Doctrine in autumn 1968 was the freezing of political reform across Warsaw Pact satellite states for the next twenty years. By declaring that every socialist state had a military duty to intervene if one-party Communist rule was threatened anywhere in the bloc, Brezhnev backed Gustáv Husák’s "normalisation" in Prague and deterred open government reform until 1989.

Option B (4/4): Split Across International Communism:

One consequence of the Brezhnev Doctrine and the invasion it justified was a deep split across the international Communist movement. Refusing to accept Moscow’s right to invade fellow socialist states, Nicolae Ceaușescu’s Romania and Tito’s Yugoslavia condemned Kremlin aggression alongside Mao’s China, while French and Italian Communist parties broke from Soviet authority toward independent "Eurocommunism".

Page 11 • Question 3 • Explain Importance (12 mins) 8 Marks
Explain the importance of Soviet measures in Czechoslovakia (1968) for international relations. [8 marks]
Edexcel Paper 2 Q3 Importance Tariff (8 Marks — AO1: 4, AO2: 4): Level 1 (1–2m): Generalised assertions • Level 2 (3–5m): Descriptive account with limited link to importance • Level 3 (6–8m): Two distinct, factually supported analytical paragraphs directly explaining why X mattered for Y.
Grade 9 Model Answer (8 / 8 Marks):

First, Soviet measures in Czechoslovakia in August 1968 were important for superpower relations because they caused only a short-lived diplomatic chill while confirming that neither superpower would risk nuclear war over the other’s sphere of influence. Although US President Lyndon B. Johnson condemned the invasion, cancelled a planned Moscow disarmament summit, and supported a UN Security Council resolution (vetoed by Moscow), Washington was bogged down with 500,000 troops in the Vietnam War and took zero military action—demonstrating tacit acceptance of the Iron Curtain that allowed détente talks to resume within two years.

Second, Moscow’s armed intervention and the Brezhnev Doctrine permanently fractured unity across the international Communist movement. Inside Eastern Europe, Nicolae Ceaușescu’s Romania refused to send troops and aligned more independently alongside Tito’s Yugoslavia, while Mao Zedong’s China denounced Soviet "social imperialism" fearing a similar Soviet strike. Simultaneously, outrage led the powerful French and Italian Communist Parties to distance themselves from Kremlin control under "Eurocommunism", eroding Moscow’s authority as leader of world communism.

Grade 9 Model Answer (4 / 4 Marks):

One consequence of Khrushchev’s November 1958 Berlin ultimatum was a three-year series of high-stakes superpower summit meetings between 1959 and 1961. Because neither Eisenhower nor Khrushchev wanted nuclear war over West Berlin, foreign ministers met at Geneva (May 1959) and the two leaders met personally at Camp David (September 1959), where Khrushchev temporarily withdrew his six-month deadline ahead of the 1960 Paris and 1961 Vienna summits.

Grade 9 Model Answer (4 / 4 Marks):

One consequence of Kennedy’s June 1963 visit to West Berlin was a major Cold War propaganda triumph for the West alongside reassurance for West Berliners. Addressing hundreds of thousands of cheering citizens with the words "Ich bin ein Berliner", Kennedy contrasted democratic freedom with a Communist system that had to build a concrete wall to keep its own people in, reaffirming that the USA would permanently defend West Berlin.

Page 14 • Question 1: Explain Two Consequences [8 Marks — 2 × 4m] Mark Each Consequence Independently out of 4
1  Explain two consequences of the construction of the Berlin Wall in 1961. [8 marks]
Pearson Edexcel Paper 2 Q1 Marking Rule: Mark Consequence 1 (0–4 marks) and Consequence 2 (0–4 marks) separately. Level 1 (1–2m): Simple/general statement • Level 2 (3–4m): Specific factual detail + explained causal outcome.
Consequence 1 — Grade 9 Model Answer (4 / 4 Marks):

One consequence of the construction of the Berlin Wall from 13 August 1961 was an immediate end to East Germany’s crippling refugee "brain drain" at terrible human cost. By sealing the 43km border through Berlin with barbed wire, concrete slabs, watchtowers, and armed guards, the GDR stopped the exodus that had cost nearly three million citizens since 1949, stabilising East Germany’s economy while splitting families and killing over 130 escapees.

Consequence 2 — Grade 9 Model Answer (4 / 4 Marks):

A second consequence was that the Wall paradoxically reduced the immediate danger of superpower war over Berlin while handing the West a lasting propaganda victory. As President Kennedy remarked that "a wall is a hell of a lot better than a war", Khrushchev abandoned his ultimatum to expel Western troops from West Berlin, while Kennedy’s June 1963 speech used the Wall worldwide as proof of Communist failure.

Page 15 • Question 2 • Analytical Narrative Account [8 Marks] (Unscaffolded) 8 Marks (AO1: 4 + AO2: 4)
2  Write a narrative account analysing the key events in superpower relations over Cuba in the years 1959–62. [8 marks]
Stimulus bullets printed: • the Cuban Revolution (1959) • the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962)
Level 3 (6–8 Marks): Organised beginning–developments–outcome chronological sequence linked by explicit causal connectives, integrating accurate own knowledge beyond the two stimulus bullets.
Grade 9 Model Answer (8 / 8 Marks):

Following Fidel Castro’s overthrow of US-backed dictator Batista in the January 1959 Cuban Revolution, Castro nationalised American-owned land and sugar refineries, prompting President Eisenhower to impose a sugar embargo and sever diplomatic ties. Pushed toward Moscow for Soviet oil, trade, and weapons, Castro defeated 1,400 CIA-trained Cuban exiles at the Bay of Pigs in April 1961—humiliating President Kennedy and leading Castro to declare Cuba Communist and request Soviet military protection.

To deter a full-scale US invasion and counter American Jupiter missiles in Turkey, Nikita Khrushchev secretly shipped medium-range nuclear missiles to Cuba in 1962. When an American U-2 spy plane photographed the launch sites on 14 October 1962, triggering the Cuban Missile Crisis, Kennedy convened ExComm and announced a 500-mile US naval quarantine on 22 October while placing US nuclear forces on DEFCON 2; at the brink of war on 24 October, Soviet ships turned back before the blockade line.

Following urgent back-channel diplomacy between Robert Kennedy and Soviet Ambassador Dobrynin alongside Khrushchev’s two letters, Khrushchev agreed on 28 October 1962 to dismantle and withdraw all Soviet missiles from Cuba. In return, Kennedy publicly pledged not to invade Cuba and secretly agreed to remove US Jupiter missiles from Turkey, leading directly to the 1963 Moscow–Washington "hotline" and Limited Test Ban Treaty.
