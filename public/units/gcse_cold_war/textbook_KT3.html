Détente (1970s) Deliberate relaxation of superpower tensions through trade and arms-control treaties after the shock of Cuba and cost of Vietnam.

INF Treaty (Dec 1987) Landmark Reagan–Gorbachev treaty eliminating all land-based intermediate-range nuclear missiles (500–5,500 km) with on-site inspections.
Sinatra Doctrine & Gang of Eight 1989 Soviet policy letting Eastern Bloc states choose their own path; August 1991 hardliners’ coup that accelerated Soviet collapse.

KEY ENQUIRY “How genuinely did Détente ease superpower rivalry between 1970 and 1979?”
STAGE 1 Superpower Pressures Behind Détente 1970–1972
“Why did the USA and USSR both pursue Détente in the early 1970s?”

By the early 1970s, urgent strategic and economic pressures pushed both the USA and the USSR toward Détente—a deliberate relaxation of Cold War tension. Since the 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis, both superpowers feared Mutually Assured Destruction (MAD) and wanted to curb ruinous arms spending. Under President Richard Nixon and adviser Henry Kissinger, the USA was drained by the Vietnam War, which cost over $150 billion, killed 58,000 Americans, fuelled inflation, and sparked fierce domestic protests. Meanwhile, Soviet leader Leonid Brezhnev needed Western grain imports and modern industrial technology to rescue stagnant living standards across the USSR. Having finally achieved rough nuclear parity with Washington, Moscow also feared diplomatic isolation after its bitter Sino-Soviet border split—especially once Nixon visited Communist China in February 1972—giving both capitals strong incentives to negotiate.

STAGE 2 The Moscow Summit & SALT 1 Agreements May 1972
“What did Nixon and Brezhnev agree and leave out in SALT 1 in 1972?”

On 26 May 1972, Nixon and Brezhnev signed SALT 1 (the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks) at the Moscow Summit—the first formal superpower nuclear arms accord. First, the Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty restricted each nation to just two ABM sites (one protecting its capital and one guarding an ICBM field), preserving deterrence by preventing either side from blocking nuclear retaliation. Second, a five-year Interim Agreement froze strategic ICBMs and submarine-launched SLBMs at existing totals. Third, the Basic Principles Agreement committed both superpowers to avoid military confrontation and pursue peaceful coexistence. However, SALT 1 had serious loopholes: it froze launcher numbers rather than cutting arsenals and left MIRVs—single missiles carrying multiple independently targeted warheads—completely unrestricted, allowing warhead stockpiles to multiply throughout the 1970s.

Détente in the 1970s — SALT 1, the Helsinki Accords & SALT 2 — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 Symbolic Thaw & Space Cooperation 1973–July 1975
“In what ways did superpower cooperation deepen between 1973 and July 1975?”

Following the Moscow accords, summit diplomacy and commercial trade expanded rapidly between 1973 and July 1975. Leonid Brezhnev visited Washington in June 1973 to sign agreements on trade, agriculture, and preventing nuclear war, while Richard Nixon returned to Moscow in June 1974 shortly before resigning over the Watergate scandal and handing the presidency to Gerald Ford. Millions of tonnes of American grain arrived in Soviet ports, relieving acute food shortages. Détente reached its symbolic peak on 17 July 1975 during the joint Apollo–Soyuz mission, when an American Apollo spacecraft docked in Earth orbit with a Soviet Soyuz capsule. Watched live on television, US commander Thomas Stafford shook hands through the open docking hatch with cosmonaut Alexei Leonov, turning space rivalry into public partnership—even while covert Middle East and Third World competition persisted.

STAGE 4 The Three Baskets of the Helsinki Accords Aug 1975
“How did the August 1975 Helsinki Accords reward and embarrass the USSR?”

In August 1975, 35 nations—including US President Gerald Ford, Leonid Brezhnev for the USSR, Canada, and 32 European states—gathered in Finland and signed the Helsinki Accords across three "Baskets". Basket 1 (European Borders) declared post-1945 frontiers inviolable and formally recognised East Germany, rewarding Moscow with Western acceptance of its Eastern European sphere. Basket 2 (International Cooperation) expanded East–West trade, industrial exchanges, and joint scientific research. Crucially, Basket 3 (Human Rights) committed every signatory state to respect freedom of speech, religion, press information, and movement across borders. Although Brezhnev assumed Basket 3 was harmless diplomatic window-dressing, brave dissidents formed Helsinki Watch monitoring groups inside the USSR and Charter 77 in Czechoslovakia, repeatedly embarrassing Moscow before Western journalists over political arrests.

STAGE 5 Carter, SALT 2 & Strained Détente 1977–1979
“What caused Détente and SALT 2 to falter under Jimmy Carter by 1979?”

Inaugurated in January 1977, US President Jimmy Carter placed human rights at the centre of American diplomacy, writing to Soviet dissident Andrei Sakharov and linking trade to Moscow’s treatment of dissidents. Leonid Brezhnev resented this as meddling in Soviet internal affairs, while US Senate hawks condemned Soviet-backed Communist interventions in Angola and Ethiopia alongside new Soviet SS-20 missiles aimed at Western Europe. Despite mounting suspicion, Jimmy Carter and Brezhnev signed SALT 2 in Vienna on 18 June 1979, setting an equal cap of 2,400 strategic nuclear delivery vehicles (falling to 2,250) and limiting MIRV launchers. Yet fierce US Senate criticism stalled ratification, and after Soviet troops invaded Afghanistan in December 1979, Carter withdrew SALT 2 in January 1980—ending Détente.

KEY ENQUIRY “Why did superpower relations plunge from Détente into a dangerous ‘Second Cold War’ between 1979 and 1985?”
STAGE 1 Crisis in Kabul & The Soviet Invasion 1978–Dec 1979
“Why did Brezhnev order the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979?”

In April 1978, the Saur Revolution brought the pro-Soviet Communist PDPA under Nur Muhammad Taraki to power in Afghanistan. Radical land and secular reforms provoked fierce armed rebellion from Islamic Mujahideen guerrillas, and in September 1979 Prime Minister Hafizullah Amin seized power and had Taraki murdered. Watching the 1979 Iranian Revolution next door, Leonid Brezhnev feared Islamic fundamentalism spreading north across the border into 30 million Muslims living in Soviet Central Asian republics. Suspecting the unpredictable Amin of secretly contacting the USA, Brezhnev invoked the Brezhnev Doctrine to preserve a bordering Communist state. Between 24 and 27 December 1979, Soviet airborne forces and armoured columns invaded Afghanistan, storming Kabul’s presidential palace, killing Amin, and installing Moscow-backed Babrak Karmal—trapping the USSR in a costly ten-year guerrilla war.

STAGE 2 The Carter Doctrine & Death of Détente Jan 1980
“How did Jimmy Carter retaliate against the USSR in January 1980?”

Calling the Afghan invasion the gravest threat to peace since 1945, US President Jimmy Carter buried Détente in January 1980. In his 23 January 1980 State of the Union address, he proclaimed the Carter Doctrine: the USA would use military force if necessary to block any outside power—meaning the USSR—from gaining control of the oil-rich Persian Gulf, backed by a new Rapid Deployment Force. Simultaneously, Jimmy Carter withdrew the SALT 2 treaty from the US Senate, increased American defence spending by 5 per cent, and imposed a punishing grain embargo alongside a ban on exporting computers and oil-drilling technology to the USSR. Under CIA Operation Cyclone, Washington also channelled money and weapons through Pakistan to arm the Afghan Mujahideen.

Soviet Invasion of Afghanistan, Olympic Boycotts & Reagan’s ‘Second Cold War’ — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 Tit-for-Tat Olympic Boycotts 1980 & 1984
“In what ways did the 1980 and 1984 Olympic boycotts deepen rivalry?”

Cold War confrontation immediately spilled into international sport when Jimmy Carter gave Moscow an ultimatum to withdraw from Afghanistan by February 1980 or face an Olympic boycott. When Leonid Brezhnev refused, the USA led over 60 nations—including West Germany, China, Japan, Canada, and Kenya—to boycott the 1980 Moscow Summer Olympics, denying the Kremlin the global showcase it had spent billions building and prompting rival Liberty Bell Classic games in Philadelphia. Although British athletes still competed in Moscow under the neutral Olympic flag, Soviet leaders resented the public diplomatic humiliation. Four years later, in May 1984, the USSR cited alleged anti-Soviet hysteria and inadequate athlete security to lead 14 Eastern Bloc allies in a retaliatory boycott of the 1984 Los Angeles Summer Olympics, underscoring frozen superpower relations.

STAGE 4 Ronald Reagan & The Second Cold War 1981–Mar 1983
“What policies launched Ronald Reagan’s Second Cold War between 1981 and 1983?”

Inaugurated in January 1981, staunchly anti-communist US President Ronald Reagan dismissed Détente as a "one-way street" exploited by Moscow and ignited the Second Cold War. Determined to negotiate only from overwhelming strength, Reagan launched a $1 trillion American defence build-up across the early 1980s, funding 100 B-1 Lancer bombers, Trident nuclear submarines, stealth aircraft, the neutron bomb, and Pershing II and Cruise missiles deployed in Western Europe. Alongside military rearmament, Ronald Reagan sharpened ideological warfare: on 8 March 1983 he denounced the Soviet Union as an "Evil Empire" and the "focus of evil in the modern world". Under the Reagan Doctrine, Washington openly funded and armed anti-communist guerrillas—from Nicaragua’s Contras to Stinger anti-aircraft missiles later supplied to Afghanistan’s Mujahideen—to roll back Soviet influence.

STAGE 5 Star Wars (SDI) & Soviet Economic Strain 23 Mar 1983–1985
“What caused Reagan’s 1983 Strategic Defence Initiative to alarm Moscow?”

On 23 March 1983, Reagan stunned Moscow by announcing the Strategic Defence Initiative (SDI)—nicknamed "Star Wars" by journalists. Rather than relying on Mutually Assured Destruction (MAD), SDI proposed a network of orbiting space satellites armed with high-powered lasers to intercept and destroy Soviet ICBMs in flight before they could hit American soil, creating a nuclear shield over the USA while bypassing the 1972 ABM Treaty. Soviet leaders—passing rapidly through elderly premiers Yuri Andropov and Konstantin Chernenko after Brezhnev’s death in 1982—viewed SDI as an existential threat that could hand Washington a first-strike capability and neutralise Soviet missiles. Because the stagnant Soviet command economy lagged far behind America in microchips and supercomputers, Moscow knew trying to match Star Wars would bankrupt the USSR.

KEY ENQUIRY “How did Mikhail Gorbachev’s ‘New Thinking’ and superpower summit diplomacy transform US–Soviet relations between 1985 and 1989?”
STAGE 1 Soviet Crisis & Exhaustion by 1985 1985–April 1986
“Why was the Soviet Union facing severe internal crisis by March 1985?”

When 54-year-old Mikhail Gorbachev became General Secretary in March 1985 after three elderly, ailing leaders (Leonid Brezhnev, Yuri Andropov, and Konstantin Chernenko) died in quick succession, the Soviet Union faced acute systemic crisis. Central planning bred industrial stagnation, falling oil revenues, rusting factories, and chronic urban food queues, while military spending swallowed roughly 25% of Soviet GDP as Moscow struggled to match Ronald Reagan’s computerised Strategic Defence Initiative (SDI). Abroad, the unwinnable war in Afghanistan bled the Soviet treasury of $8 billion every year and cost 15,000 Soviet soldiers’ lives. Worse still, the April 1986 Chernobyl nuclear power-plant explosion in Ukraine spread radioactive fallout across Europe and exposed Soviet technological decay and official cover-ups, convincing Gorbachev that survival required urgent reform.

STAGE 2 Gorbachev’s Perestroika, Glasnost & Reform 1985–1988
“How did Gorbachev try to reform communism through Perestroika and Glasnost?”

Crucially, Gorbachev remained a committed Marxist-Leninist whose ‘New Thinking’ aimed to rescue and modernise communism, not abolish it. Under Perestroika (economic restructuring), Moscow relaxed rigid state production quotas, legalised small private and cooperative businesses in 1987–88, welcomed joint ventures with Western firms, and sought deep cuts in nuclear weapons spending so money could rebuild civilian living standards. Alongside economic reform, Gorbachev launched Glasnost (political openness) to overcome entrenched party corruption and win public backing against conservative officials. State censorship loosened so newspapers and television could openly investigate Stalinist terror, economic waste, and Chernobyl; prominent exiled dissident physicist Andrei Sakharov was freed in December 1986, and 1989 brought multi-candidate elections to a new Congress of People’s Deputies.

Gorbachev’s ‘New Thinking’ & The Superpower Summits — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 Geneva, Reykjavik & Early Summit Diplomacy Nov 1985–Oct 1986
“What did Reagan and Gorbachev achieve at Geneva and Reykjavik by October 1986?”

Because Perestroika required slashing crippling defence budgets, Gorbachev urgently pursued arms-control talks with US President Ronald Reagan. Meeting face-to-face for the first time at the Geneva Summit in November 1985, the two leaders stepped away from stiff advisers for private fireside conversations, forged warm personal rapport, and signed a landmark joint declaration stating that "a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought". Eleven months later, at the Reykjavik Summit in Iceland in October 1986, Gorbachev stunned Washington by proposing a 50% cut in strategic arsenals and eventually abolishing all ballistic nuclear missiles within ten years—provided Reagan confined SDI research strictly to the laboratory. Reagan refused to scrap SDI, causing Reykjavik to collapse without a treaty, yet both sides now trusted that sweeping disarmament was achievable.

STAGE 4 The INF Treaty & Disarmament Breakthrough June–Dec 1987
“What caused the December 1987 INF Treaty to mark a historic breakthrough?”

In June 1987, speaking before West Berlin’s Brandenburg Gate, Reagan publicly challenged Moscow to prove its reformist sincerity: "Mr. Gorbachev, tear down this wall!" Meanwhile, Soviet scientists quietly advised Gorbachev that America’s SDI laser shield was technologically unproven and far cheaper to counter than to match, persuading him in February 1987 to uncouple missile cuts from SDI. Consequently, at the Washington Summit on 8 December 1987, Reagan and Gorbachev signed the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF Treaty). Unlike SALT 1, which merely capped warhead growth, the INF Treaty eliminated an entire category of land-based nuclear and conventional missiles with ranges of 500 to 5,500 km—destroying over 2,600 Soviet SS-20 and US Pershing II and Cruise missiles under intrusive on-site inspections.

STAGE 5 Leaving Afghanistan, UN Cuts & The Malta Summit 1988–Dec 1989
“In what ways did Soviet policy between 1988 and December 1989 end rivalry?”

Superpower reconciliation accelerated throughout 1988. Visiting the Moscow Summit in May–June 1988, Reagan strolled across Red Square with Gorbachev and told reporters he no longer considered the Soviet Union an "Evil Empire" because it belonged to "another time, another era". Addressing the United Nations on 7 December 1988, Gorbachev announced unilateral cuts of 500,000 Red Army troops and formally abandoned the 1968 Brezhnev Doctrine, promising that Soviet tanks would never again crush Eastern European political choices. True to his word, Gorbachev completed the full withdrawal of Soviet forces from Afghanistan in February 1989, removing a major barrier to Western trust. Finally, aboard storm-tossed warships at the Malta Summit in December 1989, Gorbachev and new US President George H. W. Bush declared the Cold War officially over.

KEY ENQUIRY “Why did abandoning the Brezhnev Doctrine topple the Berlin Wall in 1989 and bring about the collapse of the Soviet Union itself by December 1991?”
STAGE 1 The Sinatra Doctrine & Upheaval in Poland and Hungary May–Oct 1989
“Why did Poland and Hungary break from Communist control during 1989?”

Once Mikhail Gorbachev renounced the 1968 Brezhnev Doctrine—jokingly christened the "Sinatra Doctrine" by Soviet spokesman Gennady Gerasimov in October 1989 because Warsaw Pact states were now free to "do it their way"—Eastern Europe’s unpopular Communist regimes lost the Red Army tanks that had kept them in power since 1945. Reformers immediately tested Moscow’s restraint. In Poland, crippling strikes forced General Wojciech Jaruzelski to re-legalise Lech Wałęsa’s independent trade union Solidarity and hold semi-free elections in June 1989; Solidarity won 99 of 100 Senate seats, making Tadeusz Mazowiecki the Eastern Bloc’s first non-communist Prime Minister since 1948 while Moscow stayed silent. Simultaneously, reformist Hungary dismantled its barbed-wire border fence with neutral Austria between May and September 1989, opening a huge gap in the Iron Curtain.

STAGE 2 East German Protests & Fall of the Berlin Wall Oct–9 Nov 1989
“How did protests and Schabowski bring down the Berlin Wall in 1989?”

Tens of thousands of East Germans escaped westwards through Hungary and Austria. Hardline East German (GDR) leader Erich Honecker rejected reform, but after Gorbachev warned in October 1989 that Soviet troops would stay in their barracks, vast peaceful Monday protests of 70,000+ citizens in Leipzig forced the Politburo to replace Honecker with Egon Krenz. Hoping to calm public anger with orderly travel permits, Krenz’s government drafted new exit rules on 9 November 1989. At an evening televised briefing, unprepared spokesman Günter Schabowski fumbled his notes and announced that East Germans could cross border checkpoints "immediately, without delay". Vast crowds surged to the Berlin Wall; refusing to shoot, outnumbered guards opened the barriers as jubilant Berliners hammered down the concrete divide.

The Collapse of Eastern Europe, Fall of the Berlin Wall & End of the USSR — Escalation & Consequences

Stages 3–5
STAGE 3 Eastern European Revolutions, Reunification & The Warsaw Pact Nov 1989–July 1991
“What transformed Eastern Europe and the Warsaw Pact between 1989 and July 1991?”

The opening of the Berlin Wall triggered a rapid domino collapse of Communist rule across Eastern Europe before the end of 1989. In Czechoslovakia, peaceful mass strikes during November’s Velvet Revolution swept dissident playwright Václav Havel into the presidency; Bulgaria’s Communist veteran Todor Zhivkov was ousted, while in Romania a violent December uprising overthrew and executed hated dictator Nicolae Ceaușescu. In Germany, West German Chancellor Helmut Kohl secured Gorbachev’s consent—backed by Western financial loans—for formal German reunification on 3 October 1990, with a united democratic Germany remaining inside NATO. Once newly elected governments in Poland, Hungary, and Czechoslovakia demanded the withdrawal of Soviet garrisons, the Warsaw Pact lost all purpose: its military structures dissolved in March 1991 and the alliance formally ended on 1 July 1991.

STAGE 4 Nationalist Secession & The August 1991 Coup 1990–Aug 1991
“What caused the August 1991 hardline coup in Moscow to backfire on its leaders?”

Watching Eastern Europe break free inspired non-Russian nationalities among the USSR’s fifteen republics to demand independence too. While Perestroika disrupted state supply chains—bringing rationing and soaring inflation—Glasnost allowed Baltic movements to win elections: Lithuania declared full independence in March 1990, soon joined by Latvia and Estonia. Hoping to hold the union together voluntarily, Gorbachev drafted a decentralised New Union Treaty for 20 August 1991. One day beforehand, on 19 August 1991, eight senior Communist and KGB hardliners—the Gang of Eight—launched a coup in Moscow and placed Gorbachev under house arrest in Crimea. The plotters fatally miscalculated: newly elected Russian President Boris Yeltsin climbed atop a tank outside the Russian White House parliament in Moscow, rallied vast crowds and elite army units to defy martial law, and forced the coup’s collapse within seventy-two hours.

STAGE 5 The CIS, Gorbachev’s Resignation & End of the USSR Dec 1991
“In what ways did December 1991 events dissolve the USSR and Cold War?”

Although Gorbachev returned to Moscow, his authority had evaporated while Boris Yeltsin emerged as Russia’s decisive leader and suspended the Communist Party in Russia. Following Ukraine’s 90% independence vote on 1 December 1991, the presidents of Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus signed the Belavezha Accords on 8 December 1991 declaring the USSR dissolved and establishing a loose Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS). Eight further republics joined the CIS on 21 December 1991, leaving Gorbachev a president without a state. On Christmas night, 25 December 1991, Gorbachev resigned on live television; minutes later, the Soviet hammer-and-sickle banner was lowered over the Kremlin, splitting the USSR into fifteen independent countries, leaving the United States as sole superpower, and ending the Cold War.
