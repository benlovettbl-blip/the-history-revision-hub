Stage 1 of 5 January 1066

The Succession Void

Why did the death of Edward the Confessor trigger a succession crisis?
On 5 January 1066, King Edward the Confessor died after reigning for twenty-four years. Edward left England in an existential crisis: he had died childless, leaving no direct heir to the throne. In Anglo-Saxon England, royal blood did not guarantee succession; the royal council, known as the Witan, held the constitutional right to elect the next monarch. Within twenty-four hours of Edward’s burial in Westminster Abbey, the Witan elected England’s wealthiest nobleman, Earl Harold Godwinson.

Bronze Starter Silver Facts Gold Vocabulary
A decisive factor in William’s victory was his mastery of combined arms and tactical flexibility. For over six hours on Senlac Hill, the Anglo-Saxon shield wall held firm against frontal cavalry charges. However, William exploited Saxon overconfidence by ordering a series of feigned retreats. When the undisciplined Saxon fyrd broke formation to pursue the 'fleeing' Normans, William’s heavy cavalry wheeled around and encircled them.

Deprived of the defensive protection of their locked shields and exhausted from their 190-mile forced march after Stamford Bridge, the Saxon infantry was decimated, proving that while Harold fought bravely, William's combined arms and tactical genius proved decisive.
